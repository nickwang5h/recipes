This is pork and napa cabbage stew with glass noodles: the cabbage stems are stewed soft all the way through while the leaves hold their shape, and the glass noodles are slippery and chewy, full of the savoury pork broth. The pork belly is stir-fried first to render its fat, and the cabbage and noodles then take on the flavour of the meat broth. The cabbage stems are thick and the leaves thin, so they cook at different speeds; they go into the pot separately, so the stems stew soft and the leaves do not fall apart (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Thin 3 mm slices of pork cook quickly, so you do not need to wait as long as for stewing large pieces of meat.

Glass noodles are made of starch. They are boiled and then soaked so they take on water and soften again, and in the end they are cooked briefly in the broth to take on flavour; see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). The thickness and ingredients of the noodles differ, and so do the times they need. A little thirteen-spice powder is enough to bring out the flavour; too much covers the sweetness of the cabbage (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).

## Steps

1. Cook the potato glass noodles in boiling water for about 5 minutes, then pour them with the hot water into a heatproof container, cover and soak. Follow the package instructions, and soak until they bend and still have a little chew in the centre, then drain and cut into short lengths that are easy to pick up.
2. Cut the pork belly into slices about 3 mm thick. Wash and drain the cabbage, and cut the tender leaves and the stems into slices separately, cutting thick stems on the diagonal; keep the leaves apart, and do not mix them together.
3. Heat the wok, add 15 ml of oil, and add the pork belly when the oil flows easily. Spread it out and stir-fry until the meat changes colour, the edges of the fat turn translucent and fat seeps out at the bottom of the pan; do not pile up wet meat in one place.
4. Add the dark soy sauce and stir-fry for about 1 minute, so the slices take on colour evenly. Then add the cabbage stems and light soy sauce and stir-fry for about 1 minute; if things stick to the bottom, spoon in 10 ml of the stewing water along the edge of the pan and gently push it around.
5. Pour in the rest of the stewing water, about 600 ml in total, almost covering the meat and cabbage stems. Add the thirteen-spice powder and a little chicken bouillon powder (optional), bring to a boil, turn to low heat, cover and stew for about 20 minutes, until the cabbage stems are easy to break with chopsticks.
6. Add the tender leaves and toss, lay the drained noodles over the cabbage, cover and cook for about 5 minutes more. Gently push them once midway so the noodles soak into the broth. When the noodles have no hard core in the centre and are slippery and chewy all through, and the tender leaves have softened, you can uncover.
7. Taste the broth first, then add salt in portions. If you want a thicker sauce, reduce it a little with the lid off and the heat on, and turn off the heat when there is still broth at the bottom of the pot to spoon up. Plate it as soon as the noodles are done; otherwise they keep absorbing broth and clump together.

## Key points

- **The noodles are mushy and break into short bits**: they were over-cooked in the pre-cooking, and then stewed a long time in the broth (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Pre-cook only until they bend, then finish cooking them once in the pot, going by the package.
- **The stems are hard and the leaves are mush**: thick and thin parts went in together and heat up at different speeds (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Stew the stems first and add the leaves for the last stretch.
- **The spice overpowers the cabbage and it gets saltier as it reduces**: too much powder went in, and the salt concentrates with the broth (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Use a little thirteen-spice powder, and taste after reducing to decide whether to add salt.
