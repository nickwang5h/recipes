In a bite of stuffed winter melon you get both the light, juicy melon and the salty savoury pork filling. The slices are first salted to draw out water, and once they lose some water they bend easily (see [salt](/principles/salt/)); the filling is stirred until sticky, and when steamed its proteins coagulate and bind it into one mass (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). The slices must be thin and the filling divided into small portions so the steam can cook the centre through.

The juices from steaming are kept to make the glaze. A small amount of starch gelatinizes with heat and holds the savoury juices on the surface of the rolls (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)), and you can still taste the juiciness of the winter melon. The glaze only needs to coat lightly.

## Steps

1. Cut the winter melon into thin slices about 8–12 cm long and 2–3 mm thick. Make each slice an even thickness; test-bend one first to see whether the edge is too thick or stiff.
2. Rub the salt for drawing out water lightly over the slices and leave for 10 minutes. Once the slices bend into an arc, rinse three times with clean water, drain and blot off the surface water.
3. Stir the ground pork, minced green onion whites, minced ginger, the filling salt, light soy sauce, white pepper and the filling starch together. Stir in one direction for about 2 minutes, until the filling is sticky and can gather into a mass.
4. Use a slice to form a small ring, fill it with the pork filling and set it seam side down on a heatproof plate. Press the filling into a short column and leave gaps between the rolls; spread the remaining filling into a small patty about 1 cm thick and steam it on the same plate. If using the egg, crack it into an empty spot in the centre of the plate.
5. Once the water has boiled, put in the plate, cover and steam for about 15 minutes. The slices should be translucent and break easily when pinched; a thermometer in the thickest part of the filling should read 71°C (160°F) for it to be done. If you added the egg, its centre must reach 74°C (165°F). If not, keep steaming.
6. Pour the juices from the plate into a small pot and add the second portion of water, then bring to a boil. Mix the starch with the cold water and stir it again just before pouring, then drizzle it in stages into the boiling juices, stirring as you pour. Stop when the liquid turns glossy and thinly coats the back of a spoon; you don't need to pour in all of it.
7. Drizzle the thin glaze over the rolls, sprinkle with the green onion greens and eat hot. Take the plate out with heatproof gloves or a plate lifter and carry it level so the hot juices don't spill.

## Key points

- **Slices won't roll and the edges crack**: thick slices still won't bend after drawing out water (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Shave down the stiff edges first, then salt until they bend; try rolling while you slice, which is easier than fixing it after the filling is in.
- **Filling falls apart and a lot of liquid steams out**: the slices still had water on them, or the filling wasn't stirred until sticky. Salt lets the proteins in the meat bind into a mass, so stir the filling into a mass first, then fill the drained slices (see [salt](/principles/salt/)).
- **Glaze clumps and looks like paste**: the starch wasn't mixed smooth first, or too much was poured at once (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Stir the slurry smooth again and add it in stages, stopping when you reach a thin glaze.
