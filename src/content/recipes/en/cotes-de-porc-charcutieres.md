*Côtes de porc charcutière* is French pork chops with a mustard and pickle sauce. The sauce is a Robert sauce made from onion, white wine and demi-glace, with small pickled cucumbers added just before it comes off the heat. The thick brown sauce supplies meaty flavour and body, while the mustard and pickles make the rich pork taste brighter.

The browned bits left in the pan from searing the meat carry flavour made by the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/); deglazing with wine brings them into the sauce. The pungency of mustard fades with long cooking (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)), so the sauce is reduced first and the mustard goes in last. The sauce then coats the meat and the mustard's aroma stays.

## Steps

1. Pat the pork chops dry with paper towels and sprinkle salt and pepper on both sides. Finely mince the onion, and drain the pickles and cut them into short thin strips. The demi-glace should be a ready-made sauce thick enough to coat a spoon; if using a commercial sauce mix, cook it as the package directs first, then measure out 300 ml.
2. Heat the vegetable oil in a heavy-bottomed frying pan over medium-high heat, add the pork chops and fry for about 2–3 minutes, until the bottom has an even brown colour, then flip. If the pan is not big enough, fry in batches with space between the pieces, or the juices that come out will turn the bottom of the pan into a pool of water.
3. When both sides are coloured, turn to medium-low and keep cooking and turning until the centre of the thickest part reaches 71°C (160°F). Insert the thermometer from the side, away from the bone. Set the chops on a warm plate loosely covered with foil; do not leave them in the pan to reduce with the sauce.
4. Pour off the excess frying fat, keeping the brown bits on the bottom of the pan. Turn to low, add the butter and onion and cook for about 5 minutes, until the onion is translucent with no raw sharpness. If there are black burnt bits on the bottom of the pan, remove them first, or the whole sauce will be bitter.
5. Pour in the white wine and scrape up the brown bits from the bottom of the pan with a wooden spatula. Reduce over medium heat to about 70 ml, with the onion still sitting in a shallow layer of liquid. Keep the acidity of the wine in the sauce; do not reduce it to just a few drops, and do not boil the pan dry.
6. Add the demi-glace and simmer over low heat for about 10 minutes, so the onion gets softer and the sauce comes together. The sauce should leave a thin layer on the back of a spoon, and a line drawn through it with a finger should not close up at once; if it is too thick, thin it with a little water.
7. Move the pan off the heat and stir in the mustard, sugar, pickles and the juices from the plate of meat. Taste, then decide whether to add salt. Return the chops to the warm sauce, turn them over to coat both sides, and plate straight away. Do not bring the sauce to a boil again after the mustard goes in.

## Key points

- **Sauce is sharply sour**: too much white wine reduced too hard, and the sourness left behind stacks with the pickles. Reduce the wine to the stated amount and add the pickles after draining them (see [acid](/principles/acid/)).
- **Sauce lacks aroma, or tastes only burnt and bitter**: the surface of the chops was too wet, or the bottom of the pan was fried until black. Dry the meat, fry in batches, and leave a brown, not black, base in the pan (see [the Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).
- **Pork is dry and the mustard aroma is faint**: the meat was cooked for a long time in the sauce, or the sauce was kept on high heat after the mustard went in. Take the cooked meat out first, and take the sauce off the heat when it is reduced to season it (see [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) and [aroma extraction](/principles/aromatics/)).
