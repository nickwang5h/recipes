Watercress and pork bone soup is a Cantonese home-style slow-simmered soup: the meat on the bones is soft, the greens are soft and the soup tastes of watercress. Meaty pork bones simmer first to make the soup, and the watercress goes in later and cooks until the stems and leaves are tender and the soup takes on a pale green tint. Watercress darkens with long cooking; what matters is that the soup tastes of it.

The tendons and connective tissue on the bones soften slowly in moist heat, and some of the [collagen turns into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), giving the clear soup a little body. Leave meat on the bones; with only a few bare bones the soup tends to taste thin. Blanching first to remove scum, then keeping a low, barely rolling simmer, reduces how much fat and debris is churned into the soup (see [Blanching and Removing Off-Odours](/principles/blanching/) and [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

## Steps

1. Snip the tough old stems off the watercress and separate the sprigs. Swish them in a large basin of clean water 3 times until there is no sand or mud at the bottom of the basin, then lift out and drain. Smash the ginger slices, and check the pork bones for bone chips, removing any visible fragments.
2. Put the 1420 ml of water for blanching in a pot and heat over high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until it boils. Add the pork bones; it takes about 2 minutes to come back to a boil, then cook for 2 more minutes, until the scum gathers and the meat turns greyish white. Pour out the blanching water, rinse the scum off the bones and wash the pot.
3. Put the pork bones, ginger slices and the 1900 ml of water for the soup in the pot and heat over high heat for about 10 minutes, until it boils. Immediately turn to low heat, cover and simmer for 90 minutes, until the soup smells of meat and the meat on the bones begins to loosen. The surface should show only scattered bubbles.
4. Add the salt, cover and keep simmering over low heat for 30 minutes, until the meat on the thickest bone can be pulled apart fairly easily with chopsticks. Use a fine skimmer to remove floating debris, then skim the excess fat from the surface with a ladle.
5. Add the watercress and press it gently into the soup. Heat over medium heat for about 2–3 minutes, until the soup returns to a gentle simmer. Turn to low heat, cover and cook for 30 minutes, until the stems break easily when bitten, the leaves are dark green and the soup tastes of watercress. Keep the lid on to reduce evaporation.
6. Add the white pepper and stir gently for about 15 seconds, until evenly seasoned. Turn off the heat and ladle out the soup, greens and meaty bones together. If you are serving light soy sauce, pour it into a small dish for dipping the meat.

## Key points

- **Lots of grey debris in the soup**: the coagulated protein from blanching stayed on the bones, or the pot was not washed. Rinse well after blanching and simmer the soup in fresh water (see [Blanching and Removing Off-Odours](/principles/blanching/)).
- **Soup is cloudy and greasy and the meat falls apart in bits**: it was kept at a hard rolling boil throughout, and the fat and debris were broken up again and again. Turn to low heat once it boils and skim the fat in the later stage (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **Meat is cooked but still tough**: collagen takes time to soften, so meat colour is not enough to judge. Keep simmering gently until the tendons are soft and the meat comes off the bone easily (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)).
- **Soup tastes of watercress but has grit in it**: the sprigs of watercress are fine and dense, and rinsing the whole bunch leaves sand behind. Separate them before washing and make sure the last rinse water is clean.
