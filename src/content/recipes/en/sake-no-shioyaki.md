Sake no shioyaki (salt-grilled salmon) has crisp skin, tender flesh and just enough salt. It is a common grilled fish on a Japanese home table, served with white rice, grated daikon and a wedge of citrus. This version uses fresh fish salted for a short time: the salt gradually moves into the flesh and the surface also releases liquid. Blotting off that liquid before cooking lets the skin dry out and brown (see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/)). Weigh the salt at about 1% of the weight of the fish. Use weight for both fine and coarse salt, so a teaspoon of a different salt does not make the fish saltier.

The broil setting on a Canadian oven can do this kind of grilled fish. Heat hits the surface first and then moves toward the thickest part, so the thickness of the fillet and its distance from the element matter more than the stated power (see [Heat Transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Buy skin-on fillets and cut them on a slant into thinner pieces, so the skin side can face the broiler. A thick whole fillet is likely to have charred skin and an undercooked centre.

## Steps

1. Pull out any small bones you can feel with tweezers and blot the surface dry with paper towels. Sprinkle 3 g of salt over both sides and the skin, put the fish in a shallow dish, cover and refrigerate for 30 minutes, until small beads of liquid appear on the surface and a little liquid collects in the dish.
2. Blot off the liquid with paper towels and wipe the edges of the skin dry too. Keep the skin on, and handle the fillets gently so you do not tear the flesh, which has firmed up slightly.
3. Set the oven rack about 15 cm below the top heating element, turn on Broil at medium and preheat for 5 minutes, until the element is hot. Line the baking sheet with aluminum foil. Do not use parchment paper under the broiler.
4. Place the fillets apart on the baking sheet, skin side up as far as possible. Broil for 8–10 minutes, until the skin blisters and has golden-brown spots, the thickest part of the flesh is opaque and it separates into flakes when you nudge it with a fork. Start checking at 8 minutes, and if any flesh is still translucent, give it 1 more minute at a time.
5. While the fish cooks, grate the daikon into a purée, drain off the extra liquid lightly and cut the lemon into wedges. Plate the fish as soon as it is cooked and serve it with the grated daikon and lemon. Do not keep it warm in the hot oven after turning it off.

## Key points

- **Skin is charred but the centre is still translucent**: the fillet is too thick or too close to the broiler, so the surface gets too far ahead. Move the baking sheet down a rack, and check the centre after each extra 1 minute. Next time cut the fish into pieces of similar thickness first (see [Heat Transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **Flesh is dry and tough, or breaks into crumbs**: it kept cooking after it was done, so the muscle proteins contracted and lost water. Take it out as soon as the thickest part is just opaque and flakes apart (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **Surface is wet and soft, and the salt is too strong**: the liquid was not blotted off after salting, or you bought pre-salted fish and salted it again. Use unseasoned fresh fish and blot it thoroughly before cooking. Weigh the salt again when the weight of the fish changes (see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/)).
