Chikuzenni is a home-style simmered dish of chicken and root vegetables that are stir-fried first and then simmered. The name comes from Chikuzen, in what is now the Fukuoka area. Chicken thigh, burdock, lotus root, taro and shiitake cook in a sweet and salty dashi, and the konnyaku adds some spring. It is served with a little liquid, and each ingredient has its own texture, with taro that is soft and sticky without falling apart.

Root vegetables in the same pot differ in hardness, so adjust thickness with [knife work](/principles/knife-work/). The drop lid sits directly on the food, so boiling liquid runs back down along the lid, helping the parts above the liquid heat and pick up the sauce (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). After the vegetables soften, the liquid is reduced moderately, which keeps their shape and also keeps the soy sauce from becoming too salty as water evaporates.

## Steps

1. Soak the dried shiitake in 240 ml of warm water for 30 minutes, until the centre of each cap is soft. Cut off the hard stems and halve the caps. Pass the soaking water through a fine sieve and keep it. Cut the chicken into diagonal chunks, toss with the sake and soy sauce for marinating the chicken, and refrigerate for 15 minutes, until the surface is evenly coated.
2. Lightly scrape the skin of the burdock and cut it into thin diagonal slices. Peel the lotus root and cut it into slices about 6 mm thick. Divide the 1000 ml of water between two bowls and add 0.5 tbsp rice vinegar to each. Soak the two vegetables separately for 15 minutes, until the cut surfaces stay pale, then drain.
3. Peel the taro, cut into chunks of about 3 cm and rinse off the surface slime. Cut the bamboo shoots to a similar size, the carrot into rolling chunks about 1 cm thick and the konnyaku into short slices about 6 mm thick. String the snow peas.
4. Bring the other 1000 ml of water to a boil over high heat for about 5 minutes, until the surface boils steadily. Add salt and blanch the snow peas for 30–60 seconds, until bright green and slightly pliable, then lift out and drain. Blanch the konnyaku in the same pot for 3 minutes, until the alkaline smell fades, then drain and discard the water.
5. Heat 1 tbsp of the toasted sesame oil in a wide pot over medium heat for about 1 minute, until it flows freely. Add the chicken and stir-fry for 3 minutes, until the surface turns white, then remove it. Add the remaining 0.5 tbsp of oil with all the root vegetables, shiitake and konnyaku, and stir-fry for 3 minutes, until each piece is glossy with oil.
6. Pour in the 480 ml of dashi and the strained shiitake water, and cook over medium heat for about 4 minutes, until it bubbles steadily at the edge, skimming off the foam. Add the sake, mirin, sugar, usukuchi soy sauce and 0.5 tsp salt for the simmering liquid, and return the chicken. The liquid should come to about eight tenths of the height of the ingredients.
7. Set a drop lid slightly smaller than the pot, or a round of parchment paper with a hole in the centre, on top. Simmer over low heat for 10 minutes, until the outside of the taro starts to soften. Remove the drop lid and cook another 10 minutes, until a bamboo skewer goes through the taro and lotus root, the centre of the chicken reaches 74°C (165°F), and some liquid is still left in the pot.
8. Taste the liquid and adjust with salt. Turn off the heat, cover and rest for 10 minutes, until the boiling stops and the surface of the ingredients is evenly coated. Add the snow peas when plating and serve with a little of the liquid.

## Key points

- **The lotus root is still hard but the taro has fallen apart**: the lotus slices are too thick, or repeated stirring broke up the soft taro. Cut the lotus thin, leave the taro in larger pieces, and swirl the pot instead of turning with a spatula in the second half. See [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/).
- **Root vegetables above the liquid are dry and hard**: the liquid is not circulating enough and the food is piled too high. Use a wide pot and a drop lid close to the food, and keep a small, continuous boil. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **Coated with sauce but bitterly salty**: it was reduced too dry, or usukuchi soy sauce was mistaken for a low-salt soy sauce. Usukuchi means light in colour. Stop once the root vegetables are cooked through and the liquid already tastes salty, and do not chase a completely dry pot. See [salt](/principles/salt/).
