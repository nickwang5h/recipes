Fish slice congee starts with a pot of fully cooked plain congee, then raw fish slices are rolled in and it is served as soon as they are just cooked. The rice grains break apart and the congee liquid becomes one with them; the fish slices stay whole, and the aroma of the ginger and green onion is kept for last. Evenly thick fish matters more for doneness than which white fish you use.

The silkiness of the congee comes from the rice absorbing water, [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) and the grains falling apart once soft. The fish slices only need a short time on the heat: once the proteins have set, more cooking makes the flesh tighten (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). So the congee is cooked through first and the fish goes in last; that is how you get both a silky congee and tender fish. The thin coating makes the surface slippery, but whether the fish stays tender still depends on turning off the heat in time.

## Steps

1. Rinse the rice in cold water 3 times, until the water is clearer than the first rinse, and drain. Run a hand over the fish and pick out any bones, pat dry and cut on the diagonal into large slices about 6 mm thick; shred the ginger and chop the green onion greens and cilantro.
2. Mix the cornstarch, the water for the coating, the salt for the fish and the white pepper, toss the fish slices in gently, then mix in the egg white and finally the oil. Refrigerate for about 15 minutes, until the coating clings thinly and there is no pool of liquid at the bottom of the bowl; use this time to cook the congee.
3. Put 1420 ml of water in a deep pot and heat over high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until the water boils. Add the rice, push it apart once along the bottom of the pot, and wait about 1–2 minutes for it to come back to a boil; add the oil for cooking the congee, turn to low heat, leave the lid ajar and cook for 25 minutes, until the grains have burst open and the centres crush easily.
4. Uncover and stir gently with a whisk for 1–2 minutes to break up the grains, until the congee liquid turns silky. Add 120 ml of hot water and cook over low heat for about 2 more minutes, until the rice and water have merged and the congee pours off the spoon in a continuous stream; if the rice still has a hard core, cook for 5 more minutes.
5. Turn the congee to medium heat for about 1 minute, until bubbles return to the surface. Add the fish slices one at a time, nudge them apart with the back of a spoon, add the shredded ginger, turn to medium-low and cook at a roll for 2–3 minutes, until the slices are completely white and opaque and the thickest part flakes easily; the egg white in the coating must also be cooked through, with the centre reaching 74°C (165°F).
6. Add the finishing salt, fish sauce and white pepper and nudge gently for about 15 seconds, until the seasoning is mixed in. Turn off the heat, divide among bowls, sprinkle with the green onion greens and cilantro, and eat hot.

## Key points

- **Rice is hard and the congee is thin**: the rice has not absorbed enough water or cooked apart. Finish the congee base before adding the fish; once the fish is in, longer cooking cannot rescue it (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)).
- **Fish slices are dry and stringy or fall into crumbs**: the slices were too thin, cooked too long or stirred repeatedly. Cut them into large slices of similar size and only nudge gently after the fish goes in (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **Pot bottom scorches while the top does not boil**: the congee is too thick or the heat is concentrated at the bottom. Use a heavy-bottomed pot, watch whether the bottom is sticking, and add the measured hot water and nudge gently (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
