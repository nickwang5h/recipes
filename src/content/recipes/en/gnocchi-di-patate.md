*Gnocchi di patate* are small dumplings made from mashed potato and flour. Cooked, they are soft but hold their shape, and the dimples catch the sauce. This is the traditional eggless version, with potato and flour in a ratio of 400 g to 150 g. Butter, sage and cheese tossed together are enough, or you can spoon over [ragù alla Bolognese](/recipes/ragu-alla-bolognese/).

The potatoes are cooked first, and their own [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) happens; the flour then binds the loose mash into a dough that can go into water. The wetter the potato, the more flour you have to add. More flour and longer kneading make the [gluten](/principles/gluten/) stronger, and the gnocchi turn heavy and tough. So cooking the potatoes in their skins, ricing them hot and letting the steam escape works better than adding flour again and again.

## Steps

1. Choose potatoes of similar size and put them in cold water with their skins on, with the water covering them. Cook until a thin knife slides easily through the centre with no hard core, usually about 30–40 minutes. Cook them whole, not cut into small pieces, because cut surfaces take in water.
2. Drain, and while hot peel them with a cloth between you and the potato, then press through a ricer or food mill into a fine mash. Spread the mash out and wait until the obvious steam has gone and no water pools on the surface. Do not blend it at high speed in a food processor, which makes it gluey.
3. While the mash is still warm, add the flour and fold with a bench scraper, then press gently by hand into an even dough. It only needs to hold together when pinched; do not knead until smooth and springy. Take one small dumpling and test it in gently simmering salted water; continue once it floats whole.
4. Dust the counter lightly with flour, divide the dough into several pieces and roll each into a rope about 2 cm thick, then cut into pieces about 3 cm long. Press each piece lightly over the back of a fork with your thumb to make shallow ridges and a dimple, and set them on a lightly floured tray with gaps between them.
5. Bring water to a boil in a wide pot, add salt and lower to a gentle simmer. Add the gnocchi in batches, nudging them gently to prevent sticking. When they float to the surface, lift one out and cut it open. If the centre has no dry flour and is evenly cooked, lift the rest of that batch with a slotted spoon.
6. In another pan, melt the butter and add the sage. Cook until the leaves are fragrant and the butter foams but has not browned or blackened. Add the drained gnocchi and shake gently to coat, without frying a hard crust. If the sauce is too dry, add a little of the gnocchi cooking water. Take off the heat, sprinkle with the cheese and plate right away.

## Key points

- **They fall apart in the water, leaving only crumbs in the pot**: the potato holds too much water, or there is not enough flour to bind it (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Test one first. If it falls apart, add a little flour and test again; next time drain the potatoes well and let enough steam escape.
- **Heavy gnocchi that chew like rubber**: too much flour was added or the dough was kneaded too long, so the gluten is too strong (see [gluten](/principles/gluten/)). Weigh the main ingredients, dust the counter only lightly, and stop handling once the dough comes together.
- **Rough surface, or whole gnocchi broken up by the water**: the water boils hard, or too many go into the pot at once (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Cook in batches in gently simmering water, and check and lift them out soon after they float rather than cooking them long.
