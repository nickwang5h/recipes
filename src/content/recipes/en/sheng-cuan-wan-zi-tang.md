Pork meatball soup puts raw ground pork straight into hot water to cook into meatballs, tender and springy to bite. The key comes before they go in the pot: first beat the meat with [salt](/principles/salt/) until it turns sticky, then beat in the green onion, ginger and Sichuan peppercorn water in portions. The muscle proteins that dissolve help the meat bind and hold water, and then [set](/principles/protein-coagulation/) when heated.

The egg white and potato starch also help the meatballs hold their shape, but the meat itself must be beaten sticky first. The soup is served with wood ear, dried daylily buds and glass noodles, so the broth is clear and the meatballs a little more substantial; the sides are cooked through first and put in the bowl, and the hot broth poured over them only brings the flavours together.

## Steps

1. Soak the dried wood ear and dried daylily buds separately until fully spread out, cut off the hard roots and wash; soak the glass noodles until soft (about 15 minutes). Soak the green onion, ginger and Sichuan peppercorns in 100 ml of water to bring out the flavour, rub the green onion and ginger, then strain and measure 80 ml of the flavoured water for later.
2. Cut the chilled pork into small pieces and chop it into a fine mince. Add 6 g of salt and 1 g of white pepper and stir in one direction until the mince turns sticky and holds together as one mass; hand-chopped or ground both work, and checking for coarse sinew matters more than the tool.
3. Add the flavoured water in three or four portions, waiting each time until the water is absorbed and there is no free water at the bottom of the bowl before adding the next portion. Mix in the egg white, then the potato starch until even, and finally the vegetable oil. The finished mince should be thick and resist the spoon, and a scoop should not spread flat at once; keep it refrigerated until needed.
4. Bring 1500 ml of water to a boil, add the wood ear and daylily buds, and cook until completely soft with no hard core. Add the glass noodles and cook through (about 3–4 minutes), lift out the sides and divide them between the bowls, leaving the broth in the pot.
5. Bring the broth to a bare simmer, with small bubbles at the edge of the pot. Squeeze out meatballs of about 2.5 cm through the ring of your thumb and forefinger and slide them into the broth with a wet spoon; keep the size even and add them in batches, letting the surface set first as they go in, and do not push them apart with the spoon.
6. After the meatballs float, keep cooking gently until cooked through (about 1 minute), and check with a food thermometer that the centre of the largest meatball reaches 71°C (160°F). Taste the broth and add salt, pepper and the chicken bouillon powder, if using, then ladle the soup with the meatballs into the bowls of sides.
7. Sprinkle with chopped green onion and the cilantro, if using, drizzle with toasted sesame oil in portions and eat hot.

## Key points

- **Meatballs fall apart at a touch**: the mince was not salted and beaten sticky first, or the water went in too fast. [Salt draws out some of the muscle protein](/principles/salt/); add water only after each portion is absorbed, and touch the meatballs as little as possible before they set.
- **Texture is powdery and lacks meat flavour**: starch was used in place of beating. [Starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) can help the shape, and 40 g is the amount for 500 g of meat, so for more people do not just add more starch.
- **Hard outside and raw inside**: the meatballs are uneven in size and the broth boiled too hard. [Protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) goes from the outside in, so keep it at a bare simmer and check the centre temperature.
