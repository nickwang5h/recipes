Béchamel (*Sauce béchamel*) is one of the five French [mother sauces](/cuisines/french/), made from milk, butter and flour. It can be spread over a gratin of vegetables, sandwiched into a *croque-monsieur*, and it is also the *besciamella* in Italian lasagne. A finished sauce should coat the back of a spoon and still pour slowly, and its colour should stay creamy white.

**The thickness comes from the starch in the flour.** Flour is first stirred into butter to make a pale roux, which disperses the grains; once the milk goes in, the starch granules take up water and undergo [gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) as they heat. The butter helps disperse the flour and adds flavour, but it is the starch swelling with water that thickens the milk.

## Steps

1. Heat the milk in a small pot for about 3–5 minutes, until steam just rises at the edge, then take it off the heat; do not let it boil. Weigh out the flour and butter, and have a whisk and a soft spatula that can scrape along the bottom of the pot ready.
2. In a separate heavy-bottomed pot, melt the butter over medium-low heat, add the flour and stir well, stirring constantly for about 2 minutes, until there is no dry flour and the roux is fine and bubbling. Cook the roux only slightly, keeping it pale, without browned spots.
3. First move the pot off the heat, pour in a little warm milk and whisk the roux apart. At first it turns into a thick paste; stir until smooth before adding the next addition. Once the paste flows smoothly, pour in the rest of the milk a little at a time.
4. Put the pot back over medium-low heat and keep stirring, especially sweeping the corners of the bottom, for about 2–4 minutes. The sauce gradually thickens until slow bubbles break on the surface and the whisk leaves faint trails.
5. Turn the heat to low and scrape the bottom as it cooks, keeping the sauce at a gentle bubble, for about 1 more minute, until it no longer tastes of raw flour. Lift a spoonful: the sauce should be an even layer on the back of the spoon, and pour back into the pot as one continuous ribbon, not fall in clumps.
6. Add the salt, white pepper and a little nutmeg, taste, and turn off the heat. Use it straight away for a gratin or a filling; if it has to wait, press plastic wrap against the surface so a skin does not form, and stir before using.

## Key points

- **White lumps of flour**: the milk went in too fast, so the outside of a lump of flour [gelatinized](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) first and wrapped the dry flour inside. Stir the first addition of milk into an even thick paste before thinning it further; whisk small lumps apart, and strain out stubborn ones.
- **Thin sauce with a raw-flour taste**: [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) is not finished, or too little flour was used. Get the whole pot bubbling and cook it evenly over low heat; do not sprinkle dry flour into hot sauce, because it forms new lumps.
- **Brown colour and a burnt taste**: the roux was cooked too dark, or the bottom of the pot scorched after the milk went in. The darker the roux, the less thickening power it has (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Cook the roux only to a pale colour; if the bottom has scorched, switch to another pot and do not scrape the burnt layer in.
- **Too thick once cooled**: gelatinized starch thickens as it cools (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Reheat over low heat, adding milk a little at a time and stirring until it flows.
