*Kongnamul-muchim* is a common Korean side dish of seasoned soybean sprouts. The bean heads taste of cooked beans, the stems keep a little crunch, and the garlic and sesame oil only lightly coat the surface. It can be eaten warm or cooled. Make sure you use soybean sprouts: the big soybean heads attached to thicker stems are not the mung bean sprouts common in mainstream supermarkets.

Cooking covered in a little water works because the boiling water at the bottom makes steam, which carries heat to the sprouts above (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Judge doneness by the bean heads: the stems can be soft before the heads are cooked through. Drain well before seasoning, because water clinging to the sprouts dilutes the dressing (see [salt](/principles/salt/)).

## Steps

1. Gently wash the soybean sprouts in clean water, skim off the floating bean skins, and pick out any brown or mushy ones. The root tails can stay; cut off only the clearly browned parts, then drain.
2. Put them in a pot with a tight-fitting lid, add 120 ml of water and a pinch of salt, and cover. Pile the sprouts loosely and don't press them down by hand, so steam can move between the stems.
3. Cook over medium-high heat for about 10 minutes, checking the bottom of the pot if you smell scorching. The sprouts should be hot all the way through, the heads no longer hard and raw, and the stems still springy when bent; if the heads are still hard, keep cooking covered, and add a little hot water if the pot goes dry.
4. Tip into a colander and drain off the cooking water thoroughly, then spread the sprouts out to let the strongest steam escape. Don't dunk them back into a bowl of cold water, and don't squeeze them; just drain until no more water collects in the bowl.
5. While the sprouts are still warm, add the garlic, green onion, soy sauce, toasted sesame oil and toasted sesame seeds. For a slightly spicy version add the gochugaru too, and toss gently from the bottom with two spoons so the seasoning coats evenly.
6. Taste a sprout with its head, adjust the salt, and transfer to a small plate. To serve cold, put it into a shallow container and refrigerate promptly; eat refrigerated leftovers within two days, and don't leave the whole bowl out on the table for long.

## Key points

- **Stems are soft but the heads are still raw and hard**: the bean halves need longer than the thin stems to heat through. Check the large heads; don't stop the heat at the quick blanching time you'd use for mung bean sprouts. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **Water pools at the bottom of the bowl after tossing**: the cooking water wasn't drained, and the salt draws out more. Drain well before seasoning and serve right after tossing; don't add more soy sauce to cover a thin flavour. See [salt](/principles/salt/).
- **The sesame oil tastes faint and the garlic tastes dull**: the seasonings were cooked together for a long time and the volatile aromas are gone. Cook the sprouts first, then drain them off the heat before tossing with garlic and sesame oil. See [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).
