*Spaghetti all'amatriciana* comes from Amatrice. Its stars are cured pork jowl, tomatoes and the local sheep's milk cheese. The jowl is rendered first, the tomatoes are reduced in that fat, and then the sauce is combined with the pasta and cheese. Pecorino Romano is easier to find in Canada and is usually saltier, so use less salt in the pasta water.

The rendered fat gives the sauce body, and the tomatoes and aged cheese supply [umami](/principles/umami/). The final tossing disperses the fat into the tomato juice and a little pasta water, forming a sauce that clings to the pasta (see [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)). The recipe recognized by the municipality of Amatrice uses 125 g jowl and 100 g sheep's milk cheese for every 500 g of pasta, starts the pan with one tablespoon of olive oil, may include a little dry white wine, and has no garlic or onion; the amounts here are converted for 400 g of pasta. The cheese goes in after the sauce has reduced, which keeps its texture soft.

## Steps

1. Cut the guanciale into strips of even thickness and grate the cheese finely. Crush the tomatoes open in a bowl and keep the juice with them. Wash the red chili and cut a slit along its length.
2. Put the guanciale, chili and olive oil in a frying pan and heat over medium heat. Once fat starts to render, lower the heat. Stir until the fatty parts are translucent and the edges of the lean parts are golden, with enough fat in the pan; the meat should stay springy and not be fried hard and dry.
3. Pour in the white wine and scrape up the light brown bits stuck to the bottom of the pan, then cook until the alcohol smell is gone. Lift out the guanciale, leave the fat and chili in the pan, and add the tomatoes and juice. If there are any black burnt bits, remove them before cooking the sauce.
4. Simmer gently, uncovered, for about 20–30 minutes, stirring now and then, until the tomatoes have broken down and a line drawn across the bottom of the pan holds briefly, with no ring of watery liquid around the edge. Return the guanciale, cook a little longer and take out the chili.
5. When the sauce is nearly ready, cook the pasta in a separate pot with less salt than usual. Cook until the centre still has a slightly firm core, set aside a cup of the pasta water, then lift the pasta into the sauce pan.
6. Toss over medium heat until the pasta is cooked to your liking and the sauce coats the strands. If it is too dry, add pasta water a tablespoon at a time. The bottom of the pan should have a moist, thin sauce, not tomato soup.
7. Turn off the heat and move the pan off the burner. Wait until the sauce stops bubbling. Sprinkle in the sheep's milk cheese in batches, tossing as you go, and add a little pasta water if it gets too tight. Finally taste the pasta with the sauce for saltiness and plate right away.

## Key points

- **The guanciale is hard and bitter**: the lean part has been fried until burnt, or the strips were cut too thin. After the fat renders, lower the heat and lift the meat out once it is golden; keep it out of the pan while the tomatoes cook and put it back at the end. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **The sauce floats oil and will not cling to the pasta**: the tomatoes were reduced too dry, and the water and fat were not mixed. Add a little pasta water and keep tossing until the sauce turns glossy. See [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/).
- **The cheese clumps and the dish is too salty**: the pan was still at a rolling boil, or too much salt went in the pasta water early on. Stir in the cheese in batches off the heat, taste a whole forkful before adding salt, and remember that more cheese also adds more saltiness. See [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/) and [salt](/principles/salt/).
