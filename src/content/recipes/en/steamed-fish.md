Cantonese cooking prizes "clear steaming": the fish must be fresh, the steaming time just right, and seasoning comes only at the end. A few slices of ginger under the fish lift it off the plate, so steam surrounds the body on all sides and delivers heat by condensing on it (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Fish protein coagulates fast, and the flesh goes from translucent to milky white often within a minute or two. Steam a little longer and the flesh tightens and turns dry (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).

The liquid that steams out carries blood and a fishy smell, so pour it off. The fragrance comes from the last two steps: sizzling oil wakes up the shredded ginger and green onion, and the hot soy sauce carries the savoury saltiness onto the flesh.

## Steps

1. Mix the ingredients for the soy sauce mixture in a small bowl and set aside.
2. Cut two or three diagonal slashes on each side of the fish at its thickest part, down to the bone. Put three slices of ginger on a plate and rest the fish on top.
3. Bring the water in the steamer to a full boil and wait for plenty of steam before putting the plate in. Cover tightly and steam over high heat for about 9 minutes.
4. Pull apart a slash on the thickest part of the back with chopsticks: if the flesh next to the bone has turned white and comes away from the bone easily, turn off the heat; if it is still translucent, steam for 1 more minute.
5. Take out the plate, carefully pour off all the liquid on it and remove the ginger slices underneath. Spread half of the shredded ginger, the green part of the green onion and the cilantro over the fish.
6. Put the oil for the hot oil step in a small pot with the remaining shredded ginger, and heat until bubbles form around the ginger and the oil just begins to smoke lightly. Pour in the soy sauce mixture and bring it to a boil, then add the white part of the green onion.
7. Pour the hot oil and sauce from the pot over the fish together; you will hear a sizzle. Serve right away.

## Key points

- **The flesh is dry, but still red next to the bone**: the heat was too low and the steam too weak, so the steaming took longer and the outside overcooked first. Always wait for a full boil and plenty of steam before putting in the fish. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **Coarse flesh and a lot of water on the plate**: it was steamed too long, and the protein tightened and squeezed out water. Better to steam 1 minute less and check. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **A fishy smell**: the steamed liquid was not fully poured off, or the fish was not fresh. A fresh fish has clear eyes and bright red gills, and the body springs back when pressed.
- **A different size of fish**: time depends on the thickness at the thickest point, not the weight. Thin fish fillets need only 3–5 minutes, and a large fish needs more than 10 minutes, always judged by the flesh next to the bone turning white.
