White fungus and lotus seed congee is a sweet soup made without rice: the white fungus is soft and tender, the lotus seeds are floury in the centre, and the soup is slightly silky. Tearing the white fungus into small florets makes it easier for water and heat to reach the inside; keep the heat at a gentle boil to leave plenty of time for softening (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

Lotus seeds contain starch, and they only turn floury once the centre has absorbed enough water and [gelatinized](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Check the white fungus and the lotus seeds separately for doneness: a thick soup does not mean the seeds are soft, and cooked seeds may mean the fungus is still crunchy. Dried white fungus swells after soaking, so buy and weigh it by dry weight, discard the soaking water, and use clean water for the actual cooking.

## Steps

1. Soak the white fungus and the cored lotus seeds separately in clean water for about 2 hours, until the fungus has fully opened and the seeds have taken up enough water. Snip off the yellow hard stem of the fungus and tear it into small florets. Check the lotus seeds for any remaining green bitter core.
2. Wash the jujubes, soak them for 10–20 minutes and remove the pits; wash and drain the goji berries. Rinse dried ingredients before cooking, so there are fewer fine bits of grit in the soup.
3. Add clean water to the pot and bring it to a boil, then add the white fungus, lotus seeds and jujubes. Push gently with a spoon so the fungus is submerged, and cook until the whole pot comes back to a boil.
4. Cover with the lid slightly ajar and adjust the heat so the surface keeps bubbling lightly. Cook for about 1 hour. Check the water level partway through; if the soup has clearly reduced and the ingredients are still hard, add hot water back to the original level.
5. Add half of the rock sugar and the goji berries, turn to low heat and cook for about 30 minutes more. Stir gently from the bottom every so often, so the fungus does not pile up on the bottom and get too much heat.
6. Scoop out one lotus seed and press it open: it is done when the centre crushes apart with no hard core. The fungus should be soft, and the soup should run off the spoon in a slightly connected thread. If it is not there yet, keep cooking over low heat; do not turn off the heat just by the clock.
7. Add the remaining rock sugar and stir gently for 5–10 minutes, until the sugar has fully dissolved. Taste, turn off the heat, and when serving, divide the fungus, lotus seeds and soup together into the bowl.

## Key points

- **Lotus seeds soft outside and hard inside**: the centre has not absorbed enough water and has not [gelatinized](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Pick one and press it open to check, and keep cooking gently; if the pot is low on water, add hot water first.
- **Soup thick but the fungus still crunchy**: the water evaporated, so the soup thickened first while the fungus had not softened. There must be enough water and enough time (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)); add water and keep cooking until soft.
- **Scorched at the bottom and thin on top**: the fungus sat in a pile at the bottom and the bottom of the pot was heated too hard in one spot. Turn the heat down and stir up from the bottom (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)); if there is already a burnt smell, do not scrape the burnt layer into the soup.
