Steamed pork with rice flour coats pork belly in coarse rice flour, with potatoes underneath, and steams the whole bowl until soft and tender. The rice flour [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) as it absorbs water, going from dry hard grains to a soft layer of flour that holds the meat juices; the skin and connective tissue soften with moist heat and time (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)).

Choose pork belly with layers of fat and lean, so that the rendered fat moistens the flour layer and the potatoes. Moisten the rice flour first, and leave a little space between the pork slices. Steam heats the outside of the bowl first, and the centre of the bowl heats through slowly; if the meat is pressed into a tight lump, the outside is cooked while the inside is not.

## Steps

1. Cut the pork belly into slices about 5 cm long, 3 cm wide and 5 mm thick, and finely chop the ginger and garlic. Keep the slices as even in thickness as possible and cut through the skin as well, so they are easy to pick up after steaming.
2. Mix the pork with the light soy sauce, dark soy sauce, Shaoxing wine, minced ginger, minced garlic, sugar and the optional doubanjiang, then cover and marinate in the refrigerator for 30 minutes. If the rice flour already contains salt, first reduce the soy sauce and doubanjiang according to the package directions.
3. Add the rice flour and toss for about 2 minutes; pour in half of the water for mixing the flour first, then add more in stages. The flour should cling evenly to the meat, with the grains moist, no pool of thin batter at the bottom of the bowl and no patches of dry flour.
4. Peel the potatoes, cut them into slices about 8 mm thick and lay them on the bottom of a heatproof bowl. Arrange the pork slices loosely on top with the flour side facing out, without pressing them down; choose a bowl that leaves room for expansion.
5. When the water in the steamer has boiled, put in the bowl of pork, cover and steam for 60 minutes over a heat that keeps the steam coming steadily. Check the water at the bottom along the way, and when the level has clearly dropped, add hot water along the side of the pot so the water does not pour directly into the bowl of pork.
6. Check the thickest piece of pork in the centre of the bowl: a chopstick should pass through the skin and meat easily, the flour grains should be soft with no hard core, and the potatoes should break apart when pinched. If there is still some resistance, steam for another 10–15 minutes; take it out when soft and tender.

## Key points

- **The rice flour is dry and hard with undercooked grains**: there was not enough water when mixing in the flour, and fat cannot replace the water needed for gelatinization (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Sprinkle a small amount of hot water on the dry parts and keep steaming until the flour grains are soft all the way through.
- **The outside is soft but the skin in the centre of the bowl is still tough**: the pork is piled too thick or pressed too tight, so [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/) to the centre is slow. Arrange the slices loosely, and judge by whether a chopstick can pierce the skin in the centre of the bowl, not by the fat showing on the surface.
- **The meat is cooked but hard to bite**: the collagen in the skin and sinew has not yet softened (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep the steam going and give it more time; there is no need to make the water in the pot boil violently.
- **The flour layer is a thin paste and the potatoes sit in water**: too much water was used to mix the flour, or condensation dripped into the bowl. Add the water in stages next time, and check whether the lid is dripping into the bowl continuously.
