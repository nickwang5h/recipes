Cantonese braised beef brisket with daikon is a slow braise of tendon-rich meat and white radish. Brisket goes from cooked but tough to easy to bite through because the collagen slowly converts (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Daikon takes up liquid quickly and softens quickly, so it goes in only when the meat is nearly tender, which lets both main ingredients keep their shape when they come out.

Chu hou paste and red fermented bean curd are the base of the savoury flavour, with oyster sauce and light soy sauce adding salt and savour. Several of these sauces contain salt, so mix them and fry them until fragrant first, and check the saltiness at the end (see [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/)). Dark soy sauce only deepens the colour, and it is easier to control when added in portions.

## Steps

1. Peel the daikon and cut into rolling chunks about 3 cm. Put the whole piece of brisket in cold water with 2 slices of ginger and the green onion knot, bring to a boil and skim off the foam, then keep a low rolling boil for 5–10 minutes, until the foam clearly lessens. Lift out and rinse off anything clinging to it. Discard the blanching water and the green onion knot (see [blanching and removing off-odours](/principles/blanching/)).
2. Cut the brisket into pieces about 4 cm, drain and pat off the surface water. The blanched brisket will still shrink later, so keep the pieces thick when cutting and avoid cutting the lean meat into small crumbly bits.
3. Crush the red fermented bean curd and mix it with the chu hou paste, oyster sauce and light soy sauce. Keep the rock sugar separate so you can watch it melt, and leave the salt to the end. Have the ginger and garlic ready, and put the spices into a spice bag you can lift out.
4. Add the oil to a pot and stir-fry the remaining ginger and garlic slices over medium heat until fragrant, then add the brisket and stir-fry until the surface steam lessens. Turn to medium-low, add the mixed sauces and stir until the meat is evenly coated and smells of the sauce. Add the dark soy sauce in portions if you want a darker colour.
5. Pour in the starting hot water, then move the brisket and sauce to a small stewpot and add the rock sugar and spices. The liquid should just cover the meat; add hot water if the pot is wide. Bring to a boil, cover and turn to low, so small bubbles keep rising at the edge of the pot.
6. Braise for about 2 hours first, checking the level partway through, and add hot water if the top of the meat is exposed and the liquid has clearly reduced. Test the thickest piece with a chopstick; when it goes in and the tendon still has a little chew, add the daikon. If the meat is still hard, keep braising and add the daikon later.
7. Keep braising for about 30 minutes, until the daikon is slightly translucent and a chopstick passes through it, and the tendon in the meat is soft. Remove the spices, uncover and reduce a little, leaving a thick sauce that is good over rice. Taste a piece of daikon with some broth, then decide whether to add salt.

## Key points

- **The brisket is too tough to chew, while the daikon has fallen apart**: the two went in the pot too early together. Check the tendon as in [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/) and add the daikon only when the meat is nearly soft; if the daikon softens first, lift it out first.
- **Sauce sticks to the bottom and tastes bitter**: the sauces were fried too dry, or the pot ran short of liquid during the long braise. See [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/); add hot water as soon as the sauce smell comes up, and keep liquid covering the meat as it braises.
- **Grey scum remains after blanching, and the broth tastes cloudy**: what was clinging to the meat stayed on, or blanching water was carried into the stewpot. See [blanching and removing off-odours](/principles/blanching/); change the water and clean the surface of the meat before braising.
