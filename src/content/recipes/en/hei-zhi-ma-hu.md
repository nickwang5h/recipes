Black sesame paste grinds fragrant roasted black sesame seeds fine and cooks them with rice slurry into a sweet paste, a bowl common among Cantonese sweet soups. The sesame supplies the aroma and the rice supplies the body. When done, it thinly coats the back of a spoon, feels fine and smooth in the mouth, and still flows when scooped.

Once the glutinous rice is ground into a slurry, its starch absorbs water and [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) when heated, holding the scattered fine sesame particles in the liquid. The rice slurry sinks to the bottom in cold water and sticks to the pot when heated; stir along the bottom from the moment the heat goes on so the whole pot thickens evenly (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). The sesame must be ground fine before it goes in the pot, and the rice slurry must be cooked through.

## Steps

1. Rinse the glutinous rice, add the 300 ml of soaking water, cover and soak in the refrigerator for 4 hours, until the grains have swelled and can be snapped with a fingernail. Drain off the soaking water.
2. Spread the roasted black sesame seeds in a thin layer in a dry skillet and stir over medium-low heat for 2–3 minutes, until the sesame aroma is clearly released. Tip onto a plate at once and spread out to cool for 5 minutes, until it no longer feels hot to the touch. Black sesame hides browning, so if you see burnt smoke it has already gone too far.
3. Put the glutinous rice, sesame and the 480 ml of clean water for grinding into a blender and blend for 1–3 minutes, until the rice grains disappear and the sesame becomes a fine slurry. Stop, scrape down the sides of the jar and blend again. Scoop a little to check: you should see no whole sesame seeds or coarse rice bits.
4. Pour the slurry into a heavy-bottomed pot, add the remaining 720 ml of clean water and stir well. Heat over medium heat for about 8–10 minutes, stirring along the bottom with a spatula the whole time, until the slurry has thickened slightly and bubbles appear at both the edge and the centre of the pot.
5. Turn the heat to low, add 50 g of sugar and keep stirring and cooking for 2 minutes, until the sugar has dissolved and the rice slurry no longer tastes of raw rice. When the paste runs down in a continuous stream as you lift the spoon and leaves a thin coat on the back of it, turn off the heat and divide among bowls.

## Key points

- **Gritty in the mouth**: the sesame and rice were not ground fine, and cooking longer will not make them finer; it only [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) the starch. Blend the cold slurry fine before it goes in the pot, and scrape coarse bits from the blender jar wall back in.
- **Lumps at the bottom, thin on top**: the rice slurry settles and the bottom of the pot heats and gelatinizes first (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Stir the cold slurry evenly, then keep stirring along the bottom once the heat is on; do not wait until it boils.
- **Bitter paste**: the sesame was scorched, or the rice slurry caught on the bottom; both are local overheating (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Roasted sesame only needs a short toasting to revive its aroma, so take it off the heat as soon as it is done. Once the burnt bitterness is in the whole pot, you can only start over.
