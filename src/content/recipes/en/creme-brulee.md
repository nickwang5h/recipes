*Crème brûlée* is a French custard dessert with a tender, smooth base and a thin, crisp top. The base is plain cream, egg yolks and vanilla, baked in small ramekins in a water bath. The sugar on top is torched only once the custard is completely cold, so that when you crack it you get both the crisp crust and the cool, smooth custard.

The two layers use two different kinds of heat. The yolks [coagulate](/principles/protein-coagulation/) and hold the cream in a fine network, and the water bath slows the heating around the ramekins (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). The top is heated quickly with a torch so the sugar melts and [caramelizes](/principles/caramelization/), sending little heat into the custard below. The prep time includes two hours of chilling.

## Steps

1. Preheat the oven to 150°C (300°F). Put the cream in a small pot and warm it until steam rises at the edge; take it off the heat, as it does not need to boil.
2. Gently stir the egg yolks and 50 g of sugar together, then add the warm cream in several additions, stirring as you pour. Stir in the vanilla extract without whipping the mixture into a thick foam.
3. Strain the custard, skim off the foam on the surface and divide it among 4 shallow ramekins. Each holds about 120 ml, and the depth should be kept at about 2 cm, since even thickness helps them all set at the same time.
4. Set the ramekins in a deep baking pan, put the pan in the oven, and then slowly add hot water until it comes halfway up the ramekins. Be careful not to splash water into the custard.
5. Bake in the water bath for about 35–55 minutes, checking shallow ramekins from 30 minutes. When you shake them gently, the edge should be fixed and the centre should wobble as a whole like a small piece of jelly, with no ripples of liquid; the centre temperature should reach at least 74°C (165°F). Take them out at that point, without waiting until the whole ramekin is too firm to wobble.
6. Lift the ramekins out of the water bath and set them on a rack to let the heat go. While still warm, move them to the fridge, and once cool, cover and refrigerate for at least 2 hours, until the centre is completely cold and the texture has set.
7. Before serving, gently blot the condensation off the surface, sprinkle about 5 g of sugar over each ramekin and turn it to spread an even thin layer. Keep the torch moving until all the sugar has melted to amber; let it stand for about 1 minute so the crust hardens, then eat at once.

## Key points

- **Holes in the custard, water seeping out, like scrambled egg**: the water bath boiled or the custard baked too long, and the protein network contracted too tightly. Keep the heat gentle and judge by the overall wobble and the temperature (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **Caramel thick and bitter**: the sugar was uneven, or the torch stayed too long in one spot. Spread it thin, keep the flame moving and stop when it turns amber; there is no way to rescue a blackened sugar crust (see [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/)).
- **Sugar crust goes soft quickly**: the custard was not cold through, the surface was damp, or it stood too long after torching. Sugar absorbs water from the custard, so wipe the surface dry and torch just before serving (see [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/)).
