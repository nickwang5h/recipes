Beef bourguignon (*bœuf bourguignon*) puts browned beef into red wine and stock for a long, slow stew, and is finished with pearl onions and mushrooms. The pieces of beef should come apart easily with a fork, and the sauce should coat the back of a spoon in a thin layer.

The dish rests on two things. The [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) when you brown the meat gives a roasted aroma. In the moist, slow heat of the stew, the [collagen in the connective tissue gradually turns to gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), and the meat softens. Chuck is rich in connective tissue, which is why it suits this kind of stew. The wine gives acidity and aroma; how tender the meat gets depends on how long it stews.

## Steps

1. Simmer the bacon in water over low heat for about 10 minutes, drain and pat dry, then fry it in the stew pot's oil until lightly coloured and take it out. Preheat the oven to about 230°C (450°F).
2. Brown the beef in batches. When the oil in the pot is hot, add the dried pieces of beef with gaps between them, and brown them on all sides before taking them out. After each batch, wait for the pot to heat up again before starting the next.
3. In the same pot, brown the carrot and onion, then pour off the excess oil. Return the beef and bacon, and toss with a little salt, black pepper and the flour. Roast uncovered in the oven for about 4 minutes, stir, roast for about 4 minutes more, then take the pot out and lower the oven to about 160°C (325°F).
4. Add the red wine and enough stock to just cover the meat, scrape up the brown film from the bottom of the pot, and stir in the tomato paste, garlic, thyme and bay leaf. Bring to a gentle simmer on the stove.
5. Cover, move to the oven, and adjust the stovetop heat as needed so the liquid only bubbles lightly. After about 2.5–3 hours, test the meat with a fork. It is done when the fork goes in easily and a twist of the fork loosens the piece.
6. In another pot, use part of the butter and oil to brown the pearl onions on the surface, add the garnish stock, cover and braise over low heat until the centres are soft and the onions still hold their shape, then cook off the extra liquid at the end.
7. In one more pot, use the rest of the butter and oil to stir-fry the mushrooms in batches, until the steam has cleared and the surface is golden, then set aside. If the pot is crowded, do two batches so the mushrooms do not sit in their own juices.
8. Lift out the meat and bacon, strain the braising liquid into a small pot and skim off the fat. Reduce it if it is too thin, or add stock if it is too thick, until it coats the back of a spoon in a thin layer, then season with salt and black pepper.
9. Return the meat, bacon, pearl onions and mushrooms to the sauce and warm gently, then sprinkle with parsley. Serve with boiled potatoes or bread, which suit the rich sauce.

## Key points

- **The surface of the meat is grey**: too much moisture or a crowded pot makes it hard for a full [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) to happen on the surface. Pat it dry, brown in batches, and do not rush to move the pieces.
- **The meat is cooked but still tough**: the [collagen needs more time to turn to gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/). Check that there is still liquid in the pot, keep stewing gently, and judge by how the meat gives under a fork.
- **The meat falls apart but is dry and stringy**: the stew boiled too hard and too much water was squeezed out of the meat. Gelatin makes the mouthfeel silky but cannot bring back the lost juices (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep the liquid at a faint bubble and stop when a fork goes in easily.
