*Nam chim chaeo* is a dipping sauce for grilled meat from northeastern Thailand, often served with grilled chicken, grilled pork and sticky rice. The first taste is sour, then salty, then hot, and a little sugar rounds off the lime's sourness. Some households sour it with tamarind; this version uses lime, with dried chili, shallot and toasted rice powder. [Acid](/principles/acid/) makes fatty meat feel fresh, so taste the sauce with the meat to judge whether salt and sour are in balance.

The **toasted rice powder** is where the roasted aroma of this sauce comes from. Raw glutinous rice is toasted in a dry pan until brown and then ground, and the sugars and amino acids in the rice make aroma as they heat (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Cilantro and lime bring fresh aromas, so they go in last and the sauce is eaten right after mixing, which loses less aroma (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). The finished sauce flows, with fine rice grains and chili flakes in it.

## Steps

1. Toast the rice powder. Spread the raw glutinous rice in a small dry pan, with no oil, and stir constantly over medium-low heat for about 10 minutes, until the grains turn an even light brown and smell like toasted grain. Tip it onto a plate at once to cool, then grind to a powder with a few fine grains, and measure out 1 tbsp.
2. Toast the dried chili. Stir it in a dry pan over low heat, and take it out as soon as you smell it, before it turns black. Let it cool, grind to fine flakes, and measure out 1 tbsp.
3. Mix the sauce base. Stir the fish sauce, lime juice and shaved palm sugar together until you can see no sugar crumbs at the bottom of the bowl. Stir in the shallot and the ground dried chili.
4. Just before serving, stir in the cilantro and the measured toasted rice powder. Taste it with a small piece of cooked meat; the sour and salty should be clear, with sweetness in last place. If it is not sour enough, add lime juice a drop at a time, stirring and tasting again.

It goes well with [Gai Yang](/recipes/gai-yang/). Stir gently before each dip so the rice powder and chili flakes spread through the sauce again.

## Key points

- **Burnt, bitter taste**: the rice or chili was toasted black, and the roasted aroma has become a burnt one. Use lower heat and pour it out as soon as the colour is right, so the residual heat does not keep toasting it; redo a batch that has turned black (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).
- **Gritty rice grains**: the glutinous rice was ground too coarse, and whole broken grains remain in the sauce. Cool the rice before grinding, grind it slightly coarser than flour, and look for large grains before it goes into the sauce. The finer it is ground, the more aroma comes out (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
- **Only sweetness, and the grilled meat feels heavy**: too much sugar covered the sour and salty notes that should stand out. Mix it by the measured amounts first, then taste it with the meat. If it is already too sweet, mix a separate sugar-free base and blend it in (see [Acid: Balance, Softening and Stability](/principles/acid/)).
- **The aroma fades after mixing in advance**: ground rice powder and cut herbs lose aroma easily. The preparation can be done ahead, but keep the rice powder and cilantro until just before serving (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
