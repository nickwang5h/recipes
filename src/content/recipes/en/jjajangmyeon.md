Jajangmyeon (*jajangmyeon*) pours a dark brown chunjang sauce over thick wheat noodles, and it is a staple at Korean-Chinese restaurants and in home kitchens. Chunjang is made from fermented soybeans, wheat and other ingredients, with a [fermented savoury depth](/principles/fermentation/); the pork and onion add saltiness, savoury notes and sweetness, and the radish, potato and zucchini leave soft, recognizable pieces in the sauce.

The key to this sauce is that the chunjang meets the oil first and is fried briefly until fragrant, then cooked with water (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). At the end a starch slurry ties the meat and vegetable juices into a glossy, light thickening (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). A sauce that clings to the noodles yet still runs off the spoon is the one that mixes evenly.

## Steps

1. Pat the pork dry, then dice the pork and the four vegetables separately. Stir the potato starch, sugar and 60 ml of cold water together and set aside; stir again before it goes into the pot.
2. In a deep skillet, heat 1 tbsp of vegetable oil over medium-high heat for 1 minute, until the oil spreads easily. Add the pork belly and stir-fry for 4–5 minutes, until the surface is golden and the fat renders. Spoon off the extra pork fat, leaving the diced pork and a thin layer of oil.
3. Add the radish and stir-fry for 1 minute, then add the potato, onion and zucchini and stir-fry for 3 minutes, until the edges of the onion are translucent and every piece is coated with oil.
4. Push the meat and vegetables to the edge of the pan and add the remaining 2 tbsp of vegetable oil and the chunjang in the middle. Turn the heat to medium-low and keep stirring the paste in the centre for about 1 minute, until it is fragrant and fine bubbles appear at the edges, then mix it into the meat and vegetables right away.
5. Add 480 ml of water and heat over medium heat for about 2–3 minutes, until the sauce bubbles, then cover and cook over low heat for 3–4 minutes. The radish and potato should be easy to pierce with chopsticks, and the pork should reach 71°C (160°F) in the centre; if the pieces are still firm, cook another 2–3 minutes.
6. Stir the starch slurry again and pour it in two additions along the edge of the pan, pushing the sauce as you pour. Cook over medium heat for 1 minute, until the thickened sauce is evenly glossy, with no white starch, and clings to the diced vegetables. Stir in the toasted sesame oil and turn off the heat.
7. Bring a separate pot of noodle water to a boil over high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until it is boiling continuously. Add the fresh noodles and cook for the time on the package, usually about 4–6 minutes, until there is no raw starch core in the centre and the noodles are chewy when bitten.
8. Rinse the lifted noodles under cold water for about 30 seconds, until they separate and the surface is no longer slippery, drain well and divide among four bowls. Pour the hot sauce over, add the optional cucumber strips, and mix and eat right away.

## Key points

- **Sauce is bitter with scorched spots on the bottom**: chunjang is thick and burns easily if it hits a hot dry pan directly. Leave enough oil in the middle, stir over medium-low heat, and mix in the vegetables and water as soon as it smells fragrant. See [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).
- **Dark sauce around hard potato**: the dice are too big, or the thickening went in too early and made stirring hard. Cut evenly, and thicken only after the radish and potato can be pierced through. See [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/).
- **Thickener forms jelly-like lumps**: the starch settled and was not stirred up again, or it went in all at once without the sauce being pushed around. Disperse it in cold water, pour it in stages, and cook until the whole pan is glossy before taking it off the heat. See [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
