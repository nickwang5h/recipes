In celtuce leaf pancakes, the egg joins the chopped leaves into one sheet. When the beaten egg is heated, the proteins unfold and link with each other, forming a network that holds up the leaves (see [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). The starch absorbs the water from the egg and the leaves and [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) when heated, making the thin pancake easier to flip whole.

The chopped leaves should be spread evenly and the pancake should be thin. Heat enters the lower layer of the pancake from the bottom of the pan first and then slowly moves to the upper layer; when the underside is golden, the top may not yet have set (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Controlling the heat and waiting for the edges to set is a better guide to when to flip than colour alone.

## Steps

1. Pick out any tough or yellowed celtuce leaves and remove any obviously coarse leaf stems. Wash the leaves one by one, shake off the surface water and then chop; leaves that go into the egg dripping wet make the batter too thin.
2. Put the chopped leaves in a bowl, crack in the two eggs and add 15 g of potato starch, 5 ml of light soy sauce and the chicken bouillon powder if using. Stir until the whites and yolks are mixed and the starch lumps are gone, with the leaves evenly suspended in the egg. Do not add any extra water.
3. Add 30 ml of oil to a skillet and tilt the pan so the oil covers the bottom. Heat over medium-low heat until a little batter makes a gentle sizzle when it goes in; do not wait for the oil to smoke.
4. Stir the batter once more just before pouring, so the starch does not settle at the bottom of the bowl. Pour it into the pan and spread it right away into a thin pancake of even thickness. If the pan is small, fry several small pancakes and use the oil by batch, so the middle does not pile up thick.
5. Fry the first side for about 2 minutes, until the edge has set, the underside is light golden and a spatula can slide under and lift the whole pancake. If there is still a lot of runny egg on the surface, lower the heat and keep cooking; do not flip yet.
6. Support the pancake with a wide spatula and flip it. Fry the other side for about 1 minute. Check that a cut in the centre shows no runny egg and both sides are set; if not cooked, keep cooking over low heat. Take it out of the hot pan, drain any excess oil from the surface, cut into pieces and serve.

## Key points

- **It cracks when flipped and the chopped leaves fall off**: the protein network has not joined into a layer firm enough (see [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Wait until the edge and underside have set before flipping, and fry small pancakes if the pan is small.
- **Burnt outside and still wet in the centre**: the pancake was too thick or the heat too high, so the surface coloured before the heat reached the centre (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Spread it thinner, lower the heat, and go by there being no raw egg.
- **White lumps of starch and sticky patches**: the starch was not dispersed, or settled to the bottom when poured (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Stir the starch in first and mix the batter once more before it goes in the pan.
