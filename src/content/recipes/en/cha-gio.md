*Chả giò* are Vietnamese fried spring rolls. Rice paper wraps pork, shrimp, vegetables and glass noodles, and after frying the wrapper has tiny bubbles, bites crisp and leaves the inside moist. The filling is pork and shrimp with taro, and the glass noodles keep absorbing the juices from the filling as it fries (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)).

Rice paper has to take up water before it can be folded, but too much water makes the wrapper soft and sticky, and steam from the filling is more likely to burst the seal. Success comes down to controlling moisture and [oil temperature](/principles/oil-temperature/): drain the filling, moisten the rice paper only until it can be rolled, and fry the rolls in batches.

## Steps

1. Soak the wood ears in 500 ml warm water for 20–30 minutes, until fully opened with no hard centre. Snip off the tough stems, drain and slice into thin shreds. Soak the glass noodles in 400 ml cold water for 10 minutes, until they bend but are still springy, then drain and snip into pieces about 2 cm long.
2. Mix the filling. Combine the pork, shrimp, shallot, garlic, salt, pepper, sugar and the optional bouillon powder, then mix in the taro, carrot, wood ears, glass noodles and egg. Cover and refrigerate for 15 minutes, until the filling holds together and there is no obvious liquid at the bottom of the bowl, then divide into 20 portions.
3. Soften the rice paper. Wet the cloth with 100 ml water and wring it until it does not drip, then wipe both sides of each sheet of rice paper and let it sit for about 30–60 seconds, until it folds without cracking and has no water droplets on the surface. Work on one sheet at a time so the soft wrappers do not pile up and stick.
4. Roll the spring rolls. Place one portion of filling near the bottom of a wrapper and shape it into a log about 9 cm long. Roll up the bottom edge first, fold in the two sides, then roll until the seal sticks. Roll snugly but leave room for the filling to expand as it heats. Keep the finished rolls apart so they do not stick together.
5. Pour the oil into the deep pot and heat over medium heat for about 8–10 minutes, until a thermometer reads about 165°C (325°F) and the oil is not smoking. Fry 4–5 rolls per batch at about 160–165°C (320–325°F) for 8–10 minutes, turning gently partway through, until the wrapper is fully opaque with light golden spots and the centre of the filling reaches 74°C (165°F).
6. Lift the rolls onto a wire rack and spread them out to drain for 2 minutes, until the surface stops dripping and the steam eases. Before the next batch goes in, take about 1–2 minutes to let the oil return to 165°C (325°F). Serve while crisp, with [Nước Chấm](/recipes/nuoc-cham/) on the side.

## Key points

- **Rolls burst and the filling leaks out**: the wrapper or filling holds too much water, and the steam cannot escape in time (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Pat the shrimp and vegetables dry, soak the glass noodles only until they can be snipped, and soften the rice paper with a damp cloth.
- **Wrapper is soft and greasy**: too many rolls went in at once, the oil temperature dropped, and the wrapper dried out slowly (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Fry fewer per batch and spread the rolls on a wire rack after they come out so the steam can escape.
- **Wrapper has darkened but the centre is still raw**: the oil is too hot or the log of filling is too thick (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Shape the filling into even logs, and while frying, push a thermometer into the centre from one end of a roll to check.
