*Cacio* is "cheese" in Roman dialect, and *pepe* is pepper. The sauce in this dish is an [emulsion](/principles/emulsification/) formed during tossing from the fat and protein of the Pecorino and the starch and water of the pasta water. When it goes wrong, the cheese seizes in the pan into stringy clumps, with a puddle of water beside them.

## Steps

1. Grate the cheese as finely as you can and put it in a bowl. Put the black pepper in a dry skillet and toast it over low heat until fragrant, then turn off the heat and set aside.
2. (Optional insurance) Put the starch and 50 ml of cold water in a small pot and stir while heating until it thickens and turns translucent, which means it has gelatinized. Take it off the heat and let it cool to warm, then stir it into the cheese.
3. Cook the pasta in **less** water than usual (about 1–1.5 L for 200 g of pasta), so the starch in the pasta water is more concentrated. Use little salt. Cook it 2 minutes less than the time on the package.
4. While the pasta cooks, scoop out about 150 ml of pasta water and let it cool a little to one side. Add small amounts of the warm pasta water to the cheese in several additions, and stir with a fork or spatula into a smooth paste. The water must **not be hot**.
5. Add a ladle of pasta water to the pepper pan, lift the pasta straight into the pan with tongs, and toss over medium heat for 1–2 minutes, so the pasta absorbs the water and only a little thick liquid is left in the pan.
6. **Turn off the heat and wait 30 seconds to 1 minute** so the pan and pasta cool a little. Pour in the cheese paste and toss and shake the pan quickly (*mantecatura*), adding a little pasta water if needed to adjust the thickness, until the sauce coats every strand like cream.
7. Plate right away and sprinkle on a little more pepper and cheese.

## Key points

- **Clumping, stringy cheese**: it is too hot. The proteins in the cheese tighten with heat, stick together and squeeze out the fat. The 2025 physics paper in the sources calls this state the "mozzarella phase": without starch it appears at about 65°C. So always take the pan off the heat and let it cool a little before adding the cheese.
- **Starch is the stabilizer**: starch wraps around the cheese proteins and stops them sticking together. The same paper found that with starch below 1% of the cheese weight, the whole sauce seized into large clumps; at 2–3% it was both stable and good in the mouth. The starch concentration of home pasta water is hard to control, so the "insurance" in step 2 is worth trying. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **Too dry, too thick**: not enough pasta water, or it was reduced too far. Add a small spoonful of pasta water at a time, stirring as you add.
- **Can you swap in Parmigiano**: you can, but it is no longer this dish. Pecorino Romano is a sheep's-milk cheese, saltier and sharper, and the flavour of the dish depends on it.
