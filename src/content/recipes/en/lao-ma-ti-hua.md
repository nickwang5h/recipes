Lao Ma pork trotter soup is a pot of mildly seasoned trotter and white bean soup, eaten with a sour and spicy dipping sauce. The soft skin and tendon of the trotters come from [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/). The soup turns milky white because a rolling boil breaks the fat into tiny droplets scattered through the liquid; see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/).

A pressure cooker shortens the time the trotters need to soften. A little bai zhi and optional dong quai add fragrance; leave both out for a plain, meaty flavour. The beans are soaked through and pre-cooked on their own, then pressure-cooked soft in the soup, so the trotters and the beans each cook to tenderness.

## Steps

1. Soak the white beans overnight in plenty of cold water in the fridge. Pour off the soaking water, cover with fresh water and bring to a full boil. It has to be a hard boil; a low, slow simmer isn't enough. Keep it boiling for at least 30 minutes, topping up the water as it drops. Lift out and set aside.
2. Clean any stray hair and bone chips off the trotters. Start them in cold water with the green onion sections, half the ginger slices and the Shaoxing wine. Bring to a boil, skim the foam, and blanch for about 10 minutes, until the foam clearly lessens. Lift out and rinse off any clinging bits.
3. Put the trotters, the remaining ginger slices, 4 g white pepper and the optional bai zhi and dong quai in the pressure cooker, then add the soup water. Check the manual for the minimum liquid and fill limit. Lock the lid and pressure-cook for 30 minutes once it reaches working pressure.
4. Release the pressure fully as the manual directs, then open the lid and add the pre-cooked white beans. The fill limit is usually lower once beans are in, so work it out from your cooker's manual and use two pots if it would go over. Bring back to pressure and cook for about 10 minutes more once at working pressure.
5. Release the pressure fully again and check. The skin and tendon should be easy to pierce, the meat should pull off the bone, and the beans should be floury to the centre with no hard core; if they are still hard, keep cooking. Once they are soft, move to a regular soup pot. If the broth is still clear, boil it uncovered until it turns milky white, then add salt, the optional bouillon powder and half the chopped green onion.
6. Mix the remaining chopped green onion, garlic, chilies, 1 g white pepper, light soy sauce, black vinegar and the optional Sichuan pepper oil and chili oil, and spoon in 30 ml of the trotter broth. Serve the soup and the dipping sauce separately, and dip the trotters in the sauce.

## Key points

- **The broth is clear but the meat is already soft**: soft meat does not mean the fat has been broken up (see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)). Take out the fragile trotters, boil the broth on its own until it turns white, then put them back.
- **The broth is white but the skin and tendon are still firm**: the colour comes from fat droplets, while softening the tendon depends on [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/) converting. Give it more time in moist heat, judged by the thickest piece of tendon.
- **The white beans are hard at the centre**: they were not soaked or cooked long enough (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). After pre-cooking, the beans still need cooking until floury at the centre. Check the centre, not the split skin.
- **Lots of gray scum in the soup and a strong meaty smell**: the bits clinging after blanching were not rinsed off (see [Blanching and Removing Off-Odours](/principles/blanching/)). Clean the trotters and the pot, then stew in fresh water, and start with only a little of the spices.
