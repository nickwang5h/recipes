*Ragù napoletano* is made by simmering large pieces of beef and pork slowly. The meat comes out of the pot to serve as the main dish, and the deep red sauce left behind is tossed with ziti or rigatoni. The flavour of this pot comes from onion, tomato paste and meat juices concentrating slowly; the traditional simmering state is called *pippiare*, with the surface bubbling gently.

The connective tissue in beef shoulder and pork ribs needs moist heat to soften slowly (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Take the meat out as soon as it is tender and keep reducing the sauce, so the meat is not cooked to dry shreds for the sake of a thick sauce. Keep the heat gentle and steady, and never let the bottom of the pot go dry (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

## Steps

1. Put the olive oil, lard, the large pieces of meat, pancetta and onion in a heavy-bottomed pot and cook slowly over low heat. Turn the meat often and scrape the bottom, and add the red wine a little at a time, letting the liquid cook off before the next addition.
2. Keep the heat low for about 2 hours, until the onion has almost melted and the meat is browned on the surface. If the bottom of the pot gets dry, add a spoonful of hot water so the onion doesn't burn black; the aroma of the sauce depends on this step.
3. Raise the heat slightly and add the garlic, pepper and passata. Stir and cook for about 15 minutes. Add the tomato paste in several portions, stirring each one in, and keep cooking for about 30 minutes, until the paste and fat are blended and the colour has deepened.
4. Add hot water in stages until it just covers the meat, turn to the lowest heat and cover. Adjust until the surface shows only slow, occasional small bubbles, and simmer for about 2 hours more, turning the meat and stirring along the bottom, and adding hot water as it evaporates.
5. Check the thickest piece of meat. When a fork goes in easily and the fibres separate with a light twist, take the meat out and keep it warm. The centre of the pork must reach at least 71°C (160°F), and the gristle and meat must also be soft before it counts as done.
6. Keep reducing the sauce uncovered over low heat, stirring the bottom often, until it is deep red and glossy and coats the back of a spoon, then taste and add salt. If there is still a lot of liquid, keep reducing.
7. Return the meat to the sauce to heat through, then serve the meat and sauce separately. Sprinkle the cheese on at the table if you like; when tossing pasta with the sauce, use only enough to coat the noodles.

## Key points

- **The meat is cooked but still tough**: the centre heated up faster than the collagen softened (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep a light bubble and simmer until a fork pulls it apart easily.
- **The sauce is thick but the meat is dry and falling apart**: meat that was already soft kept cooking along with the sauce (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Take the meat out before reducing, and don't boil hard over high heat to speed it up.
- **Bitter bottom and black flecks in the sauce**: the onion or tomato paste was dry-cooked too far. The [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) gives brown colour and flavour, but burnt black parts should be avoided; move the unburnt sauce to a clean pot to keep simmering, and don't scrape in the scorched layer.
- **The sauce is thick, bubbles heavily and sticks**: after concentrating, convection weakens and the bottom overheats in spots (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Lower the heat, stir more often, and add a little hot water if needed.
