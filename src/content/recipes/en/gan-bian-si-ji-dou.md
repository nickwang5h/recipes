Dry-fried green beans have wrinkled skin and soft, cooked flesh with a little chew when bitten. Ground pork and chopped ya cai cling to the pieces of bean, adding a meaty note and the savouriness of the pickle. It is a Sichuan dish that is mostly vegetable, with a small amount of ground pork in the classic version.

Dry-frying gives the steam time to leave. Fry the beans through on their own first, keeping the bottom of the pan free of liquid throughout, then stir-fry them together with the other ingredients (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). The pork is fried separately until lightly browned, which leaves the meaty aroma of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). On a home stove, a wide pan and small batches make it easier to see what the beans are doing.

## Steps

1. Wash the green beans, snap off the ends and tough strings, break into pieces and wipe dry with paper towels. Mince the ginger and garlic, cut the dried chili into pieces, put the ya cai in a small bowl and pinch apart any clumps, and have both soy sauces ready.
2. Pour 0.5 tbsp of oil into a wide pan, add the green beans and salt, and dry-fry over medium heat for about 20 minutes, turning often, until most of the skin is wrinkled with light brown spots and a piece bends when lifted. Spread the beans out so they do not pile up in a heap, and keep the pan uncovered so the steam escapes.
3. Split open the thickest piece to check: the flesh should be soft all through with no hard, crisp raw core. If it is still firm, dry-fry over medium-low heat for another 2–3 minutes, until the cut surface is soft through, then take them out.
4. In the same pan add the remaining 0.5 tbsp of oil, and fry the Sichuan peppercorns and dried chili over medium-low heat for 30 seconds, until you can smell the peppercorns and the chili is still red. Add the ground pork, turn to medium heat and press it apart, drizzle in the Shaoxing wine, and fry for about 2 minutes, until the pork is cooked through and has reached 71°C (160°F), broken into fine crumbles with the edges slightly browned.
5. Add the minced ginger, minced garlic, ya cai, light soy sauce and dark soy sauce and stir-fry over medium heat for 1 minute, until the ya cai loosens and the raw smell of the ginger and garlic is gone. The ya cai is already salty, so season with only the amount of soy sauce in the list at first.
6. Return the green beans to the pan and stir-fry over medium heat for 1 minute, until the pork and ya cai cling evenly and there is no loose liquid in the bottom of the pan. Put them on a wide plate and eat hot, picking up the bean pieces together with the pork.

## Key points

- **Skin is wrinkled but the inside is still hard**: the pan was too hot, so the skin dried out before the flesh softened. Lower the heat and dry-fry longer (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)); green beans must be cooked thoroughly, with no raw crisp core left.
- **Beans are wet and soft, with the other ingredients scattered in liquid**: the beans were not wiped dry or the pan was crowded, so the water had no time to evaporate. Use a wide pan, dry-fry the beans in batches, and add no water when combining (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **Pork is not fragrant and the peppercorns taste bitter**: the pork has to be fried apart until it browns at the edges, but the peppercorns must not burn long in a dry pan. Add the pork as soon as the peppercorn aroma comes up, and let the meat bring down the temperature of the pan (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) and [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
