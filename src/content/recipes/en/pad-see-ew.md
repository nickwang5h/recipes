*Phat si-io* is a common Thai stir-fry of wide rice noodles with soy sauce. The classic combination is gai lan, egg and meat, and this version uses chicken. The noodles are chewy and have a little charred edge where they touch the pan. The sweet black soy sauce gives deep colour and sweetness, and the light soy sauce gives saltiness. The width and thickness of fresh rice noodles are the texture, so buy wide sheets and do not break them when separating.

The smoky flavour comes from the moment the noodles sit against the hot pan. Once the water has evaporated, the spots touching the pan turn brown and smell roasted (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/) and [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). The rice starch in the noodles is already [gelatinized](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), so extra cooking, stir-frying or hard pressing makes the noodles break. Cooking in two batches is easier to control than piling two servings into a small pan.

## Steps

1. Separate the fresh noodles gently. If they have hardened in the fridge, warm them a little as the package directs and stop as soon as they come apart. If using dried wide rice noodles, cook as the package directs until pliable with no hard core in the centre, rinse in cold water and drain as dry as you can.
2. Cut off the tough bottom ends of the gai lan, slice the thick stems thinly on the diagonal, and cut the leaves into pieces and keep them separate. Slice the chicken thin. Mix the soy sauces, vinegar and sugar. Divide the main ingredients, the sauce and the oil into two portions and cook one portion at a time.
3. Heat the pan and add one portion of oil. Crack in one egg with half the garlic and scramble lightly until the egg is nearly set. Add half the chicken and gai lan stems and stir-fry for about 2 minutes, until the centre of the chicken reaches **74°C (165°F)** and the stems are glossy but still crisp.
4. Add half the noodles and sauce and fold up from the bottom of the pan so the noodles take on colour evenly. If there is liquid on the bottom, stir-fry for about 1 minute more, until the sauce clings to the noodles. Do not add water and simmer.
5. Spread the noodles out and leave them alone for about 1 minute. When brown spots show on the bottom and they release from the pan, flip them and let the other side touch the pan for about 2 minutes. A roasted smell is enough; do not wait until they blacken.
6. Add half the gai lan leaves and toss for about 2 minutes, until the leaves are glossy and soft, the noodles are pliable, the egg is fully set, and the centre of the whole portion reaches **74°C (165°F)**. Then take it out. Cook the second portion, sprinkle each with white pepper, and serve right away.

## Key points

- **Only the colour of soy sauce and no roasted flavour**: there was water in the pan, or the noodles were piled too thick. Drain the vegetables and noodles, spread them out in batches and let the noodles touch the pan for a while. If you keep tossing, the contact time is too short. See [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **The noodles break and turn into a lump**: dried noodles were cooked too soft, cold stiff fresh noodles were pulled apart by force, or they were pressed again and again while stir-frying. Soften fresh noodles first and then separate them, cook dried noodles only until the hard core is gone, and lift and fold instead of cutting and pressing. See [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **Burnt edges with a bitter taste**: the sugary sauce sat too long on the dry, hot bottom of the pan. Flip as soon as brown spots appear. Sauces high in sugar scorch most easily. See [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).
