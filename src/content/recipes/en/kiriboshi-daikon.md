Simmered dried daikon is soft with some chew, and the liquid is nearly cooked away, leaving a sweet and salty dish with the concentrated flavour of sun-dried daikon. Drying concentrates the flavour of the daikon strips, and the soaking water is sweet, so cooking it together with dashi layers the savouriness (see [umami](/principles/umami/)).

The daikon strips are very thin and do not need long to cook through. An otoshibuta (drop lid) rests right on the food so the liquid rolls back and forth beneath it, and the strips pick up flavour evenly even when they are not covered by liquid (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Add the soy sauce once the liquid has come to a boil. The drier the liquid reduces, the more concentrated the saltiness, so do not add too much and do not reduce it to completely dry (see [salt](/principles/salt/)).

## Steps

1. Rinse the dried daikon strips quickly, then soak them in a large bowl of water for 15–20 minutes, until the strips are soft and have swelled.
2. Measure out 120 ml of the soaking water and keep it. Lift out the daikon strips, squeeze out the extra water by hand, and cut them into lengths of 5–7 cm.
3. Blanch the aburaage in boiling water for 30 seconds to remove the surface oil. Lift it out and cut it into thin strips. Cut the carrot into thin strips.
4. Put 1 tbsp oil in a pot and heat over medium heat. Add the daikon strips and stir-fry for a few minutes, until the surface looks shiny. Add the carrot and aburaage and stir-fry until everything is coated in oil.
5. Pour in the dashi, 120 ml soaking water, sake, mirin, sugar and salt, and cook over high heat until the edge bubbles and the whole pot boils.
6. Add the soy sauce, put on the drop lid (or baking paper with a hole cut in the middle), turn to low and simmer for 15–20 minutes, until the liquid has reduced to just a thin layer on the bottom and the daikon strips are soft but not mushy. If the heat is too low, it can drag on to 30 minutes.
7. Turn off the heat and leave it uncovered to cool until it stops steaming. Eat it hot or cold.

## Key points

- **Strong dried-goods smell from the daikon**: dried daikon smells sharp straight from the bag, and the smell fades after soaking and cooking. Do not cut short the soaking time, and keep the water to use.
- **Too salty and bitter after cooking**: the liquid was reduced too far and the saltiness concentrated (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Stop the heat when a thin layer of liquid is left; the residual heat will reduce it a little more.
- **Daikon strips taste of little and are hard**: they went in the pot without the water squeezed out, which diluted the liquid; or the heat was too low and the liquid under the drop lid did not roll. Squeeze out the water before stir-frying, and keep the heat low but with the edge bubbling constantly.
- **For a lunch box**: reduce the liquid a little drier, let it cool until it stops steaming before packing, then cover and refrigerate. Health Canada advises eating leftovers within 2–3 days. The next day, microwave it until the centre reaches 74°C (165°F), or eat it at room temperature, when it usually tastes more infused.
