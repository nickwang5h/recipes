Oyakodon (*oyakodon*, "parent and child") cooks chicken and egg together and serves them over hot white rice; the "parent and child" are the two main ingredients. The classic topping uses a sweet and savoury dashi to tie the chicken, green onion and egg together, and keeps some of the liquid at the end so it soaks into the rice. Kombu and bonito dashi and soy sauce provide [umami](/principles/umami/), and the egg forms soft curds as it heats; see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).

Two kinds of soy sauce season the dish together. Light soy sauce (usukuchi) is pale in colour but not low in salt. The chicken is cooked first and the egg goes in last, so each can be brought to the right doneness separately. In Japan the egg layer is usually soft and slightly runny; here it is cooked to 74°C (165°F) in the centre, with short heating and prompt serving to keep it moist.

## Steps

1. Divide the hot rice among four deep bowls and fluff it gently. Cut the chicken into even small slices and the green onions into diagonal pieces. Cut through the eggs with chopsticks until the yolks are broken but a few streaks of white remain; do not whip them into foam.
2. Put the dashi, both soy sauces and the sugar in a wide, shallow pan and heat over medium heat, stirring until the sugar has fully dissolved and the surface starts to simmer gently. The pan should be wide enough for the chicken to spread out instead of piling up.
3. Add the chicken and cook over low heat for about 5 minutes, keeping all the slices submerged in the liquid. Insert a thermometer into one of the thicker slices, and add the green onion only at 74°C (165°F); the meat turning white is not enough to go by.
4. Add the green onion and simmer gently for about 1 minute, until it bends soft but is still green. Keep the liquid in the pan at a gentle simmer, cut through the eggs again and pour them evenly over the surface of the pan, without stirring at first.
5. When the egg at the edges has set and starts to puff, push gently once from the edge toward the centre so the still-liquid egg reaches the hot liquid. Turn the heat to low and cook until there is no flowing raw egg and the centre of the egg layer reaches 74°C (165°F), then turn off the heat right away.
6. With a wide spoon, lift the egg layer, chicken and green onion together and slide them over the rice, then divide the cooking liquid among the bowls. The topping should be soft and in sheets, with the liquid soaking the upper layer of rice. Eat as soon as the bowls are filled.

## Key points

- **Egg breaks into small bits and the liquid turns cloudy**: it was stirred constantly after going in, which broke up egg that had not yet set. Let it set first and give it just one gentle push once the edges bubble; see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **Dry egg and tough chicken**: the ingredients stayed in the hot pan after they were cooked through. Confirm the chicken is cooked before adding the egg, and once the egg is done, put it on the rice rather than keeping it warm in the pan.
- **Rice is too salty**: usukuchi was treated as a low-sodium soy sauce, or a salted dashi was used on top of the seasoning. Use an unsalted stock base, do not cut back the rice, and do not reduce the cooking liquid to a thick sauce; see [umami](/principles/umami/).
