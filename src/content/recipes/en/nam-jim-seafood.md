*Nam jim seafood* is a common Thai dipping sauce for seafood: sour first, then garlic and heat, with a little sugar only rounding off the sharp edges of the sour and salty. Fresh garlic, chili and cilantro roots or stems are pounded and mixed with lime juice, fish sauce and palm sugar, and it comes to the table in small dishes for steamed crab, grilled shrimp or grilled squid, with a little dipped each bite.

Pounding breaks open the tissue of the aromatics, and the smells of garlic, chili and cilantro go into the sauce (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). The sauce is entirely raw, and the fresh aroma is kept by cutting and pounding just before serving. The sourness of lime keeps rich seafood feeling fresh (see [Acid: Balance, Softening and Stability](/principles/acid/)). The finished sauce is a pourable, coarse liquid in which you can see chili seeds and small bits of cilantro, and the pieces cling to the food when you dip.

## Steps

1. Wash the chilies, garlic and cilantro stems, drain off surface water, and cut them into small pieces. If you use cilantro roots, brush the dirt out of the crevices and remove the tough parts; squeeze the lime juice fresh and pick out the seeds.
2. Put the chilies, garlic and cilantro stems in a mortar and pound for about 2–3 minutes, until there are no whole pieces of garlic, the cilantro fibres have broken down, and you have a wet paste with fine bits. Grind along the inside of the mortar, and the pieces are less likely to splash out.
3. Add the shaved palm sugar and pound for about 1 minute more, until the sugar is mixed with the aromatics and no hard lumps remain. If the sugar is hard, shave it thinner on a cutting board first, then crush it with the pestle.
4. Add the fish sauce and lime juice and stir with the pestle around the bowl for 1 minute, until the sugar crystals are gone and the sauce flows freely. Scrape the paste off the walls of the bowl and mix it back into the sauce.
5. Dip a small amount with a bite of cooked seafood and taste: the sour should be clear, and the salt should bring out the sweetness of the seafood. Divide into small dishes and use right away. If you make it ahead, keep it in a sealed container in the fridge and stir up the settled bits before serving.

## Key points

- **The sauce is sour, but the garlic is weak**: the garlic and cilantro stems were only cut into large pieces, so the smell did not come out. Pound them to a wet paste before adding liquid, so the same amount spreads evenly through the sauce (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
- **Cloyingly sweet, with the seafood flavour covered**: the sugar overpowered the lime's sourness. Weigh the palm sugar after shaving rather than judging by the size of the block, and taste it with seafood so the sour stays clear (see [Acid: Balance, Softening and Stability](/principles/acid/)).
- **Too salty with the seafood**: the seafood has already been seasoned with salt or fish sauce, and more dipping sauce stacks up the saltiness. For steamed, boiled or simply grilled seafood, dip each bite in a thin layer, and do not use sugar to cover the salt (see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/)).
- **Dark colour and a dull aroma**: pounded cilantro loses its aroma when it sits in the air for a long time. Make it the same day if you can; if made ahead, keep it sealed in the fridge, open it just before eating, and take only the amount to be served each time (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
