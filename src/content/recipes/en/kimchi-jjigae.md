*Kimchi-jjigae* is often translated as kimchi soup, but it is really a stew with little liquid, made to go with white rice. Napa cabbage kimchi, pork and tofu are the usual combination. The kimchi gets its sourness from [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/), and when cooked it melds with the fat of the pork. Fresh kimchi that has not yet soured will not give the same flavour.

The anchovies and kombu give the stock its [umami](/principles/umami/), and the kimchi juice carries the sourness, salt and heat through the whole pot. The kimchi and gochujang already contain salt, which shows more as the stew reduces, so the salt is left to the end. You should be able to scoop up pieces of kimchi and pork, with just enough liquid moving between the ingredients.

## Steps

1. Remove the heads from the large dried anchovies, split open the belly and remove the black guts. Put them in a small pot with the daikon slices, kombu and 950 ml water. Boil uncovered over medium-high heat for about 20 minutes, then over low heat for 5 minutes, and strain out the solids.
2. Measure out 475 ml of the stock. If too much has evaporated, top up with water to this amount; keep the rest of the stock for another use. Cut the napa cabbage kimchi into bite-sized pieces and measure its juice separately. Slice the pork thin.
3. In a wide heavy-bottomed pot, put the kimchi, kimchi juice, pork, onion and the two green onions sliced on the diagonal. Add the sugar, chili flakes and gochujang, drizzle on the sesame oil and pour in the measured stock. Spread the pork slices apart so they do not pile into thick clumps.
4. Cover and bring the whole pot to a boil, then keep it at medium-high heat for about 10 minutes. If the foam lifts the lid, leave a small gap. The liquid should be bubbling between the ingredients, with liquid still on the bottom of the pot.
5. Uncover and stir gently so the gochujang dissolves fully, then lay the tofu on top. Turn to medium heat, cover and cook for 10–15 minutes, until the thick white stems of the kimchi have softened and the pork is cooked through, with the thickest part at 71°C (160°F).
6. Let a small spoonful of broth cool and taste it before deciding whether to add salt. If it is too concentrated, add a little hot water and stir to combine; if it is very thin, leave the lid off for a short time to reduce it. Finally, scatter on the chopped green onion and bring the pot to the table with white rice.

## Key points

- **Only heat, no sour depth**: the kimchi is still young and has not fermented enough lactic acid. Choose refrigerated, aged kimchi that is already sour, and use the juice from the jar as well. Do not leave it at room temperature for a few days to speed things up (see [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/)).
- **Stew so salty it turns bitter**: salt went in early, and then the water evaporated. Use unsalted stock first and add salt only at the end. If it is too salty, add hot water and a little more tofu and cook it together again (see [salt](/principles/salt/)).
- **Stock tastes bitter or fishy**: the anchovy guts were not cleaned out. Remove the black guts before making the stock, and strain out the fish and kombu once it is done (see [umami](/principles/umami/)).
- **Pork slices clump together and the centre is raw**: the slices are uneven in thickness, or they were stacked when they went into the pot. Gently pull them apart with a spoon and cook until the centre reaches 71°C (160°F); a boiling broth does not mean the meat is cooked (see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/)).
