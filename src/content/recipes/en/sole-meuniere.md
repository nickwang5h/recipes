*Sole meunière* is whole sole lightly dusted with flour and cooked in butter: a thin golden crust outside, tender flaking flesh inside, and finally lemon, parsley and brown butter spooned over. Keep only a thin layer of flour, so the surface of the fish browns easily into a golden crust (see [the Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Flour the fish just before it goes in the pan, because wet flour turns gluey if left to stand.

The **brown butter** (*beurre noisette*) at the end gets its nutty aroma from the milk solids in butter browning; take it off the heat when you see tiny brown flecks and smell a nutty aroma. Lemon uses [acid](/principles/acid/) to balance the richness of the butter. Canadian mainstream supermarkets commonly sell sole fillets, while whole Dover sole usually has to be found at a fishmonger. Thinner fillets cook faster, so check them earlier.

## Steps

1. Pat the cleaned sole dry, blotting the water from the belly cavity and along the bones. Sprinkle a little salt and white pepper on both sides, and warm the plates.
2. Spread the flour on a shallow plate and dust both sides of the fish thinly, then pat gently and shake off the excess. It is ready when you can still see the outline of the fish and there are no thick white patches. Put the fish in the pan right after flouring.
3. Heat the butter for frying in a skillet until the foam dies down and the butter has just started to turn golden. Add the fish and fry over medium heat for about 4–5 minutes, until the underside is golden and releases easily from the pan. If two fish don't lie flat in one pan, use two pans and divide the butter equally.
4. Support the fish with a wide spatula and turn it over gently, then lower the heat a little and keep frying for about 4 minutes. Fillets cook faster than whole fish, so check them early. Nudge aside a little flesh at the thickest part: when the flesh along the bone is opaque and separates easily into flakes, move the fish to the warm plates.
5. Put the finishing butter in a clean small pan and heat over medium heat for about 1–2 minutes, swirling gently and watching the bottom of the pan. When you see pale brown flecks and smell a nutty aroma, take it off the heat and set the pan on a trivet, so the residual heat doesn't turn the butter black.
6. Spoon a little lemon juice over the fish, scatter the parsley, then pour over the hot brown butter. The sauce foams gently as it meets the fish and herbs. Serve while the surface is still golden and the sauce is hot.

## Key points

- **Flour layer is gummy and the fish isn't browning**: the surface has too much water, or the fish waited too long after flouring (see [the Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Blot it dry before a thin dusting, shake off the excess and fry at once.
- **Fish breaks into several pieces when turned**: it was forced with a spatula before the thin crust formed, or the spatula didn't support the whole fish. Wait until the fish releases from the pan before turning; fillets in particular should be moved as little as possible. For doneness, see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **Butter turns black and tastes bitter**: the milk solids browned too far. Start a clean pan for the finish, and take it off the heat at the first pale brown flecks; if the butter burns, throw it out and start over, because lemon juice can't cover a burnt bitterness.
- **Fish is dry and firm**: it stayed in the hot pan after it was cooked, and the proteins kept contracting and losing water (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Take it out once the thickest part is opaque and flakes easily, and make the finishing butter separately.
