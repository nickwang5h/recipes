Cut open, nibuta chashu shows rings of fat and lean. The meat is soft, and the saltiness and sweetness of the soy sauce and sugar have soaked all the way in. It is often served with ramen or white rice. The pork belly is rolled, seared, then slowly simmered in soy sauce, sake and sugar to make simmered pork. The searing flavour comes from the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/), and the softening comes from moist heat [turning collagen into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).

Even when the meat is cooked through, the connective tissue can still be tough. Keep simmering gently until a bamboo skewer slides through the fat and lean layers; that is when it is tender. A hot roll falls apart easily. After chilling, the fat solidifies and the gelatin sets, so the slices stay whole. The prep time includes 8 hours of chilling, so it suits making the day before.

## Steps

1. Pat the pork belly dry and roll it into a tight cylinder with the fat and lean layers showing at both ends. Wind food-safe cotton twine around it about every 1 cm and knot the ends. The twine should hug the meat without cutting deep grooves.
2. Put the oil in a frying pan and preheat over medium-high heat for about 2 minutes, until the oil spreads smoothly. Add the roll and turn it as you sear it for 10–15 minutes, until all sides are golden brown. Turn it with tongs and do not stab the meat with a fork.
3. Put the soy sauce, sake, water and sugar in a heavy-bottomed pot that just fits the roll. Add the green onion pieces, ginger slices and pork. Heat over medium heat for about 5–8 minutes, until the liquid boils and the sugar has dissolved, and skim off the foam.
4. Turn the heat to low and cover the meat with a piece of parchment paper, with a hole in the centre, pressed against the meat as a drop lid. Simmer for 2 hours. Turn the roll every 30 minutes so the part above the liquid also touches the liquid. Small bubbles should keep rising at the edge of the pot, and the surface should not boil hard.
5. Slide a bamboo skewer into the thickest part. It should go in fairly easily, and the whole roll should not lift when you pull it out. Insert a thermometer into the centre, which must reach at least 71°C (160°F). If it is still firm, simmer for 15 more minutes and test again. As the liquid reduces, add 60 ml of hot water each time to keep the lower half of the roll submerged.
6. Move the roll and 120 ml of the strained simmering liquid to a shallow container and set it in the prepared bowl of ice water. Cool for 20–30 minutes, until it stops steaming. Cover and refrigerate right away for 8 hours, until the roll is cold inside and out and the fat is white and firm.
7. Snip off the twine and cut into slices about 5 mm thick. For each portion, put 100 g of slices in a small pot with 2 tbsp of the simmering liquid and 2 tbsp of water. Cover and heat over low heat for 3–5 minutes, until the centre reaches 74°C (165°F) and the fat is soft.

## Key points

- **Cooked through but still hard to chew**: the collagen has not softened enough. Keep it at a gentle simmer and judge by the resistance of the skewer (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)).
- **The slices break into pieces**: you removed the twine and sliced while it was hot, or the pork was overcooked and then moved around with tongs repeatedly. Support the whole roll when you move it, and chill it to set before slicing.
- **No browning, and the pan seems to boil the meat**: the surface of the meat is too wet. Pat it dry first, and turn it only after the steam has cleared (see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).
- **The outside is too salty and the liquid is like syrup**: too much evaporated from the open pot, so the salt and sugar concentrated together. Control evaporation with the drop lid and low heat, and add hot water to restore the liquid level. Use only a little of the liquid with the meat (see [salt](/principles/salt/)).
