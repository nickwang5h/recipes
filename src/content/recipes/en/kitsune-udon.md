*Kitsune udon* is udon in broth topped with sweet and salty aburaage. The thin sheets of fried tofu are blanched to remove surface oil, then simmered in dashi, soy sauce, mirin and sugar until they take on the flavour, and they still hold liquid when you pick them up. The broth is light, so the sweetness of the tofu and the savouriness of the dashi each stay distinct.

The broth gets its [umami](/principles/umami/) from the combination of kombu and bonito flakes, and you can make [dashi](/recipes/dashi/) ahead. Usukuchi soy sauce is used to keep the colour of the broth pale, but it is still salty. The simmering liquid for the tofu is thick and sweet, so keep it separate from the noodle broth (see [salt](/principles/salt/)).

## Steps

1. Heat the 1000 ml water for blanching over medium heat for about 6–8 minutes, until it boils. Cut each aburaage crosswise in half and put them in the boiling water for 2 minutes, pressing and turning them, until both sides are soaked and the surface oil has lessened. Lift them out and press gently to remove water.
2. In a small pot, combine the 120 ml dashi for simmering the tofu, the koikuchi soy sauce, 1 tbsp mirin and 1 tbsp sugar. Cook over medium heat for about 2 minutes, until the sugar dissolves and the surface starts to bubble.
3. Add the 4 pieces of aburaage, overlapping them, and press a piece of baking paper with small holes in it onto the surface as a drop lid. Turn to low and simmer for about 30 minutes, turning once halfway, until the tofu is evenly pale brown and juice drips when you pick it up, with a thin layer of simmering liquid still on the bottom of the pot. If the liquid is dropping too fast, lower the heat at once.
4. Turn off the heat and let the tofu sit in its liquid for 15 minutes, until it stops steaming and the tofu stays moist. Lift it out, press gently to remove excess liquid, and set it aside separately, keeping it soft enough that it gives juice when bitten.
5. In another small pot, put 600 ml dashi, the mirin for the broth, the usukuchi soy sauce and 1 tsp sugar. Heat over medium heat for about 3–5 minutes, until it bubbles steadily around the edge and the sugar has dissolved. Taste and add salt. Turn to the lowest heat, cover and keep warm; you have about 3 minutes to cook the noodles and fill the bowls.
6. Boil another pot of water for the noodles over medium-high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until at a full boil; you can start this while the tofu sits. Add the frozen cooked udon straight to the pot and cook for about 1 minute, until the blocks loosen and are hot through to the centre. If the package asks for longer, follow the package time until the noodles reach the same state.
7. Lift out the noodles, shake off the surface water and divide between two bowls, then pour in the hot broth at once. Put 2 pieces of aburaage in each bowl, scatter on half the green onion rings, and eat hot. The tofu simmering liquid stays in the small pot, so the noodle broth stays clear.

## Key points

- **A layer of oil on the broth and a strong tofu taste**: the aburaage was not blanched first, so the frying oil on its surface went into the broth. Blanch for 2 minutes, then press out the water, and cook the broth and tofu separately (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **Broth gets saltier the longer it cooks**: usukuchi soy sauce already contains salt, and the broth was reduced by long cooking, or the tofu simmering liquid was poured in. Keep the broth warm only briefly once seasoned, and add salt just before filling the bowls (see [salt](/principles/salt/)).
- **Noodles cooked but broth weak**: too much noodle cooking water came in with the noodles and diluted the dashi. Lift them out with a strainer and shake off the water well before pouring in hot broth, and keep the kombu and bonito savouriness of the dashi itself (see [umami](/principles/umami/)).
