Lüdagun is a Beijing snack: a glutinous rice wrapper rolled around bean paste and coated in roasted soybean flour. Cut open, it shows a swirl of white rice dough and red bean paste. The soybean flour is fragrant and dry, and it also keeps the sticky, soft dough from sticking, so rolling and handling are easier.

The dough is soft and chewy because glutinous rice starch takes up water and [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) when heated. It must be steamed through before it can be rolled out and filled. Piled thick, the batter can be cooked outside with a white core in the middle. Spread in a shallow layer, the heat is close to the centre (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)), which is more reliable than just turning up the heat.

## Steps

1. Add the glutinous rice flour to 200 ml of water in portions, stirring until even with no dry lumps. Brush a shallow steaming plate with a thin coat of 1 tsp oil, pour in the batter and spread it level, about 2 cm thick. Cover the plate with a heatproof lid, so condensation does not drip in.
2. Put 1500 ml of water in the steamer and heat over medium-high heat for about 8 minutes, until steam comes out steadily. Put the plate in, cover the pot and steam over medium-high heat for 25 minutes, until the dough looks slightly translucent throughout. Open up the centre with a chopstick and check: there should be no white batter or dry floury core.
3. Take the lid off the plate, fold and mix the dough evenly, cover it and leave for about 10 minutes, until warm and no longer too hot to handle. Fold and press with a scraper for about 2 minutes and knead into a smooth, sticky ball.
4. Scatter 20 g of roasted soybean flour on the board, put the dough on it and scatter 10 g more on top. Roll it out through baking paper to a rectangle of about 20 × 30 cm, about 0.5 cm thick. If the edge cracks, pinch it together, and do not add a lot of flour while rolling.
5. Spread the red bean paste evenly over the dough, leaving about 1 cm on each side. Roll up from the long side, tight on the first turn, then press lightly as you roll so the bean paste and dough stick together. Put the roll seam side down and scatter the remaining 10 g of roasted soybean flour over the outside.
6. Cut into 12 pieces with a sharp knife, wiping the blade clean after each cut and cutting straight down to keep the swirl clear. Eat it the same day, with only a thin layer of soybean flour on the soft, chewy rice dough.

## Key points

- **The dough has a white core and tastes floury and dry**: the centre has not fully [gelatinized](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Steam 5 minutes more and take it out when the white core is gone. A thick pile of batter in a deep bowl takes longer to cook through.
- **The dough surface is wet and slippery, and the soybean flour turns pasty**: condensation dripped onto the plate (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Cover the plate, and tilt the lid away so the drops run off the plate when you lift it. If you scatter on too much extra flour, the outer layer turns thick and dry.
- **It cracks as soon as it is rolled, and the layers fall apart when cut**: the dough was too cool, the edges were dry, or the bean paste was spread too thick (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Roll while it is warm, cover it while it waits, spread the bean paste evenly and roll the first turn tight.
