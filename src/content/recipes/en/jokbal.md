*Jokbal* is Korean braised pig's trotter: the skin soft, sticky and glossy, the meat falling off the bone at a touch. It is sliced, dipped in salted shrimp sauce and wrapped in lettuce, and eaten as drinking food with soju or beer.

The skin and sinew of a trotter are almost all collagen, which only turns slowly into gelatin after long cooking in liquid, taking the trotter from tough to tender (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Soaking out the blood first, then blanching and scrubbing, keeps the broth and the skin clean (see [blanching](/principles/blanching/)). The spices, apple and jujubes go into a cheesecloth pouch, so their flavour gets into the broth and they are easy to lift out. At the end the lid comes off and the heat goes up, and the broth is ladled over the trotter again and again; as it reduces a little, the trotter turns darker and shinier.

## Steps

1. Rinse the trotter in cold water a few times, until the water runs clear.
2. Fill a large bowl with cold water, add the trotter and soak it in the fridge for 12 hours, changing the water 3–4 times.
3. Drain, then squeeze the trotter with both hands in a bowl of cold water to push out the remaining blood; change the water and squeeze again until no more blood shows.
4. Pat the trotter dry and shave off any remaining hair with a razor.
5. Bring a large pot of water to a boil, add the trotter and the ginger for blanching, cover and boil over medium-high heat for 20–25 minutes. Dark brown foam will float up and the water will turn greyish.
6. Lift out the trotter and scrub it with a brush in cold water; wash the pot too, and put the trotter back in.
7. Pack the spice pouch ingredients into a cheesecloth or spice bag, tie it tightly, and set it next to the trotter.
8. Add the 2400 ml water, doenjang, mirim, soy sauce, brown sugar, salt and rice syrup.
9. Cover and cook over medium-high heat for 1 hour.
10. Uncover, turn the trotter over, and press the pouch down a few times with a wooden spoon.
11. Turn the heat to medium, cover and cook for another hour.
12. Poke the thickest part with a stainless steel chopstick or a long skewer: if it slides through easily and the meat is almost falling off the bone, it is done. If not, add water and keep cooking.
13. Turn the heat to high and cook uncovered for 10 more minutes, ladling the broth from the bottom of the pot over the trotter all the time, until it is darker and shiny.
14. Set the trotter on a cutting board and let it cool for 10–20 minutes, until you can handle it.
15. Make the dip: stir the salted shrimp, water, garlic, sugar, gochugaru, black pepper and green onion together in a small bowl until the sugar dissolves, then sprinkle with sesame seeds.
16. Slice the meat off the bones, cut it into bite-size pieces, sprinkle with green onion and sesame seeds, and serve with the dip and lettuce.

## Key points

- **The skin is still tough**: it hasn't cooked long enough for the collagen to turn into gelatin (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). If the skewer won't go through, add water and keep cooking; go by the skewer, not the clock.
- **The broth and skin look grey and smell off**: the blood wasn't soaked out, or the trotter and pot weren't scrubbed after blanching, so blood and scum went back into the broth (see [blanching](/principles/blanching/)). Soak for the full time, squeeze out the blood, and scrub after blanching before adding the seasonings.
- **Leftovers**: don't leave the cooked trotter at room temperature for more than 2 hours; refrigerate it and eat within 2–3 days. To eat it hot again, reheat to 74°C (165°F) in the centre.
