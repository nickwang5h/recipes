Sizzling chili oil is red and glossy, fragrant but not bitter, with the aroma of garlic and chili both in the oil. The whole spices are first steeped in the oil over low heat, and the chili flakes only meet hot oil at the end, so their aroma can move into the oil (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Chili flakes are a powder with a large contact area that heats fast, so pour the oil in rounds and stir as you pour, to avoid scorching in spots.

It has fresh garlic, bird's eye chili and sugar, and only 150 ml of oil to 100 g of chili flakes, so it is thick; when you scoop it, stir the oil and chili together. Set the oil temperature at 160–175°C (320–345°F) according to how coarse the flakes are, taking the lower end for fine powder (see [oil temperature](/principles/oil-temperature/)). One batch makes about twenty small servings of seasoning. Because it contains fresh garlic, refrigerate it once made and use it within four days.

## Steps

1. Wipe the garlic cloves and bird's eye chili dry and chop them. Put the chili flakes, toasted sesame seeds, salt, garlic, chili and optional five spice powder in a wide, deep heatproof bowl, and stir in 5 ml of white vinegar to moisten the base slightly. Leave room in the bowl for the oil to foam.
2. Pour 150 ml of refined oil into a small pot and add the star anise, Sichuan peppercorns and bay leaves, plus the optional angelica root, cracked katsumadai seed, green onion and ginger. Heat over low heat. When the spices bubble gently around the edges, hold that heat and steep for about 20 minutes.
3. When the green onion and ginger turn slightly golden and the aroma is clear, fish out all the whole spices and the green onion and ginger, and do not wait for them to blacken. If you did not use green onion or ginger, judge by the spices having released their aroma without burning. Do not leave a whole katsumadai seed at the bottom of the bowl, because it is harsh to bite into.
4. Measure the oil temperature, and take the pot off the heat at 160–175°C (320–345°F), about 160°C (320°F) for fine chili powder. First pour a small amount of oil over the base in the bowl. It will foam and smell fragrant, and the chili will deepen in colour without turning black. If you smell a sharp burnt smell, stop and let the oil cool.
5. Pour in the remaining oil in rounds, stirring as you pour each time so the dry ingredients meet the oil evenly. Pour the next round when the bubbles from the last one have calmed, and scrape up the dry powder at the bottom of the bowl. In the end everything should be wet, with no large patches of dry chili flakes.
6. Once cooled to warm, stir in 30 g of sugar. Divide into clean small containers and refrigerate promptly. Stir with a clean spoon each time you use it, and mix a little into noodles or cold dishes; use it within four days and freeze whatever you cannot finish.

## Key points

- **Chili turns black and tastes bitter**: the oil was poured too hot, or the flakes were too fine and overheated in spots (see [oil temperature](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Test with a small pour first, lower the oil temperature a little if the flakes are fine, and take the pot off the heat first if the oil smokes.
- **Burnt aroma that covers the chili flavour**: the whole spices were fried too dark, or too much five spice powder went in. Steep the whole spices only until their aroma comes out, and powders need only brief heat (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)); the five spice powder can be left out.
- **Dry at the bottom of the bowl and only the top layer of oil when scooped**: there is little oil and a lot of powder, and it was not stirred in time. Stir up from the bottom with each pour of oil, and stir again before using; if you want it thinner, add more oil, but the ratio changes with it.
- **It cannot sit at room temperature**: fresh garlic and fresh chili carry water, and neither hot oil nor a little vinegar makes it keep. Refrigerate once made and use within four days.
