*Yan du xian* puts salted pork, fresh pork and bamboo shoots into one pot to stew slowly. *Yan* means salted pork, *xian* means fresh meat, and *du* means simmering over low heat. The Shanghai home version often adds tofu sheet knots too, so the bean curd skin carries the flavour of the soup. The body of the soup comes from the two kinds of pork and the shoots, and the seasoning is kept very light.

In the first stage the pork stews on its own. The collagen in the skin and connective tissue turns into gelatin, the meat softens and the soup becomes silky (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). The [salt](/principles/salt/) in the salted pork seeps into the soup gradually, so no salt is added in the first half. The shoots and tofu sheet knots go in later, which brings out the [umami](/principles/umami/) and also lets each keep its shape, rather than stewing with the meat until it falls apart.

## Steps

1. Cut the salted pork into pieces about 2.5 cm across, put it in 500 ml of cold water and soak in the fridge for 15 minutes, until the salt crystals on the surface have dissolved. Change to the other 500 ml of cold water and soak in the fridge for another 15 minutes, then lift out. Cut the fresh pork belly into the same size, and keep the two kinds of pork separate.
2. Cut the thin tofu sheets into strips about 2 cm wide and 15 cm long, and tie each loosely into a knot, leaving a little space in the middle. Cut the precooked shoots into rolling-cut pieces about 3 cm across. If using frozen precooked shoots, cut them and add them to the pot without thawing.
3. Pour the 1500 ml of blanching water into a pot and heat over high heat for about 5 minutes, until the water boils. Add the fresh pork and salted pork, and once it returns to a boil, cook for 3 minutes, until the surface of the meat turns white and foam gathers. Lift out the meat, rinse off any foam, and discard the blanching water.
4. Put the meat, ginger and 1650 ml of stewing water in a heavy-bottomed soup pot. Heat over medium-high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until bubbles break continuously at the edge, and skim any new foam. Cover, turn the heat to low and stew for 90 minutes, keeping the surface at a gentle simmer, until a chopstick goes into the pork and the skin starts to soften.
5. Add the bamboo shoots, turn the heat to medium for about 2 minutes, until the soup returns to a gentle simmer, then cover and stew over low heat for 30 minutes. The shoots should be hot through and crisp-tender to the bite, and a chopstick should pass through the pork skin easily. Taste the soup first to see whether the salt from the salted pork is enough.
6. Add the tofu sheet knots, heat over medium heat for about 2 minutes, until the soup bubbles again, then turn the heat to low and stew for 20 minutes, until the tofu sheet knots are soft inside and out and the centre of the knots has no dry, firm bite. Taste and add salt, then serve sprinkled with chopped green onion. When ladling, use the spoon to support the meat from below, so the skin and meat stay whole.

## Key points

- **Soup too salty**: the salt in store-bought salted pork varies a lot, and a long stew concentrates the soup (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Soak the meat first, stew covered, and season only at the end. If it is already too salty, add 250 ml of hot water, simmer gently for 2 minutes, then taste again.
- **Lean meat is done but the skin is still firm**: converting collagen to gelatin takes more time (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Hold off adding the tofu sheet knots, stew the meat another 15 minutes over low heat, then check whether the skin can be pierced easily.
- **Meat falls apart, tofu sheet knots break**: the soup boiled too hard, or the knots were tied too tight and stirred often. Keep a low, gentle simmer, add the tofu sheet late, and lift from the bottom of the pot when ladling to reduce knocking.
