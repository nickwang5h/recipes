Country-style beer duck is stewed until the skin is soft and gives way at a bite, and the sauce is a little thick and smooth. This comes from moist heat and time: collagen slowly turns into gelatin, so the pieces are easy to bite through and the sauce thickens with them (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). The duck is blanched first to clear the scum from the surface (see [Blanching and Removing Off-Odours](/principles/blanching/)); pieces of different size and age soften in different times.

The whole spices are briefly stir-fried in oil, then continue to give off flavour in the beer, and the ginger, garlic and fresh chilies are added next. The garlic sprouts and large green onion are saved for last to keep their fresh aroma (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). The beer is the base of the stewing liquid, and the sauce should be reduced only after the meat is soft: if the liquid dries up first, the duck stays tough even when it is cooked.

## Steps

1. Blanch the duck. Put the duck pieces in a pot of cold water, enough to cover the meat, and add 20 ml of Shaoxing wine, one large green onion and the ginger for blanching. Bring to a boil, skim off the scum, lift out the duck and rinse off the scum with clean water, then drain well. Smash the remaining ginger and the garlic, and cut the vegetables.
2. Stir-fry the duck. Wash the pot, add 60 ml of oil and the Sichuan peppercorns over low heat. When fragrant, add the drained duck, turn to medium heat and stir-fry for about 4 minutes. At about the 2-minute mark add the tsaoko, cassia bark, star anise, bay leaves and dried chilies, and at the 3-minute mark add the remaining ginger, garlic and Thai chilies, and stir-fry until the duck skin tightens and there is less moisture at the bottom.
3. Stew. Slowly pour in 1000 ml of beer, bring to a boil, then turn to medium-low heat so the surface keeps bubbling, and stew for about 30 minutes. At about the 10-minute mark add the salt, light soy sauce and dark soy sauce. When the liquid falls below two thirds of the height of the duck, add hot water, and do not let the bottom of the pot dry-burn.
4. Add the green and red peppers. At about the 20-minute mark, insert a thermometer into the thickest part of the duck, away from the bone; at 74°C (165°F) it is cooked. Then take out a piece and check whether the skin and tendon bite apart easily. If the skin and tendon are nearly soft, add the green and red peppers; if still tough, keep stewing first, saving the last 10 minutes or so for the pepper pieces.
5. Finish. When the duck is soft and the pepper pieces are soft, if there is still a lot of sauce, uncover and reduce slightly. Finally add the garlic sprouts and the remaining large green onion sections and toss for about 1 minute, until the green onion and garlic sprouts just wilt and the aroma comes out, then turn off the heat and serve.

## Key points

- **Duck is cooked but the skin is still tough**: the meat is cooked but the collagen has not softened (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep stewing in the liquid, and reduce the sauce and add the green onion and garlic only after the skin and tendon are soft.
- **Spices turn black and the sauce is bitter**: dry spices were fried in oil too long, or the minced garlic burned in spots (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Move on as soon as you smell the aroma, and the oil need not heat until it smokes; a burnt taste cannot be saved by adding liquid.
- **Oil spatters while stir-frying and the pan is full of water**: the duck was not drained after blanching (see [Blanching and Removing Off-Odours](/principles/blanching/)). Drain it and blot up visible droplets before it goes in the pan, and stir-fry in batches if there is a lot.
