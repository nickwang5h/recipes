Eggplant and pork pancakes are made from a batter of mostly rice flour with some wheat flour, wrapped around eggplant slices and cooked cured pork and fried as small pancakes. The rice flour absorbs water as it heats and sets by [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/); the egg coagulating also helps hold the thin layer of batter together (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).

After the outside has turned golden, the eggplant still needs time to soften. Slicing the eggplant thin and keeping the batter in a thin layer lets heat reach the centre more easily (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). The cured pork must be cooked and then sliced ahead of time; its saltiness has already gone into the batter, so taste the finished pancakes before adding salt. Rice flour here means flour ground from rice; look for "rice flour" on the package when shopping.

## Steps

1. Peel the eggplant and cut it into even thin slices. Slice the cooked cured pork thin too, and cut larger pieces smaller so they lie flat in the pan.
2. In a bowl, mix the rice flour, all-purpose flour and egg, and pour in the water in portions, stirring as you add it. First stir the flour apart, then gradually adjust to a thickness that clings to the eggplant slices and drips slowly when lifted; no dry flour may remain at the bottom of the bowl.
3. Fold the eggplant slices and cooked cured pork into the batter so each piece is coated with a thin layer. Stir again from the bottom of the bowl just before frying, so the rice flour does not sink and make the first and later batches different in thickness.
4. Put the starting oil in a flat-bottomed pan and heat over low heat, turning the pan so the bottom is covered with a thin layer of oil. With a spoon, pick up eggplant slices, pork slices and a little batter and place them in the pan in small portions, not overlapping.
5. Fry until the underside has set and the edges no longer flow, then flip and keep frying until both sides are golden. Do not press on batter that has not set, or the oil and batter will mix into a lump.
6. After both sides have colour, keep frying over low heat for about 3–6 minutes, flipping in between. The eggplant should be soft through and the centre of the pancake should have no wet batter; the parts containing cooked meat should be reheated to 74°C (165°F). When the pan gets dry, add the reserve oil in portions and fry the next batch.
7. Taste a small piece of a fully cooked pancake. Sprinkle on salt if needed, toss gently and plate right away; do not judge the saltiness by tasting raw batter.

## Key points

- **Batter runs into the pan and won't cling to the eggplant**: the water went in too quickly and the batter is too thin. Add the water in portions, stir until even, then judge whether it clings; the batter only sets after heating (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)).
- **Crust is very dark but the eggplant is still hard**: the slices are too thick or the heat too high, so the centre lags behind (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Lower the heat, fry in batches laid flat, and give the heat time to reach the centre.
- **Centre is wet and sticky, with a raw flour taste**: the batter layer is too thick, or you stopped too soon after browning, so it has not gelatinized enough (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Keep frying and flipping over low heat, and cut open the largest one to check.
