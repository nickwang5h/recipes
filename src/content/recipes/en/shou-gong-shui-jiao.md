The wrappers for handmade boiled dumplings are made with cold water. After kneading, the dough is rested so the water keeps distributing and the gluten relaxes, which makes it easy to roll thin without shrinking back (see [gluten](/principles/gluten/)). The ground pork in the filling is first beaten with the salty seasonings until sticky and then mixed with the chives, using the effect of [salt](/principles/salt/) on muscle proteins to keep the egg white and meat juices in the filling.

One batch makes about 20 fairly large pork and Chinese chive dumplings. When filling, it is better to put in a little less filling and leave a clean edge for sealing. Once in the pot, keep the water turning gently so hot water goes around every dumpling; the wrapper and the meat at the centre both need time to cook through.

## Steps

1. Mix the flour with the sesame oil for the dough, add the cold water in four or five portions and stir into shaggy clumps, then knead until no loose flour is left and the sides of the bowl are mostly clean. The dough should be on the firm side but come together, and not spread out on the bottom.
2. Cover the dough and rest it for about 45 minutes. Then knead it until the surface is fairly smooth, and keep any part you are not using covered to stop a skin forming.
3. Mince the green onion and ginger. Add the salt, light soy sauce, oyster sauce and egg white to the ground pork and stir in one direction until thick and able to hold together in a mass. Mix in the green onion and ginger and the sesame oil for the filling, and refrigerate for about 30 minutes.
4. Cut the air-dried chives into short pieces of about 3 mm and mix them into the meat just before wrapping. The filling should hold a pile and not weep a layer of water at the bottom of the bowl.
5. Roll the dough into a rope and divide it into 20 pieces. Lightly flour the board, flatten the pieces one by one and roll them into rounds of about 8–9 cm, a little thicker in the middle and thin at the edge, with no obvious holes showing through when held up.
6. Put about 18–20 g of filling on each wrapper, fold over and pinch the edge tight, pressing lightly to push out the excess air. Keep filling and oil off the seal, and set the finished dumplings apart.
7. Bring a large pot of water to a boil and add the dumplings in batches, nudging them with the back of a spoon to stop sticking. Adjust the heat so the water boils gently; when it comes back to a boil, add 50 ml of cold water along the edge of the pot, up to three times, then continue to cook over low heat for about 60 seconds.
8. When the wrappers are cooked and the edges have no hard raw-dough core, take one and measure the centre of the filling; lift out the whole batch only when it reaches 71°C (160°F), and if not, keep cooking. Serve with vinegar for dipping.

## Key points

- **Dough is sticky and soft and tears when rolled**: too much water in the dough, so the gluten cannot support a thin wrapper (see [gluten](/principles/gluten/)). Add the water in portions, use only a thin layer of dusting flour, and do not rescue it by adding flour again and again.
- **Filling weeps water and the seal slips open**: the chives were too wet or the salt went in too early (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Mix the chives in just before wrapping once they are air-dried, keep the filling refrigerated while waiting, and keep the sealing edge clean.
- **Wrapper tears or filling is not cooked**: a hard boil batters the thin wrapper, and floating does not mean the centre is cooked (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Control how hard the water rolls, and measure the temperature of the filling at the thickest part.
