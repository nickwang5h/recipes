Liangfen is soft, slippery and refreshing: pea starch is cooked into an even paste and cooled to set. Cold water only disperses the starch grains. Heat is what makes them take up water, swell and thicken, and on cooling the starch chains link up again into a gel that holds water (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). With this much water the liangfen comes out soft, so lift the strips with a wide spatula.

Garlic, chili and chopped peanuts are poured over with hot oil to bring out their fragrance, then soy sauce, vinegar and sugar are added (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Mix the vinegar dressing in only after the liangfen has set: acid added while cooking the starch would get in the way of thickening and setting. Once the paste is cooked, leave enough time for it to cool.

## Steps

1. Mix the pea starch with the cold water for the slurry and stir until there are no dry lumps. Brush a thin layer of oil inside the container, so the set liangfen turns out easily.
2. Put the remaining water in a pot, bring it to a boil over high heat, then turn the heat to low. Stir the slurry again and pour it slowly in along the edge of the pot, stirring the water in the pot constantly while you pour.
3. Keep stirring the bottom and corners until the whole pot of paste is even, thick and glossy, with no white raw starch lumps, and bubbling slowly. When it first thickens, do not rush to turn off the heat. Keep stirring and cooking.
4. Pour the hot paste into the oiled container and smooth the top. Use a shallow container so the strong heat can escape, then refrigerate for 2–4 hours, until the centre is cold and the block does not fall apart when gently shaken.
5. Loosen it gently along the edge to turn it out, and cut into strips of even thickness. Lift with a wide spatula. Do not pinch one end and lift it, because soft liangfen will tear.
6. Put the minced garlic, minced bird's eye chilies, chili flakes and chopped peanuts in a heatproof bowl. Heat the oil until it flows clearly, first pour on a little to test, and once the aromatics foam and smell fragrant, pour the rest on in portions and mix. If the oil smokes, take it off the heat to cool first. If the aromatics burn black and bitter, make the dressing again.
7. Add the soy sauce, vinegar, sugar and chicken bouillon powder and stir until the sugar dissolves. Pour the dressing over the liangfen, toss lightly, taste before adding salt, and finish with the cilantro.

## Key points

- **White lumps, a raw starch taste, parts that did not set**: the slurry had settled and was not stirred up, or [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) was not complete. Stir it well before it goes in the pot and cook until the whole pot is even, glossy and bubbling.
- **It breaks as soon as it is cut, or the centre is still warm**: the liangfen has not cooled through and the starch gel is not stable yet (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Keep chilling until the centre is cold, cut thick strips and move them supported from underneath.
- **Chili tastes burnt and bitter, or the dressing is too salty**: oil that is too hot scorches the powder (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Soy sauce and chicken bouillon powder stacked on top of the [salt](/principles/salt/) can also make it too salty. Pour in portions to test, and add salt after mixing.
