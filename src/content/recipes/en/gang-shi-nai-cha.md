Hong Kong milk tea is made from strong black tea and whole evaporated milk. The tea flavour has to come through the milk, and the drink has to be smooth in the mouth. Ceylon black tea is the usual base, and fine and coarse leaves can be mixed: broken leaves give up their strength quickly, while more whole leaves add another layer of tea aroma (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).

"Silk stocking" milk tea uses fine cloth to strain out the tea dust, so the strong base goes down cleaner. The traditional method pulls the tea back and forth between two pots through a tea sock. At home, line a fine sieve with food-safe cotton cloth to strain it, then pour the tea low between two heatproof pots. The evaporated milk waits in the cup for the hot tea, the sugar is sweetened separately, and the tea and milk are combined only at the end.

## Steps

1. Line a fine sieve with a layer of clean food-safe cotton cloth and set it over a heatproof pot. Place two heatproof pots with spouts steadily in the sink, ready to catch the tea. Shake the evaporated milk and measure out 160 ml; measure the black tea in level spoons.
2. Put 700 ml of water in a small pot and heat over medium-high for about 5 minutes, until the surface is at a rolling boil. Add the black tea, stir once so all the dry leaves are wet, turn to low and keep small bubbles rising steadily at the edge of the pot.
3. Simmer uncovered for 15 minutes, until the tea is a deep reddish brown, the leaves have opened out and you can smell strong tea. Some water will evaporate, and the strained tea comes to about 540 ml. Keep the heat at a gentle simmer so the pot does not boil hard and cook dry quickly.
4. Pour the tea with its leaves into the cloth-lined sieve and let it strain for 1 minute, until the steady stream turns to drips. Lift the sieve so the tea dust stays in the cloth. The tea should show no fine sediment.
5. Pour the strained tea back and forth between the two pots, low, 4 times, taking about 1 minute, until the colour is even and a layer of fine bubbles forms on top. Keep the spout close to the receiving pot, pour a thin stream, and keep the receiving pot on the counter the whole time.
6. Put 80 ml of evaporated milk and 10 g of sugar in each of two cups. Divide the hot tea between them and stir with a spoon until no sugar grains remain at the bottom and the whole cup is an even milk tea colour. Drink right away.

## Key points

- **Milky but weak tea**: the tea base is not strong enough, or fresh milk replaced the evaporated milk and changed the ratio of tea to milk. Fix the leaf size and the simmering time first (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)), then judge the base; the tea before the milk goes in should be strong and fragrant.
- **Powdery mouthfeel, sediment at the bottom of the cup**: fine tea dust got through the sieve. Lay the cloth flat and let the tea drain through by itself. A clean tea sock with no tears improves the texture more than pouring from a greater height.
- **Sharp, astringent tea that tightens the tongue**: the leaves are too fine or were cooked too long. Hold a gentle simmer for 15 minutes and strain promptly. Tea left on the heat keeps releasing astringency, and pulling the tea or adding sugar only changes how it feels in the mouth. The wider the pot, the faster it evaporates and the more the strength of the tea shifts (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
