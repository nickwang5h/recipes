Chicken nanban (*chikin nanban*) comes from Miyazaki. Egg-coated chicken is fried until cooked, soaked while hot in sweet vinegar, and served with tartar sauce. The egg coating softens as it takes up the sweet and sour liquid, and when cut you can still see a ring of golden outer layer. The breast must be sliced thin and even, so it cooks quickly and every bite carries the vinegar.

The flour clings to the chicken first and the egg wraps around it, then sets with heat into an outer layer that holds the sauce (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). The sugar in the nanban vinegar softens the sourness, and the fat in the tartar sauce links the sweet and sour together (see [acid](/principles/acid/)). The coating here is meant to be moist. For a lunch box, pack the cold sauce separately and add it after reheating the chicken.

## Steps

1. Put the eggs for the tartar sauce in a small pot, add the boiling water and heat over medium heat for 4–5 minutes, until the water boils. Turn the heat to low and cook for 12 minutes, until the whites and yolks are fully set. Move the eggs to ice-cold water for 5 minutes, until the shells no longer feel hot, then peel and chop them.
2. While the eggs cook, finely chop the onion and soak it in 300 ml of cold water for 5–10 minutes, until the sharp smell fades, then lift it out and squeeze dry. Seed the cucumber and chop it finely. Mix with the chopped egg, onion, mayonnaise, the rice vinegar from the tartar sauce group, salt and pepper. Cover and refrigerate for at least 10 minutes, until the sauce is cold through.
3. Put all the nanban vinegar ingredients in a small pot and cook over medium heat for 2–3 minutes, until the sugar dissolves and gentle bubbles form at the edge. Turn off the heat right away and pour into a shallow dish big enough to lay out the chicken.
4. Slice the chicken breast horizontally and open it out, then divide it into 2 pieces and pound lightly to about 1 cm thick. Season both sides with salt and pepper, dredge lightly in flour and shake off the extra. Beat the egg for coating in another shallow bowl.
5. Pour the oil into a deep pot and heat over medium heat for about 4 minutes, until it reaches 170°C (340°F), flowing easily and not smoking. Dip 1 piece of chicken at a time fully in the egg and slide it into the oil. Fry for 1 minute first to set the coating, then 4 minutes in total, turning partway, until both sides are light golden, and lift onto a rack. Fry the two pieces in turn.
6. Heat the oil for 1–2 minutes, until it reaches 180°C (355°F), and refry the pieces one at a time, 0.5 minutes per side, until the coating is golden. Check that the centre of the thickest part of each piece reaches 74°C (165°F); if not, lower the oil to 170°C (340°F), fry 1 minute more and test again. Lift out with clean tongs, drain, and put straight into the nanban vinegar for 0.5 minutes per side, until the coating is evenly moist.
7. Take out the chicken, cut it into strips about 2 cm wide, plate it and spoon the chilled tartar sauce over. For the lunch-box portion, store the chicken and tartar sauce separately.

## Key points

- **The coating falls off and the pot is full of bits of egg**: the flour layer is too thick, or the chicken was moved right after going into the oil. Shake off extra flour, fry right after dipping in egg, and turn only once the outer layer has set (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **Deep colour outside, raw in the centre**: the slices are uneven, or the oil is too hot. Slice evenly, fry at two oil temperatures, and check the thick part with a thermometer (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)).
- **In a lunch box the sauce turns runny and the chicken soggy**: the onion and cucumber kept too much water, or hot chicken was covered with cold sauce. Squeeze the onion dry and seed the cucumber before packing; drain off loose vinegar from the chicken, spread it thin to let off steam and refrigerate promptly; keep the tartar sauce refrigerated separately and transport it cold. The next day, reheat the chicken until the centre reaches 74°C (165°F), then add the cold sauce, so the egg and breast are not repeatedly heated and toughened (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
