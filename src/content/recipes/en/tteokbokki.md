*Tteokbokki* is the spicy stir-fried rice cake sold on Korean streets. Short sticks of rice cake are cooked in a chili paste broth until chewy, and fish cake and hard-boiled egg are common additions. The red sauce is hot, sweet and has a [fermented](/principles/fermentation/) savouriness, with the [umami](/principles/umami/) of anchovy and kombu underneath, which gives it layers.

Although the name says "stir-fried", this dish relies mainly on simmering and reducing. The rice cakes are already cooked, and after refrigeration their starch retrogrades and hardens; as they soften again in the hot broth, the starch on their surface goes into the liquid, making it thick and glossy (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Leave a little fluidity when taking the pan off the heat, because it keeps thickening after plating.

## Steps

1. Gently separate any rice cakes that are stuck together. Soak chilled, firm rice cakes in cold water for about 10 minutes and drain; thaw frozen ones in the fridge first, then soak. Freshly made soft rice cakes go in as they are.
2. Remove the heads of the dried anchovies, split open the belly and pick out the black innards. Put them in a wide pot with the kombu and 950 ml of water and cook uncovered over medium-high heat for about 15 minutes, so the broth takes on the savouriness of the fish and kelp.
3. Fish out all the anchovies and kombu and measure the base broth to about 590 ml. If there is more, keep reducing it a little; if there is less, add hot water. This way the amount of broth does not vary much with the width of the pot when the rice cakes go in.
4. Stir the gochujang, gochugaru and sugar together first, loosen with a ladle of hot base broth, and pour back into the pot. Stir until no lumps of paste remain, then add the rice cakes and green onion, and the optional fish cake and hard-boiled eggs.
5. Cook over medium heat until bubbles return, pushing the rice cakes gently with a wooden spoon and scraping the bottom of the pot. Keep at a slow boil for about 10–15 minutes, until the rice cakes bend and the centre is soft and springy when bitten open.
6. Take it off the heat when the sauce has reduced until glossy and coats the surface of the rice cakes, and the bottom of the pot is briefly exposed when pushed aside before sauce flows back. If the rice cakes are still firm but the sauce is already very thick, add 1–2 tbsp of hot water at a time and cook until the centres are soft before reducing.
7. Serve hot in shallow bowls so the rice cakes and fish cake are coated with sauce. Without fish cake and hard-boiled eggs, this pot is better as a main for two, with the amounts of rice cake and sauce unchanged.

## Key points

- **A thick coat of sauce outside but still hard inside**: the cold rice cakes have not softened, or the liquid evaporated too fast. Add hot water and keep cooking over low heat, and bite one open to check the centre rather than going by time. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **Sauce turns into gummy clumps and the rice cakes stick to the bottom**: it was reduced too dry, and the starch keeps thickening after the heat is off. Leave a little flowing sauce before taking off the heat, and if it gets sticky, stir in hot water and push it loose. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **Only sweet heat, with a hollow base**: the base broth was skipped, or salted anchovies packed in oil were used by mistake. Use plain large dried anchovies with kombu, and cook the broth after removing the innards; fish cake also adds salt, so taste before adjusting. See [umami](/principles/umami/).
