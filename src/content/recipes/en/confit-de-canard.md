*Confit de canard* is a traditional duck confit from southwest France: salt-cured duck legs are cooked slowly in duck fat, then the skin is crisped just before serving. The cure needs a separate 24 hours in the fridge. In Canada you can buy rendered duck fat in jars or tubs, and the duck legs should be raw, not already cured.

[Salt moves into the meat along the concentration gradient](/principles/salt/) and sets the base seasoning. The duck fat carries heat evenly to the legs, so they are heated gently the whole time. The meat's own moisture helps [collagen turn into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), the connective tissue loosens, and the meat comes away from the bone easily. Tender meat comes from the low-temperature confit and crisp skin from the final pan-frying, so the work is done in two stages.

## Steps

1. Spread half the coarse salt on the bottom of a shallow dish, set the duck legs on it, then cover them with the rest of the salt. Cover the dish and cure in the fridge for 24 hours. The salt will turn damp and clump, and juices will seep into the dish; keep it refrigerated the whole time.
2. Remove the salt from the legs. In a deep sink, rinse off any leftover grains under a thin stream of water, without letting it splash, then wash the sink and any surfaces it touched. Pat the legs completely dry with paper towels, especially in the folds of the skin and around the bone.
3. Melt the duck fat over low heat in a deep, heavy pot just big enough to hold the legs. Add the legs so the meat is fully submerged, with the fat about 2.5 cm above the top piece; add more duck fat if there is not enough. The legs can be packed tightly in a staggered arrangement, but not so tightly that you cannot get them out.
4. Keep the fat at 93–96°C (200–205°F) over low heat and cook for 2–3 hours. Fine bubbles of steam at the edge of the pot are fine; it must not boil, and you should not hear the skin frying. Check the temperature every so often, and make sure the top pieces are still under the fat.
5. Check the thickest thigh. Take the legs out when the centre has reached at least 74°C (165°F), a thin skewer or small knife slides in easily, the meat pulls apart along the fibres and the bone starts to loosen. If the meat is cooked but still tough, keep cooking over low heat until the connective tissue softens. Lift the legs out with a wide spatula and set them on a rack to drain.
6. Place the legs skin side down in a frying pan and fry over medium-low heat until the skin is golden and firm and crisp to the touch, then flip and heat briefly before serving. If you are using refrigerated legs, first warm them in a 175°C (350°F) oven until the centre reaches 74°C (165°F), then crisp the skin.

## Key points

- **Skin shrinks tight and the meat is dry**: the fat was too hot, so the muscle lost water faster than the connective tissue softened. Use a thermometer to keep the heat gentle, and check whether the thick part can be pierced rather than going by cooking time alone. See [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).
- **Outer layer too salty**: the cure ran too long, or the salt grains were not cleared off. Time the cure in the fridge, remove the salt and pat dry before cooking; the fried duck usually needs no more salt. See [salt](/principles/salt/).
- **Fried skin is soft and greasy**: the surface is wet or still carries extra duck fat. Drain first, then give the skin enough contact time with the pan. See [the Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).
- **Wanting to leave it covered in fat at room temperature for a long time**: home confit must be stored like cooked food. Divide it into shallow containers, refrigerate it as soon as it stops steaming, and eat it within 2–3 days or freeze it; a layer of fat over the meat is no substitute for the fridge.
