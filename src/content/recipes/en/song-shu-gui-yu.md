The two fillets of the Mandarin fish stay joined at the tail. After boning, cut them deeply in a crosshatch. The head is fried separately. The flesh is turned outward, the tail curls up, and a sweet and sour sauce goes over the top, so the fish is crisp outside and tender inside.

The cuts increase the surface area of the flesh and let heat reach the centre faster (see [knife work](/principles/knife-work/)). The starch coating first undergoes [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) in the oil, then loses water and turns into a crisp shell. At home you can score skin-on fillets separately and fry them in batches, which skips the joined-tail shape.

## Steps

1. Cut in behind the head and slice off the flesh on both sides, leaving about 2 cm joined at the tail. Snip through the main bone before the tail, remove the middle skeleton, trim off the rib bones and pull out any remaining pin bones. Cut off the head together with the pectoral fins, and trim the cut flat so it can stand.
2. Place the fish flesh side up. Cut lengthwise first, then diagonally across, about 1 cm apart. Each cut should reach the skin without piercing it, forming diamonds. Press the cuts dry with paper towels.
3. Dissolve the sugar in 120 ml hot water and let it cool for 5 minutes, until it no longer steams. Stir in the white vinegar, pineapple juice, thickening cornstarch, the salt and the white pepper for the sauce. Separately, mix the ketchup, tomato paste and 1.5 tbsp water.
4. Heat the oil over medium-high heat for about 8 minutes, to 218°C (425°F). Toss the fish with the salt, pepper, rice wine, sesame oil and egg yolk. Press the dry cornstarch into the cuts, shake off the loose starch and put the fish into the oil right away.
5. Hold both ends of the fish with two long-handled tongs, fan the flesh outward and lower it into the oil. Fry for 15 seconds, until the coating has just set, then let go. Keep the oil at about 175°C (350°F) and fry for 5 more minutes, until the kernels have opened out, the outside is pale golden and the thickest part of the flesh is opaque. Lift out, drain and place on a long plate.
6. Reheat the oil for about 1 minute, until it returns to 175°C (350°F). Fry the head for 4–5 minutes, until the meat in the head is cooked through and the outside is golden, spooning oil over any exposed parts. Lift out, drain and place in front of the fish body.
7. Put the pine nuts in a fine mesh strainer and fry in the hot oil for 30 seconds, until pale golden, then lift out. Bring 500 ml water to a boil over high heat, about 3 minutes, add the peas and blanch for 30 seconds, until hot but still bright green. Drain.
8. Leave only 2.5 tbsp of the frying oil in the pan. Stir-fry the minced onion over medium heat for 30 seconds, until fragrant but not browned. Add the tomato mixture and stir-fry for 30 seconds, until the paste loosens and the colour is even.
9. Stir the sweet and sour mixture again and pour it into the pan. Push and cook for about 1 minute, until the sauce bubbles evenly and thinly coats the back of a spoon while still flowing. Pour it over the fish right away, scatter the peas and pine nuts, and serve.

## Key points

- **The kernels do not open**: the cuts are too shallow or the starch did not get into them. Cut to the skin without piercing it (see [knife work](/principles/knife-work/)), and spread the cuts open to press the starch in.
- **Greasy coating, dry fish**: low oil temperature stretched out the frying (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Fry the head and body separately, and watch that the oil does not drop too far after the fish goes in.
- **The sauce is thick and pasty**: it is too thickened, or it was left to cool before use. Take it off the heat once it has gone through [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) and thinly coats the back of a spoon, and pour it over the fish while hot.
