Clear-soup fish balls are a Hangzhou dish: white, delicate fish balls, as soft as tofu, with an airy lightness. Hangzhou fish balls are made from grass carp with no starch in the paste; the fish holds its shape on its own.

What makes them work is [a fish paste setting into a gel](/principles/meat-paste/). [Salt](/principles/salt/) draws the proteins out of the fish, and a long stir in one direction turns the paste sticky, so it holds the green onion and ginger water and the air beaten into it. Finally, slow heating in water that never boils sets the proteins (see [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). A formal version serves them in a duck broth simmered for three hours with a whole duck and Jinhua ham; without the broth, the poaching water seasoned with salt becomes the soup.

## Steps

1. Cut the head off the grass carp, split the fish in half, remove the large bones, and slice away the dark red meat.
2. With a spoon, scrape the flesh off repeatedly from tail to head; working in this direction leaves the bones behind.
3. Flatten the green onion and ginger separately, put them in the 300 ml cold water, and squeeze and rub them with your hand to make green onion and ginger water.
4. Blend the scraped fish with the green onion and ginger water in a blender to a fine purée.
5. Pour the purée onto a very fine sieve and press it through, sweeping your palm in one direction; throw away the fine bones and membrane left on the sieve.
6. Put the sieved paste in a large bowl, add the 15 g salt, and stir by hand in one direction for about 30 minutes, until the paste starts to set slightly and is clearly thicker. Stir by hand rather than with an electric mixer: the motor warms the paste and changes how firm the fish balls turn out.
7. Add the egg white and keep stirring in the same direction for 10 minutes.
8. Add the baijiu and stir for another 10 minutes, until the paste is sticky, folding air in as you stir.
9. Fill a large pot with cold water. Put a ball of paste where your slightly cupped palm meets your fingers, turn your hand over gently and let the ball slide into the water by itself. It should float with about a third above the surface.
10. When all the fish balls are in, heat the pot over low heat. The water should show only tiny bubbles, about 95°C (205°F), and never boil; turn the fish balls gently with chopsticks, and add cold water if it gets too hot.
11. Hold that temperature for 3 minutes, then turn off the heat. Lift out the largest ball and cut it open: the centre should be white all the way through, not translucent.
12. Leave the fish balls in the hot water, season with a little salt, and ladle them into bowls with the soup.

## Key points

- **The fish balls fall apart in the water and cook into mush**: the paste was not stirred long enough after salting, so the proteins had not dissolved and joined into a paste (see [fish and meat pastes](/principles/meat-paste/)). Stir for the full time, until the paste is clearly thick and sticky, before shaping.
- **The fish balls sink and cook flat**: the paste was not stirred long enough to take in enough air to hold them up. Shape one to test first; once it floats with a third showing, shape the rest.
- **The fish balls are hard or break up**: the water boiled, so they heated too fast and knocked against each other (see [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Keep the water at small bubbles and add cold water as soon as it starts to boil.
