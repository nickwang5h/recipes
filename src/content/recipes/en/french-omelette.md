A classic French omelette is pale yellow and smooth on the outside, with small, soft curds inside. The beaten egg is stirred quickly in the pan and only rolled up when it is close to setting. It is plain, tasting only of egg and its texture.

**Stirring decides the size of the curds, and stopping the heat decides the moisture.** Eggs heat through [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/), and constant stirring pulls apart the small curds as they form, so they don't join into large pieces. Heat enters the egg from the bottom of the pan by [conduction](/principles/heat-transfer/). Pushing the set parts aside lets the still-liquid egg reach the pan, and only then does all the egg thicken evenly.

## Steps

1. Beat the eggs. Add the salt and white pepper and beat well with a fork or whisk, until the yolks and whites are fully mixed and no long strands of white hang down when you lift the fork. Put a plate and a soft silicone spatula next to the stove.
2. Heat a small non-stick pan with sloping sides, melt the butter over medium-high heat and swirl it to cover the bottom. The butter should be foaming but still pale. As soon as it starts to brown, move the pan off the heat to cool a little before adding the eggs.
3. Pour the egg into the pan and at once stir quickly in small circles with the soft spatula, shaking the pan back and forth at the same time. Sweep the spatula across the bottom and sides, moving the newly set egg back into the liquid, until fine curds appear and the whole pan looks like thick, wet scrambled eggs.
4. Form the outer skin. Lower the heat, spread the curds evenly and stop stirring, so the bottom joins into a thin layer. The surface should still look wet and glossy, with no stream of runny egg flowing when you tilt the pan. Take the pan off the heat as soon as the bottom layer has formed.
5. Roll the omelette. Loosen the edges with the soft spatula and fold the side nearest the handle toward the middle. Then tilt the pan so the omelette slides to the far side and fold the other side back. Be gentle, so the thin skin can wrap the soft centre.
6. Turn it out onto a plate. Bring the plate close to the edge of the pan and flip the pan so the seam faces down. Tuck the two ends gently into a spindle shape and eat it hot. When cut, the centre should be soft and moist, and the skin should have no brown spots.

## Key points

- **The inside turned into large pieces of scrambled egg**: the stirring was slow, and the newly formed [protein curds](/principles/protein-coagulation/) kept joining into big pieces. Stir quickly as soon as the egg hits the pan, and don't wait for the bottom to set before you begin.
- **Brown skin and a rather dry inside**: the pan was too hot, or the omelette stayed in the pan after rolling. The pan is still hot after you take it off the heat, so the egg keeps cooking (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Roll and take it out as soon as the bottom layer has formed, and next time leave the omelette in the pan a little less.
- **The skin tears when folding, or sticks to the pan**: you started folding before the bottom layer had joined up, or the pan wasn't slick enough. Wait until the edge can be lifted whole by the spatula before you fold. If it tears, gather it gently together; the taste is the same.
- **Raw egg runs out of the centre**: you took it off the heat too early. The centre should be moist, small curds, not liquid. Tilt the pan before rolling, and fold once no runny egg flows.
