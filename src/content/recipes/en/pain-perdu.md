*Pain perdu* revives stale bread by soaking it and frying it in butter, for breakfast or dessert. In the classic method the bread is soaked first in sweet milk and then coated with a thin layer of beaten egg. The outside is golden, the inside soft, and the original pores of the bread remain. Day-old baguette soaks up more than soft fresh sandwich bread, and it is easier to pick up and turn.

The milk goes into the bread, and the egg sets with heat to hold up the moist centre (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). The surface loses water first, then the [Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) gives the fried flavour. Match the heat: by the time the surface has coloured, the centre should be hot through.

## Steps

1. Cut the baguette on the diagonal into large slices about 1.3 cm thick, and take 150 g. The bread should be fairly dry and hold together. If the crust is especially hard, shave off a little.
2. In a shallow dish, stir the milk, 10 g of sugar and the vanilla until the sugar has mostly dissolved. In another shallow dish, beat the eggs with 5 g of sugar until there are no streaks of egg white, but do not whip up a lot of foam.
3. Put the bread in the milk and soak about 30 seconds on each side, watching whether the milk enters the cut surface. When you lift a slice it should be heavier and spring back when pressed lightly in the centre, and you should still be able to hold the whole slice. Soak drier bread a little longer, and do not just wet the outside.
4. Drain the soaked bread slightly, then dip both sides in the egg so the surface is evenly coated. Hold it up for a few seconds and let the extra egg drip off. Do not scrape it with the back of a knife, or you will squeeze out the milk it just absorbed.
5. In a skillet over medium-low heat, melt half the butter until the foam is fine and has not turned deep brown, then add the bread with space between slices. Fry for about 3–4 minutes, until the bottom is golden and the egg at the edges has set, then flip with a wide spatula.
6. Fry the other side until golden. Insert a thermometer from the side into the thickest part of the centre, and plate when it reaches 74°C (165°F). If the centre is heating slowly, lower the heat and keep frying. Do not turn up the heat to rush it and let the outside burn first.
7. Wipe the blackened butter bits from the pan, add the remaining butter and fry the next batch. After taking it out, sprinkle on the remaining 5 g of sugar and eat while hot. When cut, the centre should be soft with no runny egg, and no milk should pool on the plate.

## Key points

- **Burnt outside and cold, hard inside**: the bread is too thick or the pan too hot, so heat only reached the surface. Cut slices of one thickness and keep the heat medium-low. See [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **It falls apart when turned and looks like wet mush**: the bread was too fresh and soft, or soaked too long. Use firm day-old bread and stop when it is full but still holds together. Move it as little as possible before the egg sets. See [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **It turns more bitter as it fries and has black specks on the surface**: the milk solids in the butter and the leftover bits from the last batch burned. Before the next batch, clear the black bits and add fresh butter, and stop at golden. See [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).
