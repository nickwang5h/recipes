Silky scrambled eggs are large soft curds with a moist, glossy surface. Milk is added here: a small amount of liquid separates the proteins, so the network that sets is looser (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Half the oil goes into the beaten eggs and half onto the bottom of the pan; whether the eggs are tender comes down to when you take them off the heat.

The pan heats the egg that touches it first. Push with a spatula from the edge toward the middle to move the set part aside and let the rest of the liquid egg run onto the pan (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Once it is in curds, take it out; residual heat keeps cooking the eggs. For eggs that are tender and also cooked, look for no liquid raw egg that can still flow.

## Steps

1. Crack the eggs into a bowl, add 30 ml milk and 5 ml oil, and beat until the whites and yolks are completely blended. No clear clumps of egg white should remain at the bottom of the bowl, so every scoop of curd has a similar texture.
2. Preheat a frying pan over medium heat for about 30 seconds, add the remaining 5 ml oil and swirl to spread. Heat for about 30 seconds more, and turn to low heat once the oil flows lightly; a thin pan heats quickly, so if the oil starts to smoke, take it off the heat to cool first.
3. Pour in the egg mixture and leave it for about 5 seconds. When a thin layer of set egg appears at the edge, use a spatula to push slowly from the edge toward the centre, letting the liquid egg run onto the bare pan.
4. Keep pushing and folding gently, leaving large soft curds. If one side has set first, turn the pan slightly so the other side meets the hot surface; if the pan is too hot, lift it briefly off the heat before pushing again.
5. When there is no liquid egg inside the curds and the surface is still moist and glossy, turn off the heat and plate right away. Sprinkle with salt and black pepper to taste and fold in lightly; do not leave the eggs in the hot pan to season them.

## Key points

- **Dry and hard, water on the plate**: it was heated too long, and the [protein network](/principles/protein-coagulation/) tightened and squeezed out water. Take the eggs out when no liquid egg flows; adding more milk will not make overcooked eggs tender again.
- **Brown on the bottom, runny on top**: the pan is too hot and the heat is concentrated in one spot (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Turn to low heat before pouring in the eggs, push the set egg aside, and lift the pan off the heat to cool if needed.
- **Curds break into fine grains**: constant fast stirring chops up the [protein network](/principles/protein-coagulation/) as it forms. Switch to slow pushing and folding, and move the eggs only when the thin layer can be lifted by the spatula.
- **Surface is slick but the centre still has liquid egg**: the eggs came out early on gloss alone (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Eggs must be cooked through, with the centre at 74°C (165°F); for young children, pregnant women and older people, never serve them undercooked.
