Teriyaki yellowtail (*buri no teriyaki*) has rich, firm flesh coated in a glossy sweet-salty glaze. The fish fillets are pan-fried until the surface has colour, then the sauce is reduced. Canadian fish shops often label this kind of fish Yellowtail or Hamachi; choose cuts that are not salt-cured and not pre-seasoned.

The thin coating of flour on the surface sets with the heat and helps the sauce cling. Frying the fish and reducing the sauce are done separately: the fish is nearly cooked through first, and only at the end is it basted briefly to glaze. If you simmer it in the sauce from the start, by the time the sauce thickens the fish has often tightened and dried out (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). The salt in the soy sauce also concentrates as the water cooks off (see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/)).

## Steps

1. Sprinkle salt evenly over both sides of the fillets and refrigerate for 10 minutes, until small beads of water appear on the surface. Press the fillets and their edges dry with paper towels, keeping the shape whole.
2. Stir the sake, mirin, soy sauce and sugar together. Dredge each side of the fillets in a thin coat of flour, shake off the excess, and leave no lumps of flour at the corners; have a clean plate ready for the cooked fish.
3. Add the oil to a frying pan and heat over medium heat for about 1 minute, until the oil flows faster but does not smoke. If the fillet has skin, hold it with tongs and fry skin-side down for 15 seconds, until the skin tightens slightly. Then lay down the side you want facing up and fry for 2–3 minutes, until the surface is golden and it lifts off the pan fairly smoothly.
4. Turn it over once, gently, cover the pan, turn to medium-low heat and cook for 1–2 minutes, until most of the flesh at the sides has turned opaque. Uncover and use tongs holding a paper towel to blot the leftover oil from the pan, keeping the paper away from the flame.
5. Pour the teriyaki sauce in along the edge of the pan, and cook over medium heat for 2–3 minutes, tilting the pan gently and spooning the sauce over the fish. When the bubbles get denser and the sauce coats the fish like thin syrup, and the thickest part is opaque and flakes easily, turn off the heat; if the centre is still translucent, turn to low and cook 1 minute longer.
6. Support the fillets with a wide spatula and move them to a plate, then spoon over the glossy sauce from the pan. Once the fish is in the sauce, do not turn it again; basting colours the top, and the fish is less likely to break.

## Key points

- **The fillet sticks to the pan and breaks when turned**: it was moved before the underside had set, or the thin flour coat was uneven. Wait for the underside to turn golden and lift it with a wide spatula, and in the second half only baste with the sauce (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **The sauce is bitter and the edge of the pan turns black**: the sugar and soy sauce overheated in patches as the liquid ran low. Stop once the sauce coats the fish, rather than pushing it to a thick paste in the pan (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **In a lunch box the fish is dry and hard and the rice is soggy with sauce**: long cooking dries the fish out, and too much sauce runs into the rice (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Before packing, reduce the sauce to a thin coat on the fish, spread the food in the compartments to let the steam out, refrigerate promptly, and eat it the next day; reheat on day two only to 74°C (165°F) in the centre.
