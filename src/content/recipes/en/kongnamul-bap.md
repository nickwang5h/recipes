*Kongnamul bap* cooks soybean sprouts laid on top of uncooked rice in the same pot, often with a little seasoned beef. The sprouts shrink as they soften, and the rice takes up their liquid and bean flavour, then you stir in the green onion, garlic and soy sauce dressing at the table. One heavy-bottomed pot cooks the rice, meat and vegetable, and the dressing is mixed in a small bowl.

The rice is soaked first so water reaches the inside of the grains, and heating then completes [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Soybean sprouts also release water, so you should not add a big extra portion of water the way you would for plain rice. Use sprouts with large yellow bean heads; the bean heads must cook soft, and the stems will be chewier than in a sprout salad.

## Steps

1. In a heavy-bottomed pot with a tight lid, rinse the short-grain rice, pour off the rinse water and drain as thoroughly as you can. Add the measured 240 ml water, cover and soak for 30 minutes, until the grains have taken up water and turned an even milky white. Keep this soaking water.
2. While the rice soaks, rinse the soybean sprouts, pick out the brown beans and floating bean skins, and drain for 3 minutes, until they stop dripping. Mix the ground beef with 1 tsp soy sauce, 1 tsp sesame oil and the black pepper, break it up and refrigerate for about 20–25 minutes. Take it out when the rice has finished soaking; the meat should be evenly moist with no hard clumps.
3. Lay the sprouts loosely over the rice, then break the beef into small crumbs and spread them evenly over the sprouts. Avoid pressing the beef into one patty, so steam can pass through the gaps in the sprouts to the upper layer. Do not add extra water.
4. Cover the pot and heat over medium-high heat for about 10 minutes, until steam keeps rising at the edge and, when you lift the lid, you can see liquid boiling. Use a rice paddle to turn the pot gently from the bottom, mixing the rice, sprouts and beef, then cover again at once.
5. Turn to low, cover and cook for 10 minutes, until the rice is soft with no hard centre, there is no free liquid on the bottom of the pot, and a bean head can be bitten through without a raw bean taste. Part the beef where it is piled thickest; it is done when the centre reaches 71°C (160°F). If it has not reached that, or the bean heads are still hard, cook on low with the lid on for 2–3 more minutes.
6. While the rice steams over low heat, mix the dressing in a small bowl: the soy sauce, sesame oil, chili flakes, toasted sesame seeds, garlic, sugar and green onion. When the rice is done, fluff it gently and divide among bowls. Stir 1 tsp dressing into each bowl first, taste, and add more, keeping the fresh aroma of the green onion and garlic.

## Key points

- **Wet, sticky rice like a thick porridge**: the rinse water was not drained, or the water released by the sprouts was overlooked. Drain the rice before measuring the water, drain the sprouts too, and fluff the cooked rice rather than stirring hard (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)).
- **Scorched on the bottom while the bean heads on top are still hard**: the pot bottom is too thin, the heat too high, or the beef was piled in a big clump. Use a heavy-bottomed pot and spread the beef out. Stir once after it boils, then turn to low, so both the upper and lower layers get heat (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **Weak dressing aroma and the whole pot too salty**: the green onion, garlic and soy sauce dressing was poured into the pot early and cooked a long time. Mix the dressing separately and add it bowl by bowl as you eat, which keeps the green onion and garlic fragrance and lets you control the saltiness (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
