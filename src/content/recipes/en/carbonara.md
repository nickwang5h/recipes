*Carbonara* is a Roman dish, from the same family as [cacio e pepe](/recipes/cacio-e-pepe/): on top of Pecorino and black pepper, it adds guanciale (air-dried cured pork cheek) and egg yolks. The traditional method uses no cream, garlic or onion.

The sauce depends on two things. One is that the yolk proteins unfold and link up with heat, so the sauce goes from thin to thick; see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). The other is that the emulsifiers in the yolks bring together the fat rendered from the guanciale and the pasta water; see [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/). The difficulty is thickening the yolks without heating them enough to turn into scrambled eggs.

## Steps

1. Bring a pot of water to a boil, with little salt. Remove the rind from the guanciale and cut it into strips about 1 cm wide.
2. Put the guanciale in a skillet with no oil and fry over medium heat for about 10 minutes, until the fat is translucent and the edges are golden and crisp. Turn off the heat, lift the meat out, and leave the fat in the pan.
3. Cook the pasta about 1 minute less than the package time, until there is still a small firm core in the centre.
4. While the pasta cooks, stir the egg yolks, Pecorino and plenty of black pepper into a smooth paste.
5. Add a ladle of pasta water to the skillet with the fat. Lift the pasta straight into the pan, turn on the heat and toss for half a minute, so the pasta is coated in fat and water.
6. **Turn off the heat and move the pan away**, and wait until the pan stops bubbling. Pour in the yolk paste and toss quickly right away, using the heat left in the pasta and pan to thicken the yolks. If the sauce is too thick, add a little pasta water, and keep tossing until the sauce is glossy and clings to the pasta like cream.
7. Toss the guanciale back in, plate right away, and sprinkle on a little Pecorino and black pepper.

## Key points

- **Grainy, like scrambled eggs**: the pan was too hot, and the yolk proteins coagulated too far. Always take it off the heat and wait for the bubbling to stop before adding the yolks; a heavy-bottomed pan holds a lot of heat, so you can tip the pasta into a large bowl and toss it there. Set egg bits cannot be rescued. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **Sauce stays thin, like raw egg**: the pasta and pan have cooled and there is not enough heat. Set the bowl over a pot of barely simmering water and heat it for a few seconds while tossing; take it away as soon as the sauce clings to the pasta.
- **A layer of fat at the bottom of the pan**: too little water, or not enough tossing, so the fat was not emulsified. Add a ladle of warm pasta water and toss vigorously. See [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/).
- **Safety of raw egg**: the yolks are heated only by residual heat and are not fully cooked. For pregnant women, older people or children, it is best to use pasteurized eggs.
