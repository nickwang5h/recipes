*Moules marinières* opens fresh mussels by steaming them with dry white wine, shallots, butter and herbs. The broth is the wine plus the mussel liquor, clear and light, and it goes to the table with the shells. The parsley is used twice: the sprigs and leaves cook first to build the base, and chopped leaves finish the dish (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).

The lid keeps the steam in the pot and carries the heat to the shells on top; a little wine boils at the bottom while the mussels keep releasing liquid (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). The pot needs room for turning the mussels, so for eight servings divide them between two pots and split the wine broth ingredients equally. As the mussel meat heats, its [protein coagulates](/principles/protein-coagulation/) and the meat gradually plumps and turns opaque.

## Steps

1. Check the mussels one by one, and discard any with broken shells or that stay open after a light tap. Scrub the shells under cold running water, pull off the beards and drain. Keep the mussels waiting to be cooked in the fridge, and do not leave them soaking in fresh water for long.
2. Put the wine, shallots, butter, parsley sprigs, bay leaf, thyme and pepper in a wide, deep pot. Bring to a boil for 2–3 minutes, until the shallots start to soften and the smell of alcohol fades, with plenty of liquid still left in the bottom of the pot.
3. Add the mussels, cover tightly and use high heat to get plenty of steam going in the pot again. Halfway through, use a long spoon to turn the mussels from the bottom up, cover again quickly, and let the top and bottom layers swap places.
4. Start timing once the steam has returned, and steam for 4–9 minutes depending on size. When most of the mussels have opened and the meat is plump and opaque, turn off the heat; discard any that stay closed.
5. Use a slotted spoon to lift the mussels into deep bowls, and pick out the parsley sprigs and bay leaf. Let the broth in the pot stand for a moment (about 1 minute) so any grit sinks to the bottom, then gently ladle off the clear broth on top and leave the sediment in the pot.
6. Taste the cooked broth before deciding whether to add salt. Pour it over the mussels, sprinkle with the chopped parsley and serve. The mussels are usually salty and savoury enough already, so leave the broth light and clean.

## Key points

- **The bottom layer is overcooked while the top layer stays closed**: the pot was too full, and steam and hot liquid could not reach the top (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Cook in separate pots, cover tightly and turn the mussels halfway through so each layer heats evenly.
- **The meat shrinks into hard little lumps**: continued heating tightens the protein and squeezes out water (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Start checking at 4 minutes, and turn off the heat once the meat is cooked through, without cooking it further.
- **Grit in the broth, and a bitter saltiness**: the shells were not scrubbed clean, or all the sediment was poured into the bowl; reducing the broth for a long time also concentrates the salt. Drain after scrubbing, let the grit settle when serving, and taste the cooked broth before adding salt.
- **Still not opening after long cooking**: following Health Canada's guidance for steaming, discard any that stay closed after cooking. When buying fresh mussels for eating, also remove any with broken shells or that do not close when tapped while raw.
