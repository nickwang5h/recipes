Eggplant with ground pork is about the soft, silky eggplant, and the ground pork is there only to add aroma. The eggplant is first pan-fried until it collapses, then tossed with doubanjiang, ginger, garlic and the pork, so the savoury, mildly spicy sauce can cling to its surface. When you cut slender eggplant into small pieces, keep some skin on each one, so the pieces hold their shape better as you stir.

This dish uses the heat in two stages. First give the eggplant enough time for the centre to soften too (see [Heat Transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Then fry the pork until the steam is gone and the edges turn light gold, which brings out the meaty flavour of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). If the eggplant is still undercooked, no amount of tossing at the end will fix it, and the ginger and garlic are likely to burn.

## Steps

1. Soak the eggplant. Trim off the stem, cut into pieces about 2 cm across, and put them in 1000 ml of water with 1 tbsp of salt. Press them under with a small plate and soak for 10 minutes, until the cut surfaces look wet and the pieces no longer float. Pour off the salted water and drain for 3 minutes, until it no longer drips continuously, then blot the surface dry with paper towels.
2. Season the pork. Mix 5 g of minced ginger and the Shaoxing wine into the ground pork and marinate in the fridge for 10 minutes, until the pork is moist and the wine is evenly spread. Meanwhile slice the ginger and garlic, cut the green onion, and chop the doubanjiang fine. You can do this while the eggplant soaks.
3. Soften the eggplant. Add 3 tbsp of oil to a wide wok and heat over medium for about 1 minute, until the oil flows freely. Spread in the eggplant and stir-fry over medium heat for 6–9 minutes, turning every 1 minute, until the flesh has completely collapsed and is easy to press open with the edge of a spatula. Remove it and leave the leftover oil in the pan.
4. Fry the pork. Using the oil left in the pan, stir-fry the pork over medium heat for 2–3 minutes, breaking it up as you go, until no pink remains, the edges are slightly golden and the centre of the larger pieces reaches 71°C (160°F). Push the pork to the side of the pan and leave an oily space in the middle.
5. Open up the doubanjiang. Turn to low, add the doubanjiang and stir-fry for 1 minute, until the oil turns red and the savoury aroma comes out. Add the sliced ginger, garlic slices and the white part of the green onion, and stir-fry for 0.5 minutes, until the garlic smells fragrant but the slices are still pale.
6. Toss to finish. Return the eggplant, add the light soy sauce and sugar, and toss over medium heat for 1–2 minutes, until the seasoning is even, there is no liquid in the bottom of the pan and the eggplant looks glossy. Drizzle with the toasted sesame oil, scatter the green part of the green onion, toss gently and serve.

## Key points

- **Eggplant has soaked up all the oil but is still firm**: the pieces are too big, or you took them out as soon as the surface changed colour. Cut even pieces (see [Heat Transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)), and fry until the centre presses soft before tossing with the pork.
- **The whole dish tastes bitter**: the doubanjiang and garlic burned in a dry, hot pan. Turn the heat down so the seasonings release their aroma slowly in the oil (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). If you see black specks or smell burning, replace the burnt ingredients.
- **Oil and liquid collect at the bottom of a lunch box**: the eggplant was not drained well, or loose sauce was left in the pan at the end. Toss gently until the sauce clings and only a thin sheen of oil is left before packing (see [Heat Transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Pack in a shallow container and refrigerate promptly, then reheat to 74°C (165°F) at the centre the next day.
