*Demi-glace* is the base of French brown sauces: rich, glossy, spoon-coating and full of meat flavour. First make an espagnole from brown stock, brown roux and aromatic vegetables, then reduce it by half with an equal amount of brown stock, to about 500 ml. The brown stock must be made beforehand.

The sauce's silkiness comes from the [gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/) in the brown stock, and the toasted flour helps the sauce cling to a spoon through [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). The browning and aroma of the roasted bones come from the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). So the stock itself needs both gelatin and meaty flavour; thin boxed stock reduced to a small volume will not give the same texture. The finished sauce can be portioned and frozen, and used for meat sauces.

## Steps

1. Melt the butter over low heat and skim off the foam. Pour off the clear fat from the top, leaving the milky sediment at the bottom. Mix about 30 g of the clarified butter with the flour and stir constantly over low heat, cooking to an even hazelnut brown with a nutty smell. If you see black specks or smell bitterness, start over.
2. Heat 800 ml of the brown stock. Take it off the heat and whisk it into the brown roux in several additions. Bring to a boil, then lower it to a gentle simmer with only the occasional small bubble.
3. In a separate pan over low heat, render the pork belly dice, then use the melted fat to stir-fry the carrot, onion, thyme and bay leaf until the vegetables take on a little brown. Pour off the excess fat and add these ingredients to the sauce pot.
4. Use the white wine to dissolve the brown deposits on the bottom of the vegetable pan, reduce it slightly and pour it into the sauce pot. Simmer gently for about 3 hours, skimming off foam and fat from time to time. If it reduces too fast, add a little hot water so the sauce stays pourable and doesn't turn into a paste.
5. Strain into a clean pot, pressing the vegetables lightly to extract their juices, and add the second 200 ml of stock. Keep simmering gently and skimming for about 3 hours, then strain again. Don't scrape the dried residue from the edge of the pot back into the sauce.
6. Add the last 200 ml of stock and the tomato purée, and simmer gently for about 1 hour, skimming off fat and straining again. Adjust to about 500 ml, with a texture that coats the back of a spoon in a thin layer. This is the espagnole.
7. Combine this 500 ml of espagnole with the separate 500 ml of brown stock and simmer gently, uncovered. Reduce to a total of about 500 ml, until the back of the spoon is left with a glossy thin layer and a line drawn through it does not close up immediately. The thicker it gets, the lower the heat should be; never boil it hard.
8. Pass through a fine sieve and, off the heat, stir in the Madeira if you want it. Use right away, or put it in a shallow container set in ice water and stir to cool it, then refrigerate or portion and freeze. When storing it as a base, leave out the salt and season when you finish a specific sauce.

## Key points

- **Doesn't set when cold, and is watery when hot**: the stock has too little gelatin, or the sauce was not reduced enough. First check how the stock behaves after chilling; make it from meaty bones with joints and connective tissue (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)).
- **Sauce is sticky and pasty**: it was reduced too early in the skimming stage, so the proportion of flour became too high. Add hot water to keep it pourable and finish the skimming and straining before the final reduction (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)).
- **Bitter, with black specks**: the roux or the bottom of the pot burned, and the bitterness intensifies as the sauce reduces. Cook the brown roux slowly, use a heavy-bottomed pot over low heat for the final reduction, and don't scrape the burnt bits back in (see [the Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).
