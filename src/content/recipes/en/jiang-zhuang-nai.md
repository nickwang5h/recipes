Ginger milk curd is a milk dessert from Shawan in Guangdong. The first taste is milk, followed by the heat of ginger. A spoon lifts a soft piece of milk curd with a delicate edge. Fresh ginger juice goes in the bottom of the bowl, hot milk is poured in all at once, and it sets as it stands. It is traditionally eaten warm, and the bottom of the bowl must set too.

The setting comes from a protease in fresh ginger. It acts on the proteins in the milk and turns the liquid into a gel, a kind of [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). Warm milk gives the reaction the right conditions. Boiling the ginger juice damages the enzyme, and if the milk is too cool it does not set firmly. So measure the milk temperature and the amount of juice accurately, and leave the bowl alone after pouring so the newly formed network can join up. Do not stir.

## Steps

1. Wash the mature ginger, scrape off the skin, grate it finely into a pulp, and press out the juice through a fine sieve or clean cheesecloth. Measure out 2 tbsp, which is 30 ml, and divide it between two room-temperature small bowls, 15 ml each. Keep the juice together with the sediment at the bottom, and squeeze it fresh and use it at once.
2. Put the milk and sugar in a small pot and heat over medium-low heat, stirring, for about 3–5 minutes, until the sugar has fully dissolved and the milk is clearly steaming. Take it off the heat when the thermometer reads 70°C (160°F). Keep the probe in the milk and away from the bottom of the pot, and stir gently before measuring so the temperature is even.
3. Stir the first bowl of ginger juice, then immediately pour in 200 ml of the hot milk in one continuous stream from about 10 cm above the rim, until the juice and milk are forced into an even pale yellow liquid. The force of the poured milk does the mixing; do not stir afterwards.
4. Stir the remaining milk gently and measure its temperature again. If it is below 70°C (160°F), return it to low heat for about 15–30 seconds, until it is back at 70°C (160°F). Stir the second bowl of ginger juice and pour the remaining 200 ml of milk in the same way, in one pour.
5. After pouring each bowl, cover it with a small plate and leave it where it is for 10 minutes, until the surface is smooth and the milk has become a soft curd when lightly spooned. During this time, do not move the bowl or touch the surface, and do not put it in the steamer.
6. Lift the lid and test by spooning gently along the edge of the bowl. If the curd can hold a small piece on the spoon and there are no large areas of flowing milk underneath, it is ready to eat warm. If you need to move the bowl, wait until serving, so you do not shake apart the newly set curd.

## Key points

- **Still a bowl of milk after ten minutes**: too little ginger juice, juice that was not fresh or had been heated, or the wrong milk temperature. Next time squeeze the juice fresh and use it at once, use 15 ml of juice per 200 ml of milk, and pour when the milk reads 70°C (160°F). Adding juice to the milk afterwards and stirring usually cannot restore an even gel (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **Lumpy, uneven set**: the juice sediment was not mixed in, the milk was poured in stops and starts, or it was stirred after pouring. Stir the juice first, then pour the milk in one continuous stream; the forming network needs to stand undisturbed (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **The first bowl works and the second is very thin**: the remaining milk cooled while waiting. Make the two bowls one after the other, measure the temperature before each pour, and do not use the first reading for the second (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
