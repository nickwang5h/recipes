Pan-fried pork belly is cut thin, fried until the edges are browned and meaty, and rolled in lettuce, so hot meat meets cool, crisp leaves. The moisture on the surface of the slices cooks off first, and the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) happens where the meat touches the hot pan, which brings out the roasted aroma; the fat that renders out also helps the slices lie evenly against the pan.

The centre of a thin slice heats quickly; see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/). So fry each batch flat and take it out once cooked, which browns better than piling it up and stirring. The lettuce leaves are cool and crisp, and the doubanjiang stir-fried in the leftover fat has a fermented savouriness that suits the meat; see [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/).

## Steps

1. Cut the pork belly into slices 1–1.5 mm thick, as even as you can. If the meat is too soft to cut, freeze it first until the edges are slightly firm; do not freeze it solid and force the knife through, which gives thick chunks and scraps.
2. Put the slices in a bowl with 8 g of soy sauce, 1 g of salt, 1 g of MSG, 10 g of Shaoxing wine, the ginger slices and the smashed garlic cloves. Mix gently so every slice picks up seasoning, and marinate for 10 minutes; do not soak them in water.
3. Separate the lettuce into whole leaves, wash under running water, drain, blot off the water and put them on a clean plate. Use separate tools for washing the lettuce and handling raw meat. Prepare the leaves before frying the pork, to keep them cool and crisp.
4. Take out the slices, remove the ginger and garlic, drain off the marinade and blot the surface. Wipe 5 ml of oil over a flat-bottomed pan and heat over medium heat until the oil spreads easily and the meat sizzles as soon as it touches the pan. There is no need to wait for smoke.
5. Fry the slices in batches, laid flat with a little space between them. Fry the first side until the edges curl and the side touching the pan is golden, then turn and fry the other side. Pick a thicker slice and insert a thin probe from the side into the centre; at 71°C (160°F) it is done. Charred edges are not a sign of doneness. Remove each batch once cooked.
6. Between batches, look at the bottom of the pan; carefully spoon out the fat if there is too much, and clear out any blackened bits. When all are fried, leave a little rendered fat, turn the heat to low and add the doubanjiang. Stir-fry until red oil seeps out and it smells fragrant, then move to a small dish.
7. Spread a thin layer of the stir-fried doubanjiang on a lettuce leaf, then roll up a hot slice of pork in it and eat. Discard the marinade, and put the cooked meat on a clean plate. Keep the meat and dip separate and wrap at the table; do not let the leaves sit trapped under the hot meat.

## Key points

- **Meat turns grey and liquid collects in the pan**: too much marinade, or too much in the pan at once, and the water blocks browning (see [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Drain the slices, fry them flat in batches, and let the surface lose its moisture first.
- **Edges are burnt black while the middle is soft and raw**: the slices are uneven, or the pan is too hot (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Fry thick and thin pieces in separate batches over medium heat until cooked through, and do not wait for the oil to smoke before adding the meat.
- **Dip is both salty and bitter**: the doubanjiang was spread too thick, or the finely chopped paste burnt in hot oil (see [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/)). Stir-fry it over low heat, spread it thinly on the leaves, and start with a little, adding more as needed.
