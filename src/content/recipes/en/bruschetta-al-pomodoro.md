*Bruschetta al pomodoro* is toasted bread rubbed with garlic while still hot, then topped with raw tomato, basil and olive oil. The surface of the bread dries out and turns golden, and the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) gives it a toasted aroma; the centre keeps some chew, so a bite is not all hard crust.

The tomatoes are only diced, and the basil is raw. The rough surface of hot bread grates off a little garlic, which is enough for the garlic flavour. The water in the tomato slowly softens the bread, so the order matters: prepare the toppings first, then rub with garlic and add the toppings once the bread is out of the oven (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

## Steps

1. Wash and dry the tomatoes, halve them lengthwise, scoop out the seeds and the thin juice around them that runs off easily, and cut the flesh into dice of about 1 cm. Set them in a bowl, and drain off any liquid that collects at the bottom.
2. Wash the basil leaves and dry them thoroughly, then tear into small pieces. Lightly smash the garlic cloves until they crack and peel them, keeping the large pieces whole so they are easy to hold for rubbing the bread.
3. Preheat the oven's top broiler setting and lay the bread slices in a single layer on a rack in the upper part of the oven. You can also toast them on a charcoal grill; keep the heat steady and make sure both sides of the bread meet the heat.
4. Toast for about 1–2 minutes, until the upward side is golden and the edge is crisp, then flip and toast the other side for about 1–2 minutes. Thin spots colour first, so keep watching; when black spots grow larger, move the bread farther from the heat. Take the slices out when both sides are golden and the centre still springs back a little when pressed.
5. While the bread is hot, lightly rub a clove of garlic over one side of each slice, so the garlic leaves a thin trace on the rough surface. One clove is enough for two slices in a row.
6. Spoon the diced tomato onto the garlic-rubbed side, sprinkle with the basil, a little salt and black pepper, then drizzle fine threads of olive oil over the top. On each slice, spread the tomato in a loose thin layer and leave the edge of the bread showing.
7. Serve immediately. If you are serving in batches, keep the tomato and bread apart and top each batch just before eating; the bottom should still be crisp when you bite in, with fresh tomato juice and garlic aroma on top.

## Key points

- **The bottom goes soft quickly and the slice bends when picked up**: there is too much thin tomato juice, or the topped bread waited too long. Seed the tomatoes, drain off collected liquid, and assemble only after the bread is toasted (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **Hard to bite through, and the edge tastes bitter**: the bread was cut too thin and toasted too long, so the browning has gone too far. Use thick slices and take them out as soon as both sides are golden (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).
- **Garlic heat covers the tomato and the basil aroma is weak**: the garlic was rubbed too hard, or fresh basil was toasted for too long. Rub the garlic lightly and finish with basil on top of the raw tomato (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
