Hot and sour glass noodles use sweet potato noodles as the base: the hot broth has the sourness of vinegar, the heat of chili oil and the numbing quality of Sichuan pepper, and peanuts and zha cai go on top. They are common street food in Sichuan and Chongqing. The noodles and the seasoning are prepared separately and only meet in the bowl.

Dried noodles have already undergone [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) in production, so soaking and cooking only let them take up water and soften again. To tell if they are done, bite the centre: the outside may be clear while a hard core remains inside. The vinegar stays in the bowl to build the [sourness](/principles/acid/), and the chili oil and Sichuan pepper are also added last, leaving a clear [aroma](/principles/aromatics/).

## Steps

1. Put the dried sweet potato noodles in 1000 ml warm water and soak for 30 minutes, until the noodles bend and no longer snap at once when folded. Lift out and drain; if the package calls for a longer soak, follow the package.
2. Mash the garlic into a paste, chop the zha cai and chop the green onion. Divide the vinegar, light soy sauce, chili oil, ground Sichuan peppercorns and garlic paste evenly between two large bowls and stir; keep the peanuts separate and dry.
3. Heat the water for cooking the noodles over high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until it boils steadily. Add the drained noodles, gently separate them, and cook for 5–8 minutes, until the noodles are clear and, when bitten through, have no white hard core in the centre and are still springy. Thickness varies a lot between brands, so pull out a strand and taste it ahead of time; it is more reliable than going by colour alone.
4. While the noodles cook, heat the chicken stock in a small pot over medium heat for 4–5 minutes, until small bubbles keep rising at the edge and the surface steams. Pour it evenly into the two seasoning bowls, stir and taste the broth; add salt if needed, keeping in mind that salty zha cai goes on later.
5. Lift the noodles out with a slotted spoon, drain for about 30 seconds, until they no longer drip in streams, and divide them into the broth bowls right away. Use chopsticks to lift them from the bottom of the bowl, so the garlic and Sichuan pepper that sank to the bottom also spread through the noodles.
6. Sprinkle on the zha cai, peanuts and green onion, add the cilantro and eat right away. Hold the peanuts back until this moment so they give a crisp crunch when you bite; do not leave the noodles in the hot broth for long, since they keep absorbing water and softening.

## Key points

- **The noodles are soft outside and hard inside**: thick noodles were not soaked through, and water reaches the centre slowly. Soak until they bend, then cook until there is no hard core when you bite one open, and do not rush them out just because the outside is clear. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **The noodles are mushy and the broth turns sticky**: they were cooked too long, or the cooked noodles stayed in the hot broth. Lift them out once springy, have the broth and the bowls ready beforehand, and eat right after filling the bowls. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **The broth is hot but not fragrant**: the chili oil and Sichuan pepper were cooked a long time with the broth, and the volatile aromas have gone. Heat the clear broth first, then pour it into the bowls for seasoning, and keep the peanuts for the end. See [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).
