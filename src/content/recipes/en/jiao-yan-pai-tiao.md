Salt and pepper pork ribs have a crisp crust and tender pork inside. Boneless pork chops are cut into thick strips, dusted with dry starch, and then coated in a batter. The starch in the dust and the batter [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) with heat, and the outer layer keeps releasing water, which is how the crisp crust forms. After the strips go in, the oil temperature recovers quickly and the crust sets easily; see [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/).

The strips need to be close to the same thickness, so one side does not burn while the other is still raw. The pepper salt is used twice: once to season the meat while marinating, and once as it comes out of the oil, which leaves a more direct fragrance. Store-bought pepper salt usually already contains salt, so taste a strip before deciding how much to sprinkle at the end.

## Steps

1. Bruise the green onion and ginger, put them in 100 ml of water and squeeze and press to release the flavour, then strain to get the green onion and ginger water. Bone the pork chops, tenderize lightly with the flat of the knife, then cut against the grain into thick strips of even thickness; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/).
2. Mix the strips with 8 g of the pepper salt, stirring until the surface turns slightly tacky. Add the green onion and ginger water in stages, waiting each time until the meat has absorbed it and no obvious liquid pools at the bottom of the bowl, then marinate in the fridge for 20 minutes. Any water not absorbed stays in the bowl and does not go into the batter.
3. Mix the flour, 20 g of the starch, the salt and the optional custard powder, and beat in the egg. Add the batter water in stages and stir until there is no dry flour, then mix in 10 g of oil. When you lift the chopsticks, the batter should fall in a continuous stream and cling to the strips as a thin coat.
4. Lightly dredge the strips in the remaining 60 g of starch, shake off the loose powder, then coat with the batter. If the batter is too thick, keep adding the reserved water to loosen it, and do not carry a thick lump of batter into the oil.
5. Heat the frying oil to 150–160°C (300–325°F). Add the strips one at a time and fry in batches. Do not move them right after they go in; once the surface has set, separate them with chopsticks. Fry until light golden, then lift out onto a rack to drain. Break open the thickest strip: it is cooked when the centre reaches 71°C (160°F).
6. Wait for the oil to return to 150–160°C (300–325°F), then briefly return the cooked strips to the oil, and lift them out when the crust is golden and feels firm. Take out any strips that are already very dark first, instead of waiting for the whole batch to change colour.
7. While still hot, sprinkle on the remaining pepper salt in stages and toss gently, then spread out on a plate. Do not cover them, and do not pile the hot strips in a thick stack.

## Key points

- **Crust is soft and the strips taste greasy**: too many went in at once and the oil was slow to recover (see [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Fry fewer in each batch, then drain and let the steam escape after lifting out.
- **Batter is thick and gummy with a floury taste**: the coating is too thick and the inside is not fully cooked through (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Dust the starch thinly, and make the batter only thick enough to hold on to the meat.
- **Golden outside, raw inside**: the strips were too thick or uneven in size (see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Colour only tells you about the crust. Measure the centre of the thickest strip with a thermometer, and do the second fry only once it is cooked.
