The broth of stewed squab with mushrooms is clear, bright and sweet-savoury, and the squab is stewed until soft but still in pieces. The squab is first [blanched briefly to remove off-odours](/principles/blanching/) and the surface scum, then simmered in fresh water over low heat, which is what keeps the broth clear. Fresh shiitake and poultry together supply [umami](/principles/umami/), and the corn adds a little sweetness to the broth.

The squab is halved so the broth can cover the meat and the doneness of the breast and leg is easy to check. Keep the surface of the broth barely bubbling, and the meat stays in pieces once it is cooked through. The meat is delicate, and if it cooks too long it loses water and dries out through [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).

## Steps

1. Check the gutted squab and remove any leftover innards, bone fragments and feathers, blot the surface liquid with paper towels, then cut it in half along the body. Cut the corn into short sections, remove the tough stems from the fresh shiitake and cut them into pieces, and slice the ginger thickly. Clean the cutting board that touched raw poultry before handling cooked food.
2. Put the squab in cold water, with enough water to cover the meat, and add 10 g of the ginger and the Shaoxing wine. Bring to a boil over high heat, and when the surface changes colour and the scum rises together, lift the squab out, rinse off whatever clings to it gently with hot water and drain. Discard the blanching water and this ginger.
3. In the stewpot, put the squab, corn, fresh shiitake and the remaining 20 g of ginger, and add 1000 ml of water. The water should cover the halved squab; if the pot is too wide, top up with water so the meat isn't exposed above the broth to dry out.
4. After it comes to a boil, skim off the new scum, turn to low heat, cover, and keep the surface to only small bubbles. Simmer for about 40–60 minutes, checking the water level in between; if it runs low, top up with hot water so the broth doesn't reduce by much.
5. Insert a food thermometer from the side into the centre of the thickest part of the leg and breast, away from the bone; at 74°C (165°F) it is done. The meat at the joint of the leg should pull apart fairly easily; if it hasn't reached that, keep cooking over low heat.
6. Spoon out some broth to taste first, add salt in stages and stir in, then taste a piece of squab with broth. When the shiitake and corn are soft all the way through, the broth tastes savoury and the meat pulls apart, ladle it out. Before dividing into bowls, take the ginger slices out of the pot so nobody bites into a whole slice.

## Key points

- **Cloudy broth with a lot of scum**: after [blanching](/principles/blanching/) the clinging bits were not rinsed off, or the broth was kept at a hard boil during the main simmer. Rinse clean, skim, and keep it at a gentle simmer so particles and fat aren't stirred into the broth.
- **Breast dry while the leg is not yet cooked**: you went by time alone and ignored that the parts heat differently (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). After halving, measure the thickest part, away from the bone.
- **Mushroom flavour strong and covering the savoury taste of the squab**: the form or amount of the shiitake is wrong (see [Umami](/principles/umami/)). The 100 g here is fresh shiitake; use less if you switch to dried.
