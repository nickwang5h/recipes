For Sichuan boiled beef, the beef slices are cooked in a doubanjiang red broth, spooned onto bean sprouts, and finished with hot oil. Slicing the beef thin against the grain leaves fewer long fibres to chew (see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). The egg and starch coating sets with heat and gives a smooth texture (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)).

The red broth provides the salty, savoury base, and the hot oil briefly heats the chilies and Sichuan peppercorns so their aroma passes into the fat (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Even a small amount of oil can do this, as long as it is poured onto the spices.

## Steps

1. Trim the tough membrane off the beef, cut it against the grain into slices of similar thickness, and pat the surface dry with paper towels. Cut the ginger into shreds, slice the garlic, cut the red chili into rings, and chop the cilantro stems and leaves separately. Have everything ready before turning on the heat.
2. Add 15 g of the ginger shreds, the egg, starch, oyster sauce and Shaoxing wine to the beef. Toss lightly until every slice is coated, and marinate in the refrigerator for 15 minutes. There should be no large pools of loose egg at the bottom of the bowl, and the slices should not be kneaded into a lump.
3. Add 15 ml of oil to the wok and stir-fry the doubanjiang, the remaining ginger shreds and the garlic slices over low heat. When the oil turns red and you smell the doubanjiang, pour in 400 ml of water and bring it to a boil, cooking it into an even red broth. Don't fry the doubanjiang until it turns black.
4. In a separate pot, boil water and blanch the washed bean sprouts until the stems are cooked through and have no raw bean taste. Drain and spread them in a wide, deep heatproof bowl. Don't carry over a lot of blanching water, which would dilute the broth.
5. Keep the red broth at a gentle simmer and add the beef slices one by one. Wait for the coating on the surface to set, then nudge them apart gently. Cook until the centre of the thickest slice is cooked through with no raw-meat texture, and lift the beef onto the sprouts at once.
6. Pour the red broth into the bowl, keeping the liquid below the beef and leaving room at the rim. Scatter the cilantro stems, chili flakes, ground Sichuan peppercorns and red chili rings, putting the powders on the surface of the beef, where they can catch the hot oil.
7. Heat the remaining 45 ml of oil in a small pot and take it off the heat when the surface of the oil flows and shows fine ripples but has not yet smoked. Pour a small amount over the spices first. When it bubbles normally and you smell the aroma, pour the rest over in a thin stream, then scatter the cilantro leaves.

## Key points

- **Beef chews like long rope**: the slices were cut with the grain, so the fibres were not shortened (see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Find the grain before cutting, and trim the tough membrane away separately.
- **Slices clump and the broth is full of bits of coating**: too many went in at once and were stirred hard before the coating set (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Add the slices one by one, and nudge them only after they set.
- **Chilies taste bitter and the hot oil smokes heavily**: the oil is too hot or the spices were heated too concentrated (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Test with a small amount of oil, set the bowl firmly, and pour in several additions in a thin stream once the oil is off the heat.
