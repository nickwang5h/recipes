Fresh pork mini wontons are common in Shanghai and the surrounding area. They are known for very thin wrappers and little filling, and in the clear soup the wrapper looks like soft folds. The meat is first stirred with the salty seasonings until sticky, using [salt](/principles/salt/) to help muscle proteins dissolve; when heated the protein coagulates and a little water stays in the filling (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).

The soup is simmered slowly from chicken and pork bones, kept at a gentle simmer so the flavour of the bones and meat goes into the water. The wontons are cooked in a separate pot and then topped with the soup, so the starch released from cooking the wrappers does not cloud the clear soup. These amounts make about 100 wontons, 25 per bowl.

## Steps

1. Put the chicken bones, pork bones, two slices of ginger and 2000 ml of water in a deep pot and heat over medium heat for about 10–15 minutes, until the water boils and the scum gathers on the surface. Skim off the scum with a spoon and then turn to low heat.
2. Half cover the pot and simmer gently for 3–4 hours, until the soup smells clearly of bones and meat and the meat on the bones is loose. The surface should show only small bubbles; do not let the whole pot roll. Strain out the bones, add the salt for the soup, and set aside about 1200 ml of the clear soup.
3. Mix the ground pork with the salt, wine, light soy sauce, white pepper, water, sugar, toasted sesame oil and ginger from the filling and wrapper group, and stir in one direction for 5–8 minutes, until the filling is sticky and holds together on a spoon. Mix the water in in stages so no free liquid is left in the bowl.
4. Put about 1/2 tsp of filling in the centre of each wrapper, fold the wrapper in half and pinch lightly above the filling. For dry wrappers, moisten the seal with a fingertip dipped in the sealing water; let the outer part of the wrapper loosen naturally and do not press the whole wrapper into a thick lump. Keep the wrappers you are not using covered.
5. Put the toasted sesame oil, white pepper and green onion for serving into the four bowls. Heat the clear soup for about 3–5 minutes, until it steams steadily and simmers lightly at the edge of the pot, and keep it on low heat.
6. Boil water in another pot and cook the wontons in two batches, pushing them gently with the back of a spoon. Cook each batch for about 2–3 minutes, until the wrappers are soft and translucent and the wontons float and open out. Check that the centre of the filling in one has reached 71°C (160°F) before lifting out the whole batch. If not cooked, cook for 1 more minute and check again.
7. Drain off the cooking water with a slotted spoon and divide the wontons among the bowls, then pour about 300 ml of hot clear soup over each bowl. Serve while the wrappers are still spread out and the soup is hot.

## Key points

- **Filling is loose and gives off water**: too much water went in at once before the meat was stirred sticky (see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/)). Add cold water in stages, stirring each time until the meat takes it up before adding the next.
- **Wrapper falls apart but the meat is raw**: the filling is too big, so the heating times for wrapper and meat are far apart (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Keep the filling to 1/2 tsp, and still check the centre of the filling after the wontons float.
- **Soup is cloudy and tastes starchy**: the bone soup was boiled too hard, or the wonton cooking water was used as the soup (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Keep the soup at a gentle simmer, cook the wontons in a separate pot, and pour the clear soup over after lifting them out.
