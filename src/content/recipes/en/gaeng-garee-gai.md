*Gaeng garee gai* is a coconut milk yellow curry of bone-in chicken legs, potatoes and onion. The turmeric and dry spices in the yellow curry paste make a warm aroma, the coconut milk rounds off the heat, and the tamarind adds only a touch of sourness. The result should be a thick, spoonable curry to eat with rice, with tender chicken and potatoes that still have whole edges.

First the small amount of coconut milk is cooked thick and the curry paste is fried in it, so the aroma of the spices passes into the fat (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). The oil beads at the edge of the coconut milk are oil and water separating from an [emulsion](/principles/emulsification/). The water added afterward to simmer the chicken leaves time for [collagen to turn into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/). The potatoes go in late, so when the chicken is tender, they are just cooked through.

## Steps

1. Prepare the tamarind: soak it in the 30 ml of hot water from the recipe for 10 minutes, until the pulp can be pressed apart with the back of a spoon. Rub it loose and pass it through a fine strainer, keeping the sour liquid and discarding the seeds and coarse fibres. Wipe the chicken thighs dry, and cut the potatoes and onion as described in the ingredients.
2. Put the 180 ml of coconut milk in a saucepan and heat over medium for about 3 minutes, until the edge bubbles continuously. Add the yellow curry paste, lower the heat to medium-low and stir for 3–5 minutes, until the paste becomes a thick paste with a strong aroma, and oil beads may float at the edge. Coconut milk with more stabilizers may not release oil, so judge by thickness and aroma.
3. Add the remaining 300 ml of coconut milk and stir the paste in. Add the chicken, 1 tbsp fish sauce, 1 tbsp palm sugar and half the tamarind liquid, and 180 ml of the water for simmering the chicken. The chicken should be mostly submerged in the sauce. If it isn't, add the other 60 ml of water.
4. Cook over medium heat for about 3 minutes, until it bubbles again, then turn to low, cover with the lid left slightly open and simmer gently for 30 minutes. Turn the thighs once halfway. The surface should show only scattered small bubbles, the chicken should be completely changed in colour on the outside, and no curry paste should be stuck to the bottom.
5. Add the potatoes and onion and simmer gently for another 15–20 minutes, until a fork pierces the centre of a potato and the chicken can be pulled easily from the bone. Insert a thermometer into the thickest part, away from the bone. At 74°C (165°F) the chicken is done. If the potatoes are soft but the chicken isn't yet, lift out the potatoes first and let the thighs simmer another 5 minutes.
6. Taste the curry sauce, and adjust in small amounts with the remaining fish sauce, 0.5 tbsp sugar and tamarind liquid until it is savoury, slightly sweet and a little sour. Stir over low heat for about 1 minute, until the sugar dissolves fully. The sauce should coat the potatoes and still flow. Serve with hot jasmine rice.

## Key points

- **It is spicy but not fragrant**: the curry paste was diluted by a lot of liquid before it was fried through. Fry the paste into a thick paste with the small amount of coconut milk, and add the rest of the coconut milk once you smell it. See [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).
- **The coconut milk gives no oil, but the paste starts to stick to the bottom**: canned coconut milk may stay emulsified. Once a rich aroma comes up, keep cooking. Don't scorch the paste waiting for oil beads. See [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/).
- **The chicken is tough and the potatoes have fallen apart**: the chicken needs moist heat to soften its connective tissue, but potatoes can't take that long a simmer. Simmer the chicken first and add the potatoes later, and lift them out as soon as they are cooked. See [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).
