Farmhouse pork belly with peppers and eggs stir-fries pork belly, eggs and green peppers into a salty, savoury plate seasoned with doubanjiang. The eggs are cooked first, then the pork, and finally the pepper pieces and egg pieces are combined. The fattier slices render first, and once the surface moisture is gone, the edges undergo the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). The pan-fried flavour becomes the base flavour of the whole dish.

The eggs are cooked only until just set, which leaves room for the return to the pan at the end. Soft egg pieces can coat the doubanjiang and meat juices, but if they stay in the pan with the pork from the start, the protein network tightens and turns hard (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). The pork belly's own fat is enough to bring out the fragrance of the ginger, garlic and doubanjiang, so when you cook the pork, check the bottom of the pan first, and if there is already a thin layer of fat, don't add more.

## Steps

1. Cut the pork and prepare the vegetables. Slice the pork belly into even thin slices, keeping the fattier parts apart from the lean. Cut the green peppers and bird's eye chili into pieces, smash and mince the garlic, and cut the ginger into thin strips. Blot visible drops of water off the pork with paper towels.
2. Cook the eggs. Beat the three eggs. Add 10 ml of oil to the pan, heat it over low heat and pour in the eggs. Break them into large pieces with a spatula and stir for about 1 minute, until there is no runny egg and the surfaces of the pieces are still moist, then move them to a clean bowl.
3. Render the fattier pork. Leave the thin layer of oil from the eggs in the same pan, add the fattier slices, and pan-fry slowly over low heat for about 3–4 minutes, turning occasionally. It is enough when the fat has slowly run out and the edges of the slices are pale golden. If there is already oil at the bottom of the pan, don't add more.
4. Cook the lean pork. Turn the heat to medium, add the lean slices and stir for about 1–2 minutes, until every piece has changed colour and curled at the edges, and the thickest slice shows no raw meat when cut open. Cook the meat first and add the seasonings afterward, so the doubanjiang does not burn on the bottom of the pan before the meat is done.
5. Fry the doubanjiang. Add the ginger, garlic and 10 g of doubanjiang and stir-fry until the paste breaks up, the oil is slightly red and the ginger and garlic smell fragrant. Keep oil on the bottom of the pan and spread the paste out, so that no lump of paste sticks in one place.
6. Combine the peppers and eggs. Add the green peppers, bird's eye chili and the cooked eggs, drizzle in 15 ml of light soy sauce, sprinkle with 5 g of sugar, and turn gently from the bottom so the sauce coats the pork, pepper pieces and egg pieces. Stir-fry for about 1–2 minutes, until the green peppers lose their obvious raw smell, bend slightly but are still crisp, and the egg pieces are hot again.
7. Serve. Turn off the heat and plate. The bottom of the pan should hold a thin, glossy layer of meat juices, and the egg pieces should be mostly whole. The doubanjiang and light soy sauce are already salty, so don't add salt.

## Key points

- **The pork turns pale and water collects in the pan**: the meat is too wet or too much went in at once, and the steam did not clear, so the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) hardly happens. Dry it first, render the fatty slices slowly, and spread the lean slices out to fry.
- **The eggs turn hard and break into small bits**: they went back in too early or were turned too roughly, so the pieces kept setting and shrinking (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Take them out as soon as they are just set the first time, and fold them gently with the green peppers at the end.
- **The doubanjiang is burnt and bitter, and the garlic has turned black**: the minced aromatics are small and release their fragrance quickly in a hot pan. Add the peppers and eggs as soon as you smell the fragrance, and turn the heat down if the pan is too hot (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
