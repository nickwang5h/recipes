*Zabaione* is an Italian dessert whisked from egg yolks, sugar and sweet wine. The wine aroma of Marsala stays among the tiny bubbles, and it is served warm in small glasses. With this much wine, the result is a light, wine-scented foam, one small glass for each of four people; it still contains alcohol.

Whisking first brings air into the liquid, and the proteins in the yolks gather around the bubbles. Heat makes the proteins gradually unfold and link up, and the foam turns thick (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Sugar and wine change the conditions for setting, so go by the state of the foam and its temperature. The water bath slows local heating at the bottom of the bowl, and whisking spreads the heat around (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

## Steps

1. Heat water in a small pot until it gives off gentle steam and only a few small bubbles appear on the surface. Set a heatproof round-bottomed bowl on the pot to test the fit: the bottom of the bowl should hang above the water without touching it. Then take the bowl off.
2. In the bowl, whisk the egg yolks and sugar until paler in colour and the sugar grains have mostly gone, then pour in the Marsala at once and keep whisking until even. Do not let the sugar sit on the unstirred yolks for long.
3. Set the bowl back over the pot and keep sweeping the whisk across the bottom and sides of the bowl, whisking over the water bath for about 10 minutes. The foam goes from big and loose to fine and dense, grows in volume and turns paler. If a thick film forms at the edge of the bowl first, lift the bowl, whisk it in and lower the heat.
4. Measure the temperature of the yolk mixture with a probe, without touching the bottom of the bowl. Keep whisking until the whole mixture reaches at least 74°C (165°F), and the foam falling from the lifted whisk briefly leaves a trail on the surface. The time varies with the shape of the bowl and the heat.
5. Once it has reached the temperature and the right foam, take the bowl off the hot pot at once and whisk a moment longer, then divide among four small glasses and eat while warm. Do not leave it on the pot of hot water waiting for people, because the residual heat in the bowl bottom will keep thickening the yolks.

## Key points

- **Bits of cooked egg and a skin at the edge**: a spot got too hot and the proteins clumped. Keep the bottom of the bowl out of boiling water and keep sweeping the bottom with the whisk; foam that has turned into egg bits cannot be whisked back. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **All big bubbles, and it thins quickly in the glass**: air was whisked in but the yolks have not thickened enough. Keep heating gently and whisking, judge the structure by the fine foam and the trail it leaves as it falls, and take the temperature at the same time. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **The foam looks done but the temperature was not taken**: fluffiness does not prove the yolks reached a safe temperature. Measure to 74°C (165°F), as Canadian guidance asks for egg dishes; put the thermometer in the liquid part and stir gently so the heat is even. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
