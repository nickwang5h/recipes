*Pan di Spagna* is a basic sponge cake used throughout Italian desserts: fine-textured, light and springy, not very sweet and almost free of fat. It is rarely eaten on its own; it is usually split into layers, brushed with syrup or sweet wine, and filled with cream, custard or fruit to make layered cakes.

There is no baking powder in it; all the volume comes from whipped eggs. Whole eggs and sugar are beaten together for a quarter of an hour or more, and the egg proteins unfold at the surface of countless small bubbles and link into thin films that hold the air in the batter (see [foams](/principles/foams/)). In the oven the bubbles expand with heat, while the proteins set and the starch gelatinizes, fixing the risen shape (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) and [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Replacing half the flour with starch gives a softer crumb.

## Steps

1. Heat the oven to 160°C (325°F) using top and bottom heat with no fan. Butter a 24 cm round cake pan thinly, add a little flour and turn the pan so the sides are coated, then tip out the excess. Mix the flour and starch and sift them once.
2. Break the eggs into a clean, grease-free large bowl and add the salt and lemon zest. Start beating at medium-high speed with a stand mixer or electric beaters, adding the sugar in several additions.
3. Keep beating for 15–20 minutes, until the mixture is pale, thick, full of fine bubbles and much larger in volume. When you lift the beaters, the batter that falls back should leave a clear trail on the surface that only slowly disappears.
4. Sift the dry ingredients over the batter in two or three additions, folding each in with a spatula from the bottom up while turning the bowl, and stop as soon as no dry flour shows. Be gentle; the batter will lose a little volume but should still be thick and airy.
5. Pour the batter into the pan at once and turn the pan to level the top; do not tap it on the counter.
6. Bake on the lowest rack for about 50 minutes, without opening the oven door. It is done when the top is golden, springs back when pressed lightly, and a skewer pushed into the centre comes out dry.
7. Turn the pan upside down onto a rack lined with parchment for a few minutes, then unmould and let the cake cool completely on the rack. Wrapped in plastic wrap, it keeps 2 days at room temperature, or 1 month frozen.

## Key points

- **The eggs won't whip, little volume**: the eggs were too cold, or the bowl or beaters were greasy, so the proteins could not form films at the bubble surfaces (see [foams](/principles/foams/)). Bring the eggs to room temperature, wash and dry the bowl, and beat long enough, judging by the trail rather than the clock.
- **Short cake with a dense, wet layer at the bottom**: the flour was folded in too hard, or dumped in all at once and clumped, so bubbles burst and the flour sank (see [foams](/principles/foams/)). Sift it in stages, fold from the bottom up, and stop as soon as no dry flour shows.
- **Sinks in the middle after baking**: it came out underbaked, or the oven was opened partway through, before the set proteins and gelatinized starch had fixed the structure (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Bake the full time and check that a skewer comes out of the centre dry before taking it out.
