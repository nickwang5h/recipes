Di san xian is potato, eggplant and green long pepper in one dish: the potato is cooked through, the eggplant soft and the pepper still a little crisp, with the savoury, spicy flavour of doubanjiang. The three vegetables are fried separately in oil, each stopped at the right doneness (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)).

At the end, a sauce thickened by [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) brings the flavour back onto the vegetables, and the final toss takes very little time. The garlic goes in two parts: half is stir-fried until fragrant first and half is stirred in off the heat, so both aromas are kept (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Eggplant soaks up oil and turns soft and shrinks as it heats, so judge it by how it looks, not by how much it has shrunk.

## Steps

1. Peel the potato, and cut it and the eggplant into roll-cut chunks of about 15 g each, close in size. Remove the stems and seeds from the peppers and tear them into pieces slightly larger than the potato chunks. Dry all three vegetables, including the hollows of the peppers.
2. Cut the green onion into small pieces about 0.5 cm long, mince the ginger, and chop the garlic and divide it into two portions. Stir the light soy sauce, sugar, starch and 80 ml cold water into a sauce, and stir it again just before it goes into the pan.
3. Put 150 ml oil in a smaller skillet and heat over medium heat until a small piece of vegetable dropped in bubbles steadily and the oil is not yet smoking. Add the potato in batches and fry, turning, for about 3–4 minutes, until golden on the surface and a chopstick goes through the centre. Lift out and drain.
4. In the same oil, fry the eggplant in batches for about 2–3 minutes, turning, until lightly coloured on the surface and the pieces have softened. Lift out and drain. When there is no free-flowing oil left in the pan, add 15 ml; if thick pieces are still firm, fry them a little longer, and don't wait for them to "give back oil" before lifting them out.
5. Add the dried pepper pieces and fry for about 15 seconds, lifting them out as soon as the edges just wrinkle. Take the pan off the heat to let the oil cool a little, and pour the remaining oil into a heatproof container. Never add water to hot oil, and never cover the oil with a wet lid.
6. Add 15 ml fresh oil to the pan and stir-fry the green onion, ginger and half the garlic over medium-low heat. When you smell the fragrance, add the doubanjiang, turn the heat to low and stir-fry until the oil turns red. Stir the sauce again and pour it in, turn the heat to high and push it around as it cooks, then check the thickness after about 10 seconds.
7. When the sauce turns glossy and starts to cling to the spatula, add the potato, eggplant and pepper right away and stir-fry for about 20 seconds so every piece is coated. Turn off the heat, add the other half of the garlic, toss gently and plate, so the potato and eggplant don't break apart.

## Key points

- **Browned outside, hard potato centre**: the pieces were too big, or heated unevenly after going in the pan (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Cut them the same size, fry in batches and turn them, and lift them out only when a chopstick goes through the centre; the time is only a guide.
- **Sauce turns into a stiff paste**: there is too much starch and it thickens quickly, and what settles at the bottom of the bowl clumps before it is poured in (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Stir it again before pouring and keep pushing it around as it cooks; if it is too thick, add a little hot water before tossing.
- **Oil spatters and the peppers scorch**: the water on the surface turns to steam quickly in hot oil (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Dry them first, add only a few at a time, lift them out as soon as they wrinkle, and don't use smoking as the sign that the oil is hot.
