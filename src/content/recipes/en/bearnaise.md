*Sauce béarnaise* is a warm butter and egg sauce served with pan-fried or grilled beef, with a distinct aniseed note from tarragon. It uses the same emulsifying method as [hollandaise sauce](/recipes/hollandaise/), but the base flavour comes from a reduction of white wine, vinegar, shallot and tarragon. The tarragon goes in twice: part is cooked into the reduction and part is stirred in at the end, so both the rich cooked base and the fresh aroma are kept (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).

Heat makes the yolks [coagulate](/principles/protein-coagulation/) slightly, which thickens the watery part first; then you keep whisking to break the butterfat up into it (see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)). A finished sauce flows slowly down the back of a spoon and is best eaten warm. Yolks scramble easily once the edge of the pot boils, so always be ready to lift the bowl off the heat.

## Steps

1. Cook the wine, vinegar, shallot, 1 tbsp tarragon and a little salt and pepper together for about 3–5 minutes, until the liquid has reduced to about 2 tbsp. Pour through a fine sieve, pressing the liquid out of the herbs, and let it cool until it no longer burns your hand; if it reduced too far, add water to bring it back to 2 tbsp.
2. Divide the 30 g cold butter into two portions. Separately melt the 140 g butter over low heat for about 2–3 minutes and keep it warm without letting it boil; after it stands, a white sediment settles at the bottom, so use only the clear butter on top.
3. Whisk the yolks, then add the cooled reduction and one portion of cold butter. Set the bowl over barely simmering water, with the bottom of the bowl not touching the water, and whisk constantly for 1–2 minutes, until the egg thickens and the trails you draw stay for a moment.
4. Move the bowl off the water bath and whisk in the other portion of cold butter. Then add the warm melted butter drop by drop, whisking as you add; once the sauce turns glossy and thick, add the rest in a thin stream, whisking each addition in until no trace of oil is visible.
5. Stir in the 2 tbsp of fresh tarragon for finishing, then taste and add salt. The sauce should flow slowly; if it is too thick, add water 1 tsp at a time. Serve right away; if you have to wait, keep it warm over warm water that is not boiling.

## Key points

- **Flecks of cooked egg appear**: some part was heated too hard and the yolks coagulated too far (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Heat over water while whisking constantly, and take it off the water bath as soon as the trails just hold; if it is clearly grainy, all you can do is start again with fresh yolks.
- **Butter floats on the surface**: the oil went in too fast, or the sauce got too hot and lost water (see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)). Take it off the heat at once and whisk in cold water 1 tsp at a time; if it still separates, start a base with a little warm water in a new bowl and slowly whisk the broken sauce into it.
- **Hard when cold, difficult to pour**: the butterfat has begun to solidify. Warm it over warm water while stirring and take it off as soon as it flows slowly again; putting it directly on the heat easily makes the sauce grainy.
- **Sauce is warm but the yolks are not cooked through**: a sauce whisked to a smooth stage is not the same as cooked yolks. This recipe uses pasteurized egg product; the safe internal temperature for ordinary egg dishes is 74°C (165°F), so do not judge by the thickness of the sauce.
