Beef tallow hot pot base first fries chili, doubanjiang and spices into the fat, and is then thinned into a broth when used. The red oil comes from chili pigment dissolving into the fat. The salty savouriness of the doubanjiang and fermented black beans comes from their own fermentation flavour (see [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/)), and slow frying brings out the sauce aroma further.

This is the quantity for a whole batch, with more than 10 kg of ingredients, so scale it down in proportion for a home kitchen. Leave room in the pot for stirring and foaming, and do not fill it to the rim. The solids stay in the base, and the dry spices come to 120 g in total. The more water you fry off, the lighter the finished base. The fat hardens as it cools, so when you use it, scoop out the oil and the solids together.

## Steps

1. Cut the green onion, ginger, garlic and onion, and dry the surfaces. Clean the dry spices and dry them, smash the large ones and keep coarse pieces. The fennel seed is already counted in the total spice weight and does not need to be weighed separately.
2. Melt the beef tallow in a deep pot over low heat and add the first batch of ginger and garlic, plus all the green onion and onion. Adjust the heat to slow bubbling, about 110–120°C (230–250°F), and fry until the ingredients are golden and fragrant, then lift them out before they blacken.
3. Add the vegetable oil and lard and melt them together over low heat. Move the pot off the heat source and add the pounded chili paste, with its free water drained, in batches, stirring with a long-handled spatula. When the foaming has calmed, return to low heat and keep stirring for 5–8 minutes.
4. Add the doubanjiang and stir to break it up, scraping the bottom of the pot over medium-low heat, until the steam lessens, the oil is a bright red and you can smell the sauce aroma. The solids should be loose, with no burnt black spots.
5. Add the remaining ginger and garlic slices and stir-fry until fragrant for about 15 seconds, then add the fermented black beans, dou mu zi, red Sichuan peppercorns and all the dry spices. Keep the heat low and stir until the spices are evenly spread and their aroma is released (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
6. Turn off the heat and move the pot away, away from open flame, then add the baijiu in several additions, stirring as you add. Once the vigorous foaming has died down, stir-fry over low heat until there is no free water and the oil and solids are evenly mixed.
7. Pack into shallow containers and cool them promptly. Refrigerate within two hours of making it; use it within 2–3 days of refrigeration and freeze the rest. To use, simmer it with water until it boils, and taste the broth before adjusting the salt.

## Key points

- **Violent spattering when ingredients go in**: wet ingredients hit overheated fat and the water turns to steam quickly (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Take the pot off the heat, add ingredients in batches, and leave room in the pot.
- **The red oil tastes burnt and bitter**: the doubanjiang or fine spices have stuck to the bottom and scorched (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Keep scraping the bottom, and stop once the oil is red and the sauce aroma is clear; don't aim for blackened solids.
- **No change visible after a few days, but it spoils**: fat coating wet ingredients is no substitute for the refrigerator. Portion it out and cool it promptly after frying, then refrigerate.
