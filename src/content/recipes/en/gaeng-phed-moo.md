*Kaeng phet mu* is red curry with pork, and its red comes from the dried red chilies in the paste. Coconut milk gives the sauce body and a fatty richness, makrut lime leaves add a citrus note, and Thai sweet basil leaves a layer of freshness. The round eggplant is cooked until the flesh is soft while the skin still holds its shape, and with thin slices of pork it goes over rice in a sauce that stays pourable.

Success depends first on frying the paste. When thick coconut milk is heated, its water drops and some of the fat separates, which carries the aroma out of the curry paste (see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/) and [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Once the rest of the coconut milk goes in, you are cooking in a soup. Slice the pork thin so it cooks through just as the eggplant softens, rather than waiting for thick slices and cooking the vegetables to pieces.

## Steps

1. Slice the pork thin against the grain and separate any slices stuck together. Cut the eggplant into wedges, slice the chilies on the diagonal, tear the makrut lime leaves, and wash and drain the basil. Do not shake the can of coconut milk; scoop off about 100 ml of the thick fat layer and keep the rest.
2. Put the vegetable oil, the thick coconut milk and the red curry paste in a pot and stir constantly over medium heat. When the bubbles get smaller, the aroma is strong and bright oil droplets appear at the edges, add the pork. The paste should darken but not turn black.
3. Turn the pork so every slice is coated in the paste. When the surface starts to change colour, pour in the remaining coconut milk. Push gently with a spatula to bring all the paste from the bottom of the pot into the liquid, and cook until small bubbles rise at the edge.
4. Add the eggplant, half the fish sauce and the sugar, turn to low heat, cover and cook for about 5 minutes. The eggplant is ready when a fork pierces it easily, and the pork is done when the thickest slice reaches 71°C (160°F). If the eggplant still has a hard core, cook a little longer.
5. Add the makrut lime leaves and chilies and push them gently until the aroma of the leaves is released and the chilies have softened a little. Taste the sauce and add as much of the remaining fish sauce as it needs. The salty-savoury note and the heat should be clear, with the sweetness only underneath.
6. Turn off the heat and stir in the sweet basil. Serve as soon as the leaves wilt, with hot rice. If the curry has reduced too thick, add a little water before taking it off the heat so the sauce can flow.

## Key points

- **Handsome red colour but a raw taste of paste**: coconut milk went in before the curry paste was fully fried in the fat. Wait until the smell deepens and the colour darkens before adding the main ingredients (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
- **Only big pools of oil on top, and the sauce gets saltier as it cooks**: too much water has evaporated, the fat has gathered and the seasoning has concentrated. Small oil droplets are normal; if it has reduced too far, add a little water and stop heating (see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)).
- **Pork is tough and the vegetables are mushy**: the slices were cut too thick, or cooked on after they were done, so water keeps being squeezed out of the meat. Slice thin against the grain, and lift the pork out if it is done while you wait for the eggplant (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
