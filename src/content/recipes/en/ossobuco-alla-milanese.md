After *ossobuco alla milanese* has braised, the meat is so tender that a fork parts it, and the marrow in the middle of the bone stays soft. It uses cross-cut slices of the bone-in hind shank of veal, so ask the butcher for bone-in shank slices. Milan also has a white version without tomato. This recipe is the classic one with a little tomato, finished with a *gremolada* of lemon zest, garlic and parsley.

The connective tissue in the shank needs moist heat and time to turn slowly into gelatin. That is what makes the meat tender and the sauce silky (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). The brown base flavour from searing comes from the [Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). Browning and braising are done separately so the sauce has both aroma and body.

## Steps

1. Preheat the oven to 175°C (350°F). Snip a few shallow cuts in the connective tissue around the edge of each slice, tie kitchen string around the edge, and pat the surface dry. Choose a heavy lidded pot in which the four slices lie flat in one layer.
2. Put the butter, onion, carrot and celery in the pot and cook over medium heat for about 6–7 minutes. Add the minced garlic and lemon zest strips and cook until the vegetables are soft and fragrant, then take the pot off the heat.
3. Heat the vegetable oil in a separate skillet over medium-high heat. Coat the slices lightly in flour on both sides, shaking off the loose flour. Brown them in batches until golden brown on both sides, and move them onto the vegetables. Do not turn them too early, or the flour coating will stick to the pan.
4. Pour off the leftover oil from the skillet and add the white wine, scraping up the browned bits. Cook until the wine reduces slightly, then pour it into the pot together with the scraped-up juices.
5. Heat the stock, pour it into the pot, and add the tomatoes, thyme, bay leaf, whole parsley sprigs, salt and black pepper. The liquid should come two-thirds of the way up the meat. If it does not, top up with hot stock.
6. Bring the pot to a gentle simmer on the stove, cover, and put it on the lower rack of the oven. Braise for about 2 hours. About every 20 minutes, turn the slices gently and baste them with the sauce. If the liquid runs low, add a little hot water. The dish is done when a fork slides in easily and the connective tissue around the edge is soft.
7. Mix the lemon zest, very finely minced garlic and chopped parsley. Sprinkle it into the pot and heat for no more than 2 minutes. Lift out the whole herb sprigs and the zest strips, and cut off the string. If the sauce is too thin, take out the meat first, reduce the sauce separately, then pour it back over the meat.

## Key points

- **The meat is cooked but the connective tissue is still chewy**: the collagen has not softened all the way (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep a gentle simmer and test the tissue with a fork. Do not boil hard to rush it.
- **The meat browns grey and has no browned aroma**: the surface was wet or the pan was crowded, so water cooked the meat first (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Dry the meat, brown it in batches and leave a gap between slices.
- **The finish tastes only bitter and has no fresh aroma**: the white pith of the lemon got into the zest, or the herbs were cooked too long (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Use only the yellow outer peel and add it just before serving.
