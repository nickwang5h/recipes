Beer-braised duck uses beer as the braising liquid, doubanjiang for savouriness and heat, and the fat in the duck skin to catch the aroma of the whole spices. [Blanching](/principles/blanching/) first clears the surface scum, then the duck is drained and fried, and the seasonings go in only after the skin has rendered its fat, so the braising liquid is not covered in a layer of grey scum.

The duck becomes tender through moist heat and time, which turn collagen into gelatin (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). The age of the duck, the cut and the size of the pieces all change the softening time. The meat turning white is only a starting point: check also that the sinew and meat pull apart easily.

## Steps

1. Cut the duck into pieces about 3 cm across, and check for and remove bone splinters. Start in cold water with the ginger slices and Shaoxing wine, and heat until the scum rises and collects. Skim it, lift out the pieces, rinse the surface, then drain and blot dry thoroughly.
2. Add the oil to a wok and heat over medium heat. Add the duck pieces when the oil flows quickly but does not smoke. Spread them out and stir-fry with the skin touching the bottom of the pan, until the meat changes colour, the edges of the skin turn lightly golden and duck fat starts to collect in the pan.
3. Spoon off the excess oil, leaving only about 30 ml in the pan. Push the duck to one side, turn the heat to low, add the doubanjiang and rock sugar, and stir-fry until red oil separates out and the sauce smells strongly. The sugar will then dissolve in the braising liquid.
4. Add the cloves, star anise, bay leaves, dried chilies and garlic cloves, fry briefly until fragrant, then pour in the light and dark soy sauce and mix. Do not fry the spices until they blacken, and if the bottom of the pan is too dry, pour in the beer right away.
5. Pour in the beer so the liquid nearly covers the duck; if the pot is wide and coverage is short, add a little hot water. Once it boils, add the optional chicken bouillon, cover, reduce to medium-low heat and keep it at a gentle boil, braising first for 30 minutes.
6. Take the largest piece: it is done when the centre reaches 74°C (165°F), a fork goes in easily and the sinew and meat pull apart. If it is still tough, braise 5 minutes more and check again; if there is not enough liquid, add hot water.
7. Once the duck is as tender as you like, uncover, pick out the large whole spices and reduce the sauce over medium heat. Turn gently until the sauce can coat the duck pieces, taste a piece with its sauce and decide whether to add salt, and take it off the heat while a little flowing sauce is still left at the bottom of the pan.

## Key points

- **The duck is white but still tough**: the muscle is cooked but the [collagen](/principles/collagen-gelatin/) has not softened enough. Keep braising gently. 30 minutes is only the time for the first check, not the endpoint for every duck.
- **The dish has a layer of oil and the sauce will not cling**: both the added oil and the oil rendered from the duck skin stayed in the pan. After frying, spoon off the extra oil and let the seasonings release their aroma in a small amount of oil (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
- **The sauce is bitter and the spices overpower the duck**: the sauce or the spices were scorched, or the beer itself is strongly bitter. Fry the spices briefly over low heat (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)), and redo the sauce if it has burned black.
