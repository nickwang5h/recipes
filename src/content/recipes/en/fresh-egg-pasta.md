Fresh egg pasta (*Pasta all'uovo*) is made by turning eggs and flour into thin sheets and cutting them into wide noodles, which suit [Bolognese ragù](/recipes/ragu-alla-bolognese/) well. The classic ratio is 1 egg per 100 g of flour, with no added water. Whole eggs supply the water, protein and fat. The dough is firm and smooth when kneaded, and supple and hard to tear once rolled thin.

The flour takes up water from the eggs, and with each knead and press the wheat proteins join into a [gluten](/principles/gluten/) network. During the rest the water evens out and the tension built by kneading relaxes. In the pot, the starch swells with water, and the gluten and the egg [proteins denature and coagulate](/principles/protein-coagulation/), fixing the shape of the noodles. In other words, kneading lets the dough stretch, and cooking sets the structure.

## Steps

1. Work the eggs into the flour gradually. Heap the flour into a mound with a well in the middle and put the eggs in the centre. Beat them with a fork and draw flour in from the inner ring. Keep the outer wall intact until the centre becomes a dough that holds together.
2. Push with the heel of your hand and fold the dough back. Knead in the parts that haven't mixed, until the surface is smooth, the cut face shows no dry flour, and the dough feels firm and springy. If the eggs were small, don't force in any flour that is left over.
3. Wrap the dough and rest it for 30 minutes. Cover it tightly with plastic wrap or an upturned bowl so no skin forms. After resting, press lightly: the dough should flatten more easily than when you finished kneading.
4. Divide the dough and roll it thin. Cover the pieces you aren't using. Roll from the centre outward, turning the sheet to keep it an even thickness. With a pasta machine, start on the widest setting and reduce one setting at a time, until the sheet is thin enough to show the outline of your hand through it.
5. Fold the sheet loosely and cut it into wide strips. Dust with only a thin layer of flour to stop sticking, and shake out the strips and spread them loose as soon as they are cut. The noodles should be separate, with neat edges, and the folds shouldn't stick into one lump.
6. Cook in boiling salted water. Gently separate the strands after they go in, and taste one to judge. When the noodles are soft and supple and there is no raw flour taste in the centre, lift them into the sauce you have ready and toss gently. Fresh pasta cooks quickly, so don't go only by whether the noodles float.

## Key points

- **The dough is crumbly and cracks when rolled**: there isn't enough water, or the [gluten](/principles/gluten/) hasn't joined up yet. Cover and rest it before kneading again. If it still cracks dry, knead in a little water with wet hands. Don't pour in water all at once, or the dough turns sticky.
- **It keeps shrinking back and the sheet won't spread out**: the tension built by kneading and pressing the [gluten](/principles/gluten/) hasn't relaxed. Cover it and rest again, then roll from the middle outward. If you pull it by force, the edges get thin while the centre stays thick.
- **Soft outside and hard inside after cooking**: the sheet is uneven in thickness, and the starch in the centre hasn't absorbed enough water to gelatinize (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Hold the dough to the light while rolling to spot thick areas, and thin them before cutting. After the noodles go in, taste the centre and lift them out only when cooked through.
- **Mushy noodles that break easily**: they cooked too long and absorbed too much water, and the [gluten](/principles/gluten/) network was stretched apart. Have the sauce ready first, lift the noodles out as soon as they are done, and toss gently in the sauce.
