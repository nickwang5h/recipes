*Coq au vin* is chicken cooked in red wine, with bacon, pearl onions and mushrooms. Traditionally an old rooster is stewed slowly; here an ordinary chicken from a Canadian supermarket is lifted out as soon as it is cooked, so the breast does not dry out. The dark sauce comes from the red wine and the browned bits left in the pan from frying the chicken (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).

Once the chicken is cooked, the sauce is reduced separately and a butter and flour paste is added, which thickens it by [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Cooking the onions and mushrooms separately lets you control how much colour and how soft each one gets, and keeps all the water from the mushrooms out of the braising liquid.

## Steps

1. Simmer the bacon strips gently in water for about 10 minutes, then lift out, drain and blot the surface dry.
2. Fry the bacon in the butter set aside for the chicken until lightly golden, and remove it. Pat the chicken pieces dry and fry them in batches until the skin is golden brown and there are brown deposits on the bottom of the pan. Season with a little salt and pepper, return the bacon, cover and cook over low heat for about 10 minutes, turning the pieces halfway.
3. Take the pan off the heat and pour in the brandy, then return it to the stove and let it cook uncovered for a short while. Add the red wine and scrape up the brown bits from the bottom of the pan with a wooden spoon, then add stock until the chicken is mostly submerged in liquid. Add the tomato paste, garlic, thyme and bay leaf.
4. Cover and simmer gently for about 25–30 minutes, with only the occasional bubble breaking the surface. Test the thickest part of the breast and of the leg separately, and lift each out when the centre has reached 74°C (165°F) and the meat is tender, keeping the probe away from the bone; take out the breast first as it cooks first.
5. In another pan, use the oil and butter set aside for the onions to brown the pearl onions, rolling them until the surface is mottled golden. Pour in the reserved stock, cover and simmer over low heat for about 40–50 minutes, until the centres are soft but the onions are still whole; this can be done at the same time as the chicken.
6. Use the oil and butter set aside for the mushrooms to sauté the mushrooms in batches until the surface is golden and there is no water collecting on the bottom of the pan, and set them aside.
7. Remove the bay leaf from the braising liquid, skim off the fat, and reduce uncovered to about 530 ml. Knead the softened butter and flour into a paste and stir it in a little at a time; simmer gently until the raw flour taste is gone and the sauce thinly coats the back of a spoon. Return the chicken, onions and mushrooms and simmer gently until heated through, then taste and add salt.

## Key points

- **Pale chicken skin and water in the pan**: the surface was not dried or too many pieces went in at once, so the pan turned to poaching before any browning. Fry in batches, keep the brown bits on the bottom and throw away any burnt ones. See [the Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).
- **Dry chicken breast**: cooked meat left cooking in the pot, so the proteins tighten more and more and squeeze out water. Lift the chicken out when it is done and let it wait outside the pot while the sauce reduces. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **Sauce has flour lumps**: the flour was not kneaded into the butter first, so it clumps when it meets the hot liquid. Make a smooth paste and stir it in a little at a time; if lumps have already formed, strain them out before adjusting the thickness. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
