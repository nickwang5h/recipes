Sausage and white beans is rich and savoury, with floury, creamy beans coated in a thick tomato sauce. Each bite tastes of meat and has the slight tartness of red wine.

The sausage is browned first, and the browned flavour from the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) stays on the bottom of the pan. Pouring in red wine dissolves this brown residue and carries it into the sauce. Tomato supplies glutamate and meat supplies nucleotides; together they taste more savoury than either alone (see [Umami](/principles/umami/)).

The beans are already cooked, so the simmering is not to soften them but to let them take up the flavour of the sauce. The tomato sauce is acidic, and acid makes plant tissue like beans harder to soften (see [Acid: Balance, Softening and Stability](/principles/acid/)). So the sooner the beans are fully cooked, the better, and the simmering only has to reduce the sauce and let the flavour in.

## Steps

1. Mince the shallot, put the olive oil in a pan and cook the shallot until golden. Remove the casings from the sausages and add them to the pan over high heat, turning until all sides are dark brown and a layer of brown residue sticks to the bottom of the pan.
2. Pour in the red wine and cook until the smell of alcohol is gone and the brown residue on the bottom has dissolved, then add the passata. Bring to a boil, add a little salt and stir.
3. Add the drained white beans, turn to low, cover and simmer for about 30 minutes, until the beans have soaked up the red sauce. Check partway whether the pan is too dry, and add a little hot water if necessary.
4. When the sauce is thick and the sausages are soft, check the centre of a sausage with a thermometer; 71°C (160°F) is enough. Taste, add salt and black pepper, and let it rest a short while before eating.

## Key points

- **The pan dries out**: water still evaporates slowly during a low-heat simmer. Check partway, and add a little hot water if there is too little sauce.
- **Sausage looks grey and has no browned flavour**: the pan is crowded or the surface is wet, so the temperature cannot rise and the sausage is cooked through rather than browned ([Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Do not crowd the sausages, and let all sides brown before you pour in the wine.
- **Beans fall apart**: the beans are already cooked, and stirring too hard breaks them. Stir gently with a spatula and keep the heat low.
- **For lunch boxes**: refrigerate in containers and reheat the next day to 74°C (165°F) at the centre; add a little water if the sauce is too dry.
