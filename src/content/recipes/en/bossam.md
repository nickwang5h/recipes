Bossam is boiled pork sliced thin and eaten wrapped in a leaf with kimchi and salted baby shrimp. The pork itself is mildly seasoned; the salty, savoury and sour-hot notes come mainly from the fillings wrapped inside. Pork belly has layers of fat and lean, and after being cooked tender it can still be cut into whole slices.

The tenderness comes from [collagen turning to gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), which needs moist heat and time. By the time the centre of the pork reaches a safe temperature, the skin and sinew may still be tough, so keep cooking on low heat until a chopstick goes through. After the heat is off, leave the pork in the broth for a short while so the surface does not dry out right away; slice only just before serving.

## Steps

1. Trim the pork belly into long strips about 7 cm wide and keep them whole, not sliced thin yet. Choose a pot that holds both strips flat, add 2400 ml water, the doenjang, onion, green onion, garlic, ginger, black pepper, instant coffee and salt, and stir to dissolve the doenjang.
2. Heat over medium-high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until the broth is at a steady boil, then cook for 5 minutes more, until the green onion, ginger and garlic smell clearly fragrant and the doenjang has dissolved completely.
3. Add the pork belly and heat over medium-high for about 3 minutes, until the broth returns to a boil. Cook uncovered for 5 minutes and skim off the foam. Cover and cook over medium-high heat until 60 minutes have passed since the pork went in, then turn the heat to low for 15 minutes, keeping the broth at a gentle bubble so the boiling water does not shove the strips around.
4. At 40 minutes, turn the pork once and check the thickest part. The centre must finally reach 71°C (160°F), and a chopstick should pass easily through the fat, lean and skin. If it is still chewy, add the held-back 200 ml of water, cook 10 minutes longer each time and test again; turn off the heat when it is tender but can still be lifted out whole.
5. Leave the pork in the broth for 10 minutes, until the surface stops steaming hard and it can be lifted steadily. Meanwhile arrange the washed and drained lettuce, the kimchi and the salted baby shrimp on separate plates, with the shrimp in a small dish so you can take a little at a time.
6. Lift the warm pork onto a clean cutting board and slice it across the grain into 3–4 mm slices, keeping the fat, lean and skin together as much as possible. Serve right away. On each lettuce leaf put one or two slices of pork, a piece of kimchi and a little shrimp, and wrap into a one-bite parcel.

## Key points

- **Fully cooked but the skin is still hard to chew**: being cooked through does not mean being soft; the collagen has not softened enough. Top up with water as measured, keep cooking on low heat, and test the skin and sinew with a chopstick. See [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).
- **The lean is dry and hard and the slices fall apart**: the heat was too high or the cooking too long, so water was squeezed out of the meat and the layers loosened. Simmer gently and stop once a chopstick goes through, and slice while warm rather than leaving it on the board. See [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **A bite tastes too salty**: the kimchi, the shrimp and the cooking broth all carry salt. Take only a little shrimp per bite, use plenty of lettuce, and do not salt the slices again. See [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/).
