*Cá kho tộ* is a rich braised fish from southern Vietnam, usually made with bone-in catfish steaks. The caramel, *nước màu*, gives a dark brown colour and a slightly bitter, scorched aroma. Fish sauce gives salty savouriness, and a little sugar holds up the bitter notes. The colour comes from [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/). Make the caramel separately first; once the fish goes in, it cooks slowly in moist heat.

Thick bone-in steaks keep their shape and suit a long braise in a small amount of sauce. Basa fillets also work, but thin pieces break more easily, so you cannot use the same time as for bone-in fish. The clay pot must be rated for the stovetop if you heat it directly. Make the caramel, green onion and garlic in a metal pot first, then move everything to the clay pot. The vessel decides how fast things heat; see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).

## Steps

1. Make the caramel. Add 15 ml of water to the sugar and heat over low heat until it is an even dark brown with a scorched aroma, then take it off the heat. When the violent bubbling eases, pour in 30 ml of hot water slowly, in several additions. Return to low heat to dissolve, let it cool, then add water to make about 60 ml. Measure out 30 ml of it.
2. Marinate the fish. Clean any blood and membrane from the belly and pat the surface dry. Toss the fish with the caramel, brown sugar, black pepper, salt and fish sauce to coat it. Cover and refrigerate for 15 minutes.
3. Stir-fry the green onion and garlic until fragrant. Put the oil in a small metal pot and stir-fry the sliced garlic and the white parts of the green onion over medium heat until fragrant and just pale golden. Pour them, oil included, into the room-temperature clay pot. Arrange the fish in a tight single layer and pour in the marinade as well.
4. Start braising the fish. Bring the clay pot up slowly from low heat, cover and cook for about 10 minutes, so the fish heats first in its own juices and steam. Check partway that there is still liquid in the bottom; if it looks dry, add a little of the prepared hot water.
5. Add water and keep braising. Pour in the rest of the braising water until it almost covers the fish. Keep it at small, steady bubbles, cover and cook for about 30 minutes. Do not stir with a spatula; gently tilt the pot or spoon the sauce over the exposed fish.
6. Reduce the sauce. Uncover and cook for another 15–18 minutes, until the sauce has reduced by about half, looks dark and glossy, and lightly coats the fish. The flesh at the thickest part should be opaque and flake easily along the grain. If you use thin fillets, take them out once cooked and reduce the sauce separately.
7. Spoon the sauce over and serve. Taste a little of the sauce; if it is too salty, add hot water and stir in gently. Lift the fish onto a shallow dish with a wide spatula, spoon the sauce over and eat with white rice.

## Key points

- **Sauce is black and acrid, and tastes burnt and bitter**: the caramel was cooked too far (see [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/)). If it turns black and gives off heavy smoke, throw it out and start again.
- **Fish breaks up and the flesh is dry**: a hard boil, stirring, or long cooking of thin fillets all damage the flesh (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Use thick bone-in steaks, keep the pot at a bare simmer, and lift the fish from underneath.
- **Clay pot cracks**: the temperature change was too great, or the empty pot got too hot (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Start a room-temperature pot over low heat and add only hot water. If your clay pot is not rated for direct heat, use a heavy-bottomed metal pot instead.
