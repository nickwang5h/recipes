Dorayaki is two round honey pancakes sandwiching sweet red bean paste. The classic filling is *tsubuan*, which keeps the beans whole, and it is a little thicker in the middle, so the closed pancakes look like a small drum. The pancake is fine and soft, with a complete brown face outside; the reducing sugars in the honey and the proteins in the egg and flour take part in the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/), giving colour and a toasted aroma.

Baking powder helps the pancakes rise, and the egg also supports the inner structure. Once the flour goes in, mix only until there is no dry flour; over-beating develops [gluten](/principles/gluten/), and the pancakes turn tough. Rest the batter in the refrigerator and then adjust its thickness; using a similar amount for each pancake when cooking gives the same thickness and heating. This makes six dorayaki, which means cooking twelve small pancakes of about 8 cm.

## Steps

1. Whisk the eggs, sugar and honey together for about 3 minutes, until paler in colour and the egg falls in a slightly thick ribbon when you lift the whisk. Sift in the flour and baking powder and fold with a spatula until no dry flour is visible and there are no lumps of flour at the bottom of the bowl.
2. Cover the batter and refrigerate for 15 minutes, until the batter is more even than when first mixed and the lumps of flour have absorbed water and loosened. Stir in 1 tbsp of the water first; if it falls in a clump after you scoop it up, stir in the reserved 1 tbsp water in stages, until it falls continuously and spreads out on its own.
3. Preheat a nonstick skillet on the lowest heat for 5 minutes so the bottom heats evenly, then raise it to medium-low. Wipe the bottom thinly with oil on paper towel, then wipe off any visible droplets with a clean paper towel, leaving only a nearly invisible thin film of oil on the pan.
4. Each time, pour 3 tbsp of batter from about 8 cm above the pan and let it flow naturally into a circle about 8 cm across. Cook for 1–1.5 minutes, until bubbles appear on the surface, the edges begin to set and the underside is an even brown, then flip with a wide spatula.
5. Cook the other side for 30 seconds, until the edge is dry, the centre springs back when pressed and there is no wet batter inside when you break open a test pancake. Take it out with the nicer side up and cover loosely with a wrung-out damp cloth. Cook the rest of the batter in batches in the same way, wiping off the excess oil from the pan each time.
6. Let the pancakes cool, covered, for about 5 minutes, until only warm to the touch and the surface is still soft. Divide the red bean paste into six portions and sandwich it between pairs of pancakes, mounded in the middle and thinner around the edges, pressing the edges together lightly, and wrap each one to keep it from drying out.

## Key points

- **Blotchy surface with pale round spots**: oil droplets on the pan isolate the batter in places, so contact and [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/) are uneven. Wipe off the oil after each application, and watch the pan temperature with the first pancake.
- **Very dark outside while the inside is still sticky**: the pan is too hot, and the browning gets ahead of the inside setting. Lower the heat and keep each pancake the same size (see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).
- **Pancakes are tough and hard to bite once cooled**: the batter was beaten too long after the flour went in, or the pancakes were left uncovered to dry after leaving the pan. Mix lightly, rest the batter and cover the pancakes once they are cooked (see [gluten](/principles/gluten/)).
