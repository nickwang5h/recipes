Tea eggs have brown veins across the white, and when you bite in there is the fragrance of tea, star anise and cassia. The method is to cook the eggs first, then tap the shells into fine cracks so the black tea and spice liquid seeps into the white along the cracks. The shell is not peeled. Tea and spices are simmered briefly in water so the aroma comes out slowly (see [spices and aromatic extraction](/principles/aromatics/)). The more evenly the cracks are tapped, the more evenly the liquid seeps in.

Flavour comes from time and contact (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Boiling hard the whole time only makes the outer layer firm quickly and cannot replace soaking; cooked too long, the egg's [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) goes too far and the white turns tough. So simmer briefly, strain out the solids, and soak in the refrigerator, which looks after both the marbling and the texture. The soak needs a night in the fridge, so leave that time aside.

## Steps

1. Put the whole eggs in a pot in a single layer and cover with cold water. Bring to a boil, then turn down to a steady gentle boil and cook for about 8 minutes from when the water boils; use small to medium eggs, and cook large eggs a little longer, until both white and yolk are set.
2. Move the eggs to cold water and cool until you can hold them. Tap the whole shell all over with the back of a spoon to make fine cracks spread evenly. The shell and inner membrane should stay on the egg; do not knock off large pieces of shell or crush the white.
3. Put the eggs in a pot that fits them snugly. Add the water for the braising liquid, star anise, bay leaf, cassia, fennel seeds, rock sugar, black tea and both soy sauces from the ingredient list. If the liquid is low, top up until it just covers the eggs, and taste the liquid for saltiness before deciding whether to add salt.
4. Bring to a boil over high heat, then turn to low and keep a faint bubble for 15 minutes. Turn the eggs gently partway through so every side soaks in the liquid. If the water level drops, add a little hot water so the liquid does not get more concentrated as it cooks.
5. Lift out the eggs and strain out the tea leaves and spices. Divide the eggs and liquid into shallow containers. Set the containers in cold water to help them cool, then let the eggs soak back in the liquid and put them in the refrigerator as soon as possible; do not leave the whole pot out at room temperature overnight.
6. After soaking in the fridge overnight, peel one to check: there should be brown veins along the cracks and the outer white should taste of the braising liquid. Take out only as many as you will eat and gently reheat them in the liquid until hot through; do not bring the whole batch to a boil every day.

## Key points

- **Tea tastes bitter and astringent, spices overpower the egg**: too much tea or too long a simmer (see [spices and aromatic extraction](/principles/aromatics/)). Strain out the solids after the short simmer and soak only in the clear liquid afterwards.
- **Outside is very salty, centre is bland**: [salt](/principles/salt/) takes time to travel from outside to inside, and more salt only makes the outer layer saltier. Tap the cracks evenly and soak longer in the fridge instead of making the liquid stronger to make up flavour.
- **White is tough, and you are unsure about keeping them**: they were cooked too long, so [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) went too far; eggs soaking in the liquid must be refrigerated. Put them in the refrigerator within 2 hours of cooking, and eat eggs soaked in the liquid within 3–4 days; do not soak them at room temperature for two days.
