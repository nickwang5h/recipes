*Thịt kho trứng* is a braise of pork and eggs from southern Vietnam. Coconut water adds a light sweetness, fish sauce adds salty savouriness, and caramel colour stains the pork and eggs brown. The pork leg keeps its skin and fat, so lean meat, soft skin and egg white each have their own texture, and the braising liquid is left generous so you can spoon some over rice.

Pork that is cooked through is not yet tender. The skin and connective tissue need sustained moist heat, so that collagen slowly turns to gelatin (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). The caramel colour is made separately before braising, finishing its [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/) to give a sweet, slightly bitter flavour; while the pork cooks, low heat slowly draws the colour and the salty-sweet flavour into the liquid.

## Steps

1. Make the caramel colour. Cook 50 g of sugar with 15 ml of water over low heat until it turns deep brown and smells of caramel, then take it off the heat. When the violent bubbling eases, slowly add 30 ml of hot water in portions, return to low heat to dissolve, let cool and top up with water to about 60 ml.
2. Marinate the pork. Check the pork leg and remove any stray hairs, then cut into pieces about 2.5 cm thick and 5–7 cm long, each with as much skin and fat as possible. Toss with the caramel colour, fish sauce and the sugar for seasoning, cover and refrigerate for 45 minutes, turning once partway so the surface colours evenly.
3. Brown the pork. Move the pork and marinade to a narrower heavy-bottomed pot, bring to a boil over medium heat covered, then turn to low and cook for about 5 minutes, turning the pieces gently, until the surface is an even golden brown. Keep the bottom of the pot moist.
4. Add the eggs and braising liquid. Gently lower the peeled hard-boiled eggs into the pot, pour in the coconut water, then add hot water to just cover. Bring to a boil, skim off the foam and adjust the heat so only small bubbles rise.
5. Braise until soft. Leave the lid ajar and cook for about 75 minutes, occasionally turning any exposed pork and eggs down into the liquid. A thermometer in the thickest piece reading 71°C (160°F) only shows the pork is cooked; it is tender when the tip of a knife slides easily through skin and meat.
6. Reduce the braising liquid. Uncover and cook for about 15 minutes more, until the liquid has reduced by about half and the pork is soft and intact. The liquid should still flow and be spoonable; do not reduce it to a sticky syrup. Rest for a few minutes, then skim off the excess fat.
7. Plate and serve. Halve the eggs, put them with the pork in a deep dish and pour over the braising liquid. If it tastes too salty, add hot water and stir to combine. Serve with white rice; the yolk can be broken up and mixed into the liquid.

## Key points

- **Lean meat is dry and the skin is still tough**: the cut was too lean, or there was not enough time in moist heat (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep the skin and fat, cook slowly at small bubbles, add hot water if it runs short, then see whether the skin is soft.
- **Braising liquid is bitter and burnt, and the colour is black**: the caramel colour was taken too far, or the liquid was reduced too dry at the end (see [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/)). Redo a burnt, black caramel; lower the heat in time as the liquid reduces, and leave enough to spoon over rice.
- **Pork falls apart and the egg white is hard**: constant hard boiling and repeated turning overheat the outer layer (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Keep it at a gentle simmer, turn lightly and stop the heat once it is tender.
