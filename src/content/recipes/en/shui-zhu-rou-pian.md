For Sichuan boiled pork slices, the vegetables go in the bottom of the bowl first, then the tenderloin cooked in red broth is laid over them, and the chopped chilies and hot oil go on last. The egg white and starch around the pork slices set with heat into a smooth thin layer (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Tenderloin has little sinew, so longer cooking mainly dries it out, and tenderness still depends on controlling [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).

Chopped chilies are dried chilies and peppercorns fried until fragrant and then chopped. Frying, making the broth and pouring the hot oil each use a little oil. Fry the spices only until crisp and fragrant; if they turn black they taste bitter (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).

## Steps

1. Trim the membrane off the pork and cut it into slices about 2 mm thick. Add 1.5 g of salt, 1 g of pepper, the light soy sauce and the Shaoxing wine, and stir in one direction for about 2 minutes. Mix the egg white with 7 g of starch, stir it into the pork, and finally stir in 10 g of oil.
2. Wash and drain the bean sprouts, cut the celtuce leaves into strips, and cut the celery and garlic shoots into pieces. Mince the ginger, divide the minced garlic into 15 g and 5 g portions, chop the pickled chilies, and slice the green onions.
3. In the wok, use 10 g of oil and fry 15 g of dried chilies and 3 g of green Sichuan peppercorns over low heat. Pour them out when the chilies turn brittle, slightly darker, and smell fragrant, and chop them once cool. Throw out any batch that shows black spots or a burnt smell and start over.
4. In the wok, use 20 g of oil to fry the remaining dried chilies and peppercorns until fragrant, then add the vegetables and 1 g of salt. Stir-fry until the bean sprouts and celery are cooked through and the leaves are soft, and transfer to a wide, deep heatproof bowl.
5. In the wok, use 30 g of oil to fry the minced ginger, 15 g of minced garlic and the pickled chilies until fragrant, add the doubanjiang and fry over low heat until the oil turns red. Pour in 800 ml of water and bring to a boil, add the sugar, the remaining pepper and the optional bouillon powder, then taste and add salt.
6. If you want the broth to cling slightly to the pork, mix the thickening starch with 4 ml of cold water, drizzle it into the boiling broth and stir evenly. Turn the broth to a gentle simmer and add the pork slices one by one, nudging them gently once the coating has set. When the centre of the thickest slice is cooked through, lift the pork onto the vegetables.
7. Pour the hot broth under the pork, leaving room at the rim. Scatter the chopped chilies, the remaining minced garlic and the green onions on top. Heat 60 g of oil in a separate pot until it flows and shows fine ripples but has not yet smoked, take it off the heat, test with a small amount first, then pour a thin stream over the spices.

## Key points

- **Pork slices are dry and curled tight**: the tenderloin stayed in the hot broth too long (see [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Cook the vegetables in advance, and lift the pork out as soon as it is cooked.
- **Lumps of starch in the broth**: dry starch met hot broth directly, so the outside gelatinized first and enclosed the inside (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Mix the thickening starch with cold water, and thicken only slightly.
- **Burnt, bitter taste in the numbing heat**: the chopped chilies were fried black, or the hot oil was too hot (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Take the spices out of the wok as soon as they are fragrant, and test the hot oil with a small stream first.
