Stir-fried eggplant is soft and well seasoned, each piece coated in the fragrance of soy sauce, and you can still taste the eggplant itself. Raw eggplant flesh has a loose structure and soaks up oil; heat collapses the structure, and that is when the texture turns soft. Whether it is cooked depends on whether the heat has reached the centre (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Cut the pieces to similar size so the whole pan finishes at the same time.

Star anise, dried shrimp and green onion are all optional. Bring out their aroma in gentle oil first, then add the eggplant so it picks up the flavour (see [spices and aromatic extraction](/principles/aromatics/)). Soy sauce and dried shrimp are both salty, so leave room when seasoning.

## Steps

1. Wash and drain the eggplant, split it lengthwise, then cut it into diamond-shaped chunks of similar size. Prepare the green onion and break up the star anise; measure the sugar and vinegar separately and keep them for the end.
2. If using ground pork, break it up in the pork-frying oil over medium heat. Stir-fry for about 1 minute, until all the meat has changed colour with no raw meat left, then cook it through and remove.
3. Wipe the pan dry, add the oil for the eggplant, and heat until the oil flows lightly without smoking. Add the star anise, dried shrimp and green onion as desired, and stir-fry until you smell the aroma, then add the eggplant; do not let the spices blacken in the oil.
4. Stir-fry over medium-high heat for about 40 seconds, then add half the light soy sauce, drizzling it in directly and tossing. Add the soy sauce in stages, and use a little less when there is little eggplant or you used dried shrimp.
5. Keep stir-frying for about 1 minute, until the eggplant begins to turn half-soft, then return the cooked pork to the pan. Scoop up from the bottom and toss, stir-frying until all the pieces have collapsed and the thickest piece has no white hard core. If the pan is dry but the eggplant is still hard, add a little of the measured water and turn down the heat.
6. Add the sugar and vinegar as desired and stir-fry for about 20 seconds more. Taste a piece of eggplant with sauce to decide whether to add more light soy sauce or salt; take out the star anise, and plate when only a thin layer of sauce is left in the pan.

## Key points

- **A pan full of oil but the flesh is still hard**: more oil does not mean the centre is cooked (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Keep turning and judge by the thickest piece; if the pan is dry add a little water, and do not keep adding oil to fix a hard core.
- **Star anise and dried shrimp taste burnt and bitter**: the aromatics sat too long in overheated oil (see [spices and aromatic extraction](/principles/aromatics/)). Add the eggplant as soon as you smell the aroma, and cool the pan first if the oil starts to smoke.
- **Soy sauce flavour turns dead salty**: all the soy sauce went in at once, on top of the dried shrimp and the reduction (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Use part of the soy sauce first, and just before serving taste a piece of eggplant with sauce to decide whether to add the rest.
