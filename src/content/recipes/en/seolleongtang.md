*Seolleongtang* is a Korean beef bone soup: the broth is as white as milk and tastes rich, yet it is barely seasoned. Thin slices of beef go in the bowl with rice or thin noodles, and salt, pepper and green onion sit on the table for each person to add.

For the broth to turn white, the heat has to stay high enough to keep it at a rolling boil. The churning breaks the fat from the marrow into tiny droplets, and the gelatin and proteins drawn out of the bones hold them in the broth (see [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)); a gentle simmer gives only a clear stock. Soaking and blanching the bones first removes blood and scum, so the white is clean (see [blanching and removing off-odours](/principles/blanching/)). The brisket goes in later and comes out as soon as its connective tissue is tender (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)).

## Steps

1. Put the bones in a large bowl, cover with cold water and soak in the refrigerator for 1–3 hours, changing the water once, until it turns pale pink. Soak the brisket in a separate bowl of cold water in the refrigerator for 1 hour.
2. Drain the bones, put them in the stockpot and cover with cold water. Bring to a boil over high heat, then boil over medium heat for 5 minutes, until a lot of scum rises. Pour off the water, rinse each bone clean, and scrub the brown bits off the sides and bottom of the pot.
3. Return the bones to the pot, add cold water to within a few centimetres of the rim, leaving room for it to boil, bring to a boil over high heat and skim. Turn to medium, cover, and keep the broth at a rolling boil, not a gentle bubble. Boil for 5 hours, topping up with boiling water when the level nears the tops of the bones, usually once or twice. If the broth is still clear after 2–3 hours, turn the heat up a little.
4. Once the broth is white, like thin milk, add the drained brisket, topping up with boiling water to cover it. Keep boiling over medium heat for 1.5–2 hours, until chopsticks slide easily into the thickest part of the brisket.
5. Lift out the brisket, let it cool, and slice it thinly across the grain.
6. Strain the broth through a fine sieve into another pot and discard the bones. For a richer broth, first return the bones to the pot with fresh cold water, boil another 3–5 hours, strain, and combine with the first batch.
7. Remove the fat: divide the broth among several shallow containers, and once it has stopped steaming, refrigerate overnight; the next day lift off the white fat that has set on top. To eat it the same day, skim the fat off with a ladle. Refrigerate or freeze leftover broth.
8. To serve, bring the broth back to a boil.
9. Cook the noodles in another pot and drain.
10. Put noodles or rice in each bowl, lay brisket slices on top and fill with the boiling broth.
11. Put salt, pepper, green onion and a dish of [kkakdugi](/recipes/kkakdugi/) on the table for each person to add after tasting.

## Key points

- **After hours of cooking, the broth is still clear**: the heat was too low, so the broth only bubbled gently and the fat was never broken into fine droplets (see [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)). Turn the heat up and keep it at a rolling boil; when the water runs low, top up with boiling water, not cold.
- **Greyish broth with an off smell**: the bones were not soaked enough, or the bones and pot were not washed after blanching, so blood and scum went back into the broth (see [blanching and removing off-odours](/principles/blanching/)). Soak until the water turns pale, and after blanching rinse each bone and scrub the pot.
- **Brisket dry and stringy, or too tough to chew**: added too early, it falls apart; added too late, its connective tissue is still tough (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Add it once the broth is white and lift it out as soon as chopsticks pass through.
- **The whole pot is too salty**: salt went into the pot, and the long boil concentrated it. Leave salt to each person's bowl.
