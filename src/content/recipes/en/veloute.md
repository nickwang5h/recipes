*Velouté* means "velvety" in French: the sauce is milky white with a hint of ivory, smooth in the mouth, and it leaves a thin film on the back of a spoon. It is one of the five mother sauces Escoffier listed, and a close relative of [béchamel](/recipes/bechamel/): béchamel uses milk, velouté uses white stock.

Two things thicken it. When the flour in the roux meets hot liquid, its starch takes up water and swells (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)); the gelatin drawn out of the bones into the stock adds a further silky body (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Both Escoffier and a modern culinary textbook land at about 50 g butter and 50 g flour per litre of stock; this uses 600 ml of stock and makes about 400 ml of sauce.

It is rarely served on its own; it is the base for other sauces. Finish it with cream and it becomes *sauce suprême*; add egg yolks and cream and it becomes *sauce allemande*; the sauce in [blanquette de veau](/recipes/blanquette-de-veau/), made from the cooking liquid and a white roux, is also a velouté.

## Steps

1. Melt the butter in a small heavy-bottomed pan over medium-low heat until the foam turns fine. Add the flour and stir constantly with a wooden spoon or whisk for 2–3 minutes, until the paste bubbles finely, smells faintly of biscuits and turns from white to pale straw. Take it off the heat then; stop as soon as the roux starts to colour, because a brown roux makes a sauce of a different colour and taste.
2. Let the roux cool off the heat for 1–2 minutes. Pour in the cold stock in three additions, whisking each until there are no lumps before adding the next.
3. Return to medium heat and keep whisking across the bottom of the pan until the sauce boils and thickens noticeably. Add the mushrooms.
4. Turn the heat to low so only the odd bubble breaks the surface, and cook gently for 25–30 minutes. Stir the bottom of the pan every few minutes and skim off the foam and skin that collect at the edge. It is ready when it has reduced by about a third, no longer tastes of raw flour, and a finger drawn across the back of a coated spoon leaves a clean line.
5. Strain through a fine sieve into a clean pan or bowl and discard the mushrooms. Taste, and season with salt, white pepper and a little nutmeg.
6. If using right away, keep it warm over low heat, stirring now and then. To hold it, brush a thin layer of butter over the surface or press plastic wrap onto it so no skin forms; once cool, refrigerate, and reheat over low heat, stirring in a tablespoon or two of stock to bring back the consistency.

## Key points

- **Lumps of flour**: hot roux hit a large amount of hot stock, so the starch on the outside gelatinized at once and sealed dry flour inside small balls (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Let the roux cool a little, use cold stock, add it in stages and keep whisking; strain out any lumps that do form.
- **Tastes of raw flour, feels pasty**: it has not cooked long enough for the starch to gelatinize fully. Simmer another 10 minutes over low heat, stirring the bottom.
- **Too thin to coat a spoon**: the stock was weak or not reduced enough, so there is too little gelatin and starch (see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep reducing over low heat; if it is too thick, add a few spoonfuls of hot stock.
- **Grey or brownish colour**: the roux went too far, or the bottom of the pan scorched. Take the roux off the heat as soon as it colours, and scrape the bottom every few minutes during the slow simmer.
