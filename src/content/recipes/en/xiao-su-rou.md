The crisp shell of these pork strips comes from the batter losing water. Sweet potato starch first [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) when it meets water and heat, then releases steam as it fries, while egg white and flour help the outer layer set. The two fries do different jobs: the first cooks the pork, and the second makes the shell drier and crisper (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)).

## Steps

1. Put the ginger, green onion, Shaoxing wine and 80 g of water in a bowl and squeeze and knead by hand for about 5 minutes, until you smell a strong green onion and ginger aroma. Strain out the solids and keep the green onion and ginger water.
2. Remove the skin from the pork, pat dry and cut into strips 8–10 cm long and about 1.5 cm thick. Add the salt, thirteen spice, pepper, both kinds of Sichuan peppercorns and light soy sauce. Use either MSG or chicken bouillon powder, or neither; if you use a salted bouillon powder, start with less salt.
3. Add the green onion and ginger water to the pork in several additions, gripping the meat to mix after each one and adding the next only when no liquid pools at the bottom of the bowl. Knead for about 10 minutes, until the surface of the pork is tacky and the liquid is absorbed, then cover and refrigerate for 30 minutes.
4. Add the flour, sweet potato starch and the whites of 2 eggs and knead for about 15 minutes. Break up any coarse starch lumps so the batter is fully moistened. The kneaded strips should carry an even thin coat of batter, with no dry starch pockets and no thin liquid dripping off.
5. Add oil to a deep pot to a depth of at least 3 cm, leaving room below the rim. Heat the oil to 150°C (300°F), then lower the heat to hold it there. Add the strips one at a time, frying in batches, and gently separate them once the outer layer has set.
6. Fry for about 3–5 minutes for the first fry, until the shell is light yellow and feels set. Take the thickest strip and insert a thermometer into the centre; it is cooked at 71°C (160°F). If it hasn't reached that, keep frying; a hard shell doesn't count. Lift the cooked strips onto a wire rack to drain.
7. Skim the crumbs out of the oil and raise it to 180°C (350°F). Return the first-fried strips to the oil in batches. Fry until the shell is golden and the bubbles lessen, then lift out right away. Spread them out to drain and let the steam escape. They are crispest eaten hot.

## Key points

- **Batter falls off, dry starch lumps**: the starch wasn't dispersed and moistened, so it [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) unevenly when heated. Break up coarse lumps before coating, coat the strips in a thin batter, and avoid leaving loose water at the bottom of the bowl.
- **Golden outside, raw inside**: the centre of a thick strip heats slowly (see [frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Judge the first fry by the centre temperature, and leave the second fry only to crisp the shell. If you want to hold the strips in the fridge, still fry them through first, spread them out to cool, then refrigerate.
- **Greasy, soft as soon as plated**: too many strips in one batch dropped the oil temperature, and stacking them when they come out traps steam (see [oil temperature](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Let the oil recover between batches and drain the fried strips on a rack.
- **Saltiness covers the Sichuan peppercorn aroma**: salt, soy sauce and bouillon powder stack up (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Count the salt in a salted bouillon powder together with the salt, use only one savoury booster, and don't sprinkle salt on after frying.
