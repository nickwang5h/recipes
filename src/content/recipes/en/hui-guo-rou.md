Twice-cooked pork has glossy, slightly curled slices coated in the red oil of doubanjiang, rich without being greasy. The method is boil first, then stir-fry: boiling cooks the whole piece through, and stir-frying renders the thin slices and coats them in red oil. Use skin-on pork belly with garlic sprouts; green chili can be added or left out. Slice the pork about 2 mm thick, with fat and lean joined in each slice, so the edges curl as they are fried (see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/)).

The water on the surface of the slices has to be driven off first, so the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) can build its fragrance. The savoury depth of doubanjiang comes from [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/); in the pan it only needs to be fried until the red oil and fragrance come out, then the pork slices should catch it right away, because paste fried empty for long will burn.

## Steps

1. Clean any leftover hairs off the pork skin. Put the pork belly in a pot, cover with cold water, and add the knotted green onion, the ginger for boiling and the Shaoxing wine; if the piece is too large, cut it into long pieces first so it heats through.
2. Bring to a boil over high heat, skim, then turn to low and keep it at a gentle simmer, not a hard boil. After 15 minutes, start checking: insert a thermometer into the centre of the thickest part, and at 71°C (160°F) it is done, and a chopstick should slide into the lean meat fairly easily. Thick pieces often need longer.
3. Lift the pork out, cool it briefly in clean cold water, then take it out, drain it and blot the surface dry. Slice it about 2 mm thick, trying to have skin, fat and lean in every slice; if it will not hold steady for slicing, chill it first to firm it up.
4. Cut the green peppers into rings and the garlic sprouts into sections, keeping the white and leaf parts separate, and slice the remaining ginger thin. Chop the doubanjiang finely, and mix half of it with the light soy sauce and set aside.
5. Coat the wok with 1 tsp of oil, add the pork slices and stir-fry over medium heat. When the fat gradually turns translucent, the edges curl slightly and oil has collected at the bottom of the pan, spoon off the extra oil and leave only enough to coat the pan.
6. Turn the heat to low, push the pork to one side, pour in the doubanjiang and soy sauce mixture and the optional MSG, and stir-fry for about 15 seconds before tossing it back with the pork. Fry the paste only until the red oil comes out and you smell its fragrance; do not leave it to blacken on a dry, hot pan.
7. Add the green pepper, ginger slices and the remaining doubanjiang, and stir-fry for about 30 seconds. Add the garlic sprout whites first, then the leaves, stir-frying for about 60 seconds in total; take the pan off the heat when the whites are just cooked and the leaves have just softened and are still bright green.

## Key points

- **Slices thick, fat greasy and not curling**: the slices are cut too thick, so heat and rendering are slow (see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Cool the cooked pork until it is easy to cut, then slice it evenly thin.
- **Slices spit as soon as they hit the pan and will not turn fragrant**: the slices went in with water from cooling, and the steam evaporates violently. Wipe them dry before frying, leaving a dry surface for the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).
- **Paste bitter, garlic sprouts yellow and limp**: the doubanjiang was fried empty for too long, or the vegetables went in too early. A few quick stir-fries are enough for the [fermented seasoning](/principles/fermentation/) to coat the pork, and add the garlic sprouts in order, whites before leaves.
