*Chả trứng hấp* steams ground pork, glass noodles and egg together. It comes out soft but can be cut into whole pieces, and suits rice. Fish sauce seasons it, and shallots and cilantro add fragrance.

The egg's [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) binds the ground meat together, and the glass noodles soften as they absorb water, giving the cake a little springiness (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). The key is to spread it evenly in a shallow dish and give the centre enough time to heat (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Turning the heat up until the steam is steady is enough; a fierce fire only overcooks the outer ring and does not cook the centre any faster.

## Steps

1. Soak the glass noodles in 500 ml hot water for 5–10 minutes, until the whole bundle is soft and bends, then lift out and drain for 2 minutes. Snip into pieces no longer than 2 cm. Finely chop the shallot and cilantro.
2. Put the ground pork in a large bowl. Add the shallot, cilantro, fish sauce, pepper, sugar and 2 tsp of the oil, and stir with a fork for about 1 minute, until the pork is loosened and evenly seasoned. Add the glass noodles and 135 ml water, and mix for another 0.5 minutes, until the noodles and pork are evenly combined.
3. Beat the five eggs in a separate bowl and pour them into the pork mixture. Stir for about 1 minute, until there are no clumps of meat or separate streaks of egg white. Grease a heatproof shallow dish about 20 cm across with the remaining 1 tsp oil, pour in the egg and pork mixture, and spread it to a thickness of about 2–3 cm, with the noodles and pork evenly distributed.
4. Add 1500 ml water to the steamer, set the rack in place, and heat over high heat for about 6–8 minutes, until the water boils steadily. Loosely cover the dish with a sheet of aluminum foil, leaving a gap at the edge for steam to escape. Set it on the rack, leaving room between the rim of the dish and the pot wall for steam to pass.
5. Cover and turn the heat to medium, enough to keep the steam steady, and steam for 35–40 minutes, until the centre is fully set and no liquid egg moves when you gently shake the dish. A thermometer in the centre should read 74°C (165°F); if it does not, steam 3 minutes more at a time. Halfway through, check the water level in the pot so it does not boil dry.
6. Turn off the heat, uncover and remove the foil. Let it rest for 5 minutes, until less heat rises from the surface and the cut edges hold together. Run a knife around the edge of the dish, cut into pieces, and lift them onto a plate with a wide spatula.

## Key points

- **Outer ring is tough, centre is still wet**: the dish is too deep or the mixture too thick, so the centre heats slowly while the outer layer is already overset (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Spread it in a shallow dish, keep the steam passages open, and do not rush it with more heat.
- **Falls apart when cut**: the egg and pork mixture was not mixed evenly, or the glass noodles are too long and pull the pieces apart. Soak and snip the noodles first, and break the pork up thoroughly so that [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) can hold the filling together evenly.
- **Hard cores in the noodles, dry patches in the cake**: the noodles were under-soaked and keep drawing water from around them once in the dish (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Use them only once the whole bundle bends, and measure the water for the mixture carefully.
- **Weeps water and turns tough when reheated for lunch**: repeated heating tightens the protein (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Cut into small pieces before packing, divide into shallow containers, and refrigerate promptly. The next day, reheat under a loose lid, turning the container partway through, until the centre of the thickest piece reaches 74°C (165°F).
