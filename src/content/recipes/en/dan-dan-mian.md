Dan dan noodles are a Sichuan street-food noodle dish. The bottom of the bowl holds a red chili oil sauce, topped with thin noodles and ya cai pork, and you mix it all up from the bottom as you eat. Toasted sesame paste carries the chili oil and the savoury flavour, so the sauce coats the noodles in a thin layer and the noodles stay loose, strand by strand.

The pork has to be stir-fried until its water is cooked off before it gets the dry fragrance of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). Ya cai adds salty savoury flavour to the topping. The Sichuan pepper and chili oil stay in the bowl sauce to keep the last of the peppercorn fragrance (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Prepare everything first, so the noodles can go into the bowls as soon as they are cooked.

## Steps

1. Mix the ground pork with 2 tsp of light soy sauce and the Shaoxing wine, and break it up with chopsticks. Chop the ya cai, ginger, garlic and green onion, and cut the thick stems of the bok choy. Stir the sesame paste so the oil on top is mixed with the paste settled at the bottom of the jar, then measure it.
2. Heat the vegetable oil in a wok over medium heat for about 1 minute, until the oil flows freely. Add the pork and stir-fry for 5–6 minutes, constantly breaking it up, until the pork is cooked through, the juices have cooked off and the edges are slightly brown, and the centre of the thickest lump reaches 71°C (160°F).
3. Add the minced ginger, ya cai and chili flakes, turn to medium-low heat and stir-fry for 1 minute, until the ya cai smells fragrant and the surface of the pork bits is dry. Take it out. Only a thin film of oil should be left in the wok, with no pooled liquid in the topping.
4. Put the sesame paste in a bowl and stir in the 1.5 tbsp of warm water in 3 additions, until the paste goes from heavy to smooth and runs slowly. Then add the garlic, 2 tbsp of light soy sauce, black vinegar, Sichuan pepper powder, chili oil and sugar, stir well and divide between the bottoms of two noodle bowls.
5. Bring the cooking water to a boil over high heat, which takes about 8–10 minutes, until it is at a full rolling boil. Add the thin noodles, separate them with chopsticks and cook for 4–6 minutes. Start checking at the time on the package, until there are no hard spots in the centre and the noodles are still springy to the bite. Put in the bok choy for the last 1 minute, and cook until the leaves are soft and the thick stems can be bitten through.
6. Lift out the noodles and vegetables, drain, and while still hot put them into the two bowls with the sauce. Divide the pork topping, crushed peanuts and green onion over the top. Turn the sauce up from the bottom of the bowl and mix until every noodle is glossy and there are no lumps of sesame paste, then eat right away.

## Key points

- **Topping is soft and wet with weak fragrance**: it came out of the wok before the water was cooked off, and wet ground pork can't brown (see [the Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Break up the pork first and stir-fry until no liquid pools at the bottom and the edges are coloured, then add the ya cai.
- **Sesame paste clumps and the noodles won't mix**: thick paste met the seasoning liquid directly, so only the outside dissolved and the inside stayed hard. Stir in warm water a little at a time to make a smooth, flowing paste first, then add the chili oil and the other seasonings.
- **Noodles stick in a lump and the sauce coats thickly**: the noodles were overcooked, or left standing after draining. Once the starch in the noodles has gelatinized they stick together easily (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Prepare the bowl sauce first, drain the noodles as soon as they are done and mix while hot.
