Ants climbing a tree is ground pork clinging to glass noodles. It looks like ants crawling up a tree, which is where the name comes from. Sweet potato glass noodles are chewier than fine mung bean ones. The noodles were already gelatinized when they were made (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)), so in the pan they mostly absorb water and soften again; do not cook them like raw starch.

The doubanjiang is fried in the oil first so the red oil and savoury aroma spread into the pork, as described in [Fermented Seasonings](/principles/fermentation/). A little sauce is left at the end because the noodles keep absorbing after they leave the pan, which keeps them loose when eaten. If the liquid cooks away completely, the smooth noodles stick into a clump.

## Steps

1. Soak the dry noodles in water for about 20 minutes, until they bend and separate gently without breaking, then drain. If thick noodles still have a hard core, keep soaking according to the package; do not snap them into short pieces.
2. Finely chop the garlic and ginger separately. Chop the doubanjiang until no large pieces of broad bean remain, and mince the optional green onion. Keep the seasonings and water within reach, so you can move straight on while the doubanjiang is frying.
3. Add 10 ml oil to the pan and stir-fry the garlic and ginger over medium-low heat until fragrant but still pale. Add the ground pork and break it up with the spatula.
4. Stir-fry until all the pork has changed colour, the bits separate and a little fat comes out, then add the doubanjiang and keep stirring. Keep the heat medium-low. It is enough for the oil to turn red and the sauce aroma to be clear; it does not need to go dark brown.
5. Add the dark soy sauce and part of the light soy sauce, mix, then pour in 300 ml water and bring to a boil. Taste, then decide whether to add the rest of the light soy sauce. Add white pepper if using.
6. Add the soaked, drained noodles and cook over medium-low heat for about 5 minutes. Gently loosen them with chopsticks and let them sit in the liquid until the centres have no hard core, the strands are soft and chewy, and the pork clings evenly between the noodles.
7. Stop when only a thin layer of liquid is left at the bottom of the pan, and sprinkle on the optional green onion. If the noodles are not yet soft and the liquid is low, add a little hot water and keep cooking. If the noodles are soft but there is too much liquid, briefly turn up the heat to reduce it. Plate and loosen the noodles while hot.

## Key points

- **Noodles clump and get drier as they sit**: the liquid was cooked down too far, and the noodles keep absorbing water after leaving the pan (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Leave a thin layer of sauce when you turn off the heat; do not cook the pan dry.
- **Noodles soft outside, hard inside**: the noodles differ in thickness or in how far they were soaked (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Soak until they bend, then check for a hard core while cooking, not just the 5 minutes.
- **Doubanjiang and garlic taste bitter**: the finely chopped seasonings stayed too long in hot oil (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Add the water as soon as you smell fragrance and the oil turns red.
