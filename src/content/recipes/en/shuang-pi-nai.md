Double-skin milk pudding is a milk dessert from Shunde, traditionally made with water buffalo milk, egg white and sugar. First let hot milk form a skin in the bowl, then pour out the milk underneath, mix it with egg white, pour it back and steam it until set; as it cools, a skin forms on the surface again. The skin stays on the finished dessert, and the milk custard under it is pure white, tender and soft, and holds its shape on a spoon.

The skin comes from water evaporating at the surface of the hot milk, so protein and fat gather there. The custard sets as the egg white connects when heated, forming a network that can hold up the milk (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). The skin and the custard want opposite temperatures: the milk must be fully hot before the skin forms, but cooled down before the egg white is mixed in. Steam with steady, gentle steam so the inside and outside of the bowl set gradually together (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

## Steps

1. Pour the water buffalo milk into a small pot and heat over low heat for about 5–6 minutes, stirring gently so it doesn't scorch, until fine bubbles form a ring at the edge and steam clearly rises from the surface. Take it off the heat just as it starts to move at a simmer. Divide it among 3 heatproof bowls about 9 cm across, about 80 ml each.
2. Leave the bowls uncovered and let them cool for about 15–20 minutes, until a complete, slightly wrinkled skin has formed on the surface and the milk in the bowl is no longer hot to the touch. Keep the bowls level, and don't shake them or break the surface with a spoon.
3. In each bowl, make a small cut in the skin near the edge and slowly pour the milk underneath into a large bowl. Leave about 1 tsp of milk in each small bowl to support the skin, so it doesn't dry and stick to the bottom of the bowl.
4. Lightly break up the egg whites with chopsticks, and stir them with the sugar and the poured-out warm milk for about 1–2 minutes, until the sugar has disappeared and the egg white is not in clumps. Pass it through a fine strainer once and skim off the foam on top. Don't whip it into a meringue.
5. Slowly pour the milk and egg mixture back along the side of the bowl through the original cut, dividing it among the 3 bowls, so the skin floats up. Cover each with a small plate. Put the water in the steamer and heat over high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until the water is boiling and there is plenty of steam, then put in the bowls.
6. Turn to medium-low heat and keep the water at a steady gentle simmer. Steam for about 8–10 minutes, then turn off the heat and leave covered for 10 minutes. Take the bowls out only when the surface of the custard has set, the centre jiggles lightly without any liquid milk flowing, and the centre reaches 74°C (165°F). If not, steam for another 2 minutes.
7. Remove the small plates and let the bowls cool flat for about 5 minutes, until a thin skin has formed on the surface again and the custard can be scooped up with a spoon. Serve warm.

## Key points

- **The skin sinks, tears or won't float**: the first layer had not set firmly, the milk was poured too fast, or too much milk was poured out of the bottom. Wait for a complete skin, cut the opening at the edge, and leave a little milk to support it; pour the mixture back slowly through the same opening (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **Coarse and firm, with holes, or water at the edge of the bowl**: too much air went in, or it was steamed too hard or too long. Only break up the egg white, strain the milk, cover the bowls with small plates, and take them out promptly once set (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **The surface has set but the centre is still liquid**: the bowl is too deep or the steam is too weak, so the centre lags behind in heat. Use small shallow bowls, put them in only when the water is boiling, and when extending the time add only 2 minutes at a time (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
