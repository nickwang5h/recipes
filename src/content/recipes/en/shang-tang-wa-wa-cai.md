Baby napa cabbage in superior broth cooks the leaves soft while the ribs keep a little bite, with a savoury broth you can spoon up and drink at the bottom of the plate. The broth is made from plain water, luncheon meat and oyster sauce, and its savouriness comes from how these ingredients combine in the liquid (see [umami](/principles/umami/)); the century egg goes on at the end and stays as soft, intact little pieces.

[Blanching](/principles/blanching/) first makes the leaves collapse, and then simmering in a small amount of broth makes it easier for the whole pot of cabbage to touch the liquid. The green onion, ginger and garlic are first fried in oil to release their aroma, and the broth then carries it onto the cabbage (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). The point of the finished dish is soft cabbage and a savoury broth; the broth only needs to stay fluid.

## Steps

1. Check the baby napa cabbage layer by layer and rinse it, cut it lengthwise into narrow wedges, and slice the thick ribs thinner. Cut the green onion into sections and the ginger and garlic into slices. Cut the luncheon meat and the peeled century egg into dice of about 1 cm and keep them apart.
2. If using enoki mushrooms, cut off the tight base, tear them into small bunches and wash. The enoki must be cooked through together with the cabbage.
3. Bring a pot of water to a boil, enough to submerge the cabbage, and add the cabbage in batches, blanching for about 10 seconds. Once the outer leaves have wilted a little, lift out and drain; the ribs are still hard at this point.
4. Add the vegetable oil to a pot and fry the green onion, ginger and garlic over low heat until fragrant and the edges of the garlic slices have just begun to change colour. Do not fry them to a deep brown, or the broth will taste bitter.
5. Add 300 ml of water along the edge of the pot, then add the cabbage, luncheon meat and the enoki, if using. Arrange the thick ribs facing down in the liquid, add the oyster sauce, sugar and MSG, if using, and bring to a boil.
6. Keep the broth at a gentle roll for about 3 minutes, turning the wedges midway so the upper layer touches the broth too. It is done when the leaves are soft and a chopstick goes through the ribs; if the thick ribs are still hard, keep cooking, add a little hot water if the liquid runs low, and add salt after tasting the broth.
7. Move the cabbage and other ingredients to a deep plate, scatter the century egg dice on top, pour on the hot broth and serve at once. The plate should hold a clear broth that flows, and the wedges should keep their shape when lifted.

## Key points

- **Leaves fall apart while the ribs are still hard**: the difference in thickness makes the heat reach them unevenly, and the ribs still need to be cooked after blanching (see [blanching](/principles/blanching/)). Slice thick ribs thinner and lay them facing down, and do not make up for it by boiling the whole pot longer.
- **Broth is so salty it covers the sweetness of the cabbage**: luncheon meat and oyster sauce already carry salt, and rich savouriness can also stack up as saltiness (see [umami](/principles/umami/)). Add salt last; if it is too salty, add a little hot water first, then taste a spoonful of broth together with cabbage.
- **Broth tastes scorched and bitter**: the garlic slices were fried too far and the burnt flavour went into the oil and broth (see [aroma extraction](/principles/aromatics/)). Add water as soon as it smells fragrant; if the garlic has already turned black, replace it and start again.
