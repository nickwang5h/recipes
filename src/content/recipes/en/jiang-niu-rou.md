Soy-braised beef is best made with beef shank that has tendon in it: the tendon turns soft and sticky, the meat stays firm, and when cold it slices into neat thin slices. During the low-heat braise, the [collagen in the connective tissue turns into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/) and the tendon slowly softens. When you turn off the heat, the pieces should still hold their shape. Bay leaf, cassia bark and star anise release their fragrance slowly in the braising liquid; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).

Make this dish a day ahead: soak, marinate, braise, then chill overnight. The actual hands-on time is only a small part. Chilling sets the pieces so they are easy to slice. Cut against the direction of the muscle fibres to shorten them; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/).

## Steps

1. Trim off any very thick membrane and visible impurities from the beef shank, put it in a container of clean water and soak in the fridge for 1 hour. Drain, then toss with the Shaoxing wine, Sichuan peppercorns and ginger slices, cover and marinate in the fridge for 4–6 hours, turning once halfway. Do not pierce the meat.
2. Cut the shank into pieces about 8 cm long, no longer than 10 cm, and as close in size as you can. Put them in a pot of cold water that covers the meat. Bring to a boil and skim off the foam, then boil for another 3 minutes. Lift the meat out and wash off the clinging foam with warm water. Discard the marinade.
3. Put the beef in a snug stewing pot and add about 2500 ml of clean water, adjusting so it just covers the meat. Add the bay leaf, green onion, cassia bark, rock sugar, star anise, light soy sauce, dark soy sauce and soybean paste. Bring to a boil, then turn to low heat at once.
4. Cover and keep the liquid at a gentle bubble. Stew for 90 minutes. Check the water level every 30 minutes; if it falls below 80% of the height of the meat, add hot water until it nearly covers. Turn over any pieces sticking out of the liquid, and do not let the bottom of the pot dry out.
5. Taste the braising liquid, add the salt, and stew for about another 90 minutes. Test the thickest piece with a fork: stop the heat when it goes in fairly easily, the tendon is soft and the meat is still whole when you lift it gently. Take out any pieces that are ready first, so they do not fall apart.
6. Put the pieces in a clean shallow container, spaced apart. Once the heavy steam has gone, refrigerate as soon as possible: cover loosely at first and seal once fully cold. Do not leave at room temperature for more than 2 hours, and do not wrap the scalding pieces tightly in a pile to wait for them to cool.
7. Chill for several hours, until the centre is cold through, ideally until the next day. Take out the meat you will eat at that meal, find the grain, and slice thin against it. Keep the remaining whole pieces refrigerated and eat them within 2–3 days; freeze what you cannot finish in time.

## Key points

- **Tendon is still hard but the meat has dried out**: the water boiled too hard or the pieces were too thick, so the tendon softened slowly while the meat lost moisture first (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep the heat low and the water level up, and check pieces separately instead of going by total time.
- **Slices crumble into shreds**: it was stewed until it fell apart, or was sliced before it was fully cold (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Stew until the tendon is soft and the pieces are still whole, chill completely, then slice with a sharp knife.
- **Thin slices are still hard to chew**: the knife ran along the long fibres (see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Cut across the grain; the muscle bundles in a shank can run in different directions, so separate them before slicing if needed.
