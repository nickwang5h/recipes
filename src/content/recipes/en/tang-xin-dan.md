In a soft-boiled egg the white holds its shape while the centre of the yolk still flows. Heat travels from the shell toward the centre, so the outer white warms first and the yolk at the centre follows more slowly (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)); different proteins also set in different ways (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). A short cook makes use of this gap between outside and inside.

Bring the water to a bare simmer before the egg goes in, and count the time from the moment the egg enters the water. Starting in cold water and then turning off the heat to soak depends on how fast your stove heats up, how much heat the pot holds and how much water there is, and the same six minutes can give very different results. For a large refrigerated egg, six minutes is a starting point; if you change to an extra-large egg or a room-temperature egg, or put in many eggs at once, check the doneness again.

## Steps

1. Choose eggs with intact shells and keep them refrigerated until they go into the pot. Add enough water to the pot to cover the eggs, and get the timer and a second bowl of cold water ready; several eggs should fit in a single layer, not stacked on each other.
2. Bring the water to steady bubbling, then adjust to a bare simmer. Small bubbles should keep rising to the surface with no violent rolling; use a spoon to hold the egg and lower it slowly to the bottom, so the shell doesn't crack.
3. Start timing when the egg goes into the water, and cook for 6 minutes at a bare simmer. The egg should stay under the hot water the whole time, and the water should stay at a bare simmer; don't let the eggs knock around in the pot.
4. When the time is up, lift the egg out immediately and move it into cold water to cool it down. Turn the egg so all sides touch the cold water, and change to fresh cold water when it turns warm; the cold water stops the heat in the outer layer from travelling further into the yolk.
5. When it is cool enough to hold, tap the shell gently and start peeling from the wider end. Lift away slowly along the boundary between the white and the inner membrane, and if needed work under clean water; don't dig in with a fingernail and break the soft white.
6. Cut it open to check: the white should be fully set, and the centre of the yolk thick and still slowly flowing. Eat right away; note the size of the egg, its starting temperature and the state of the centre this time, and adjust the time next time under the same conditions.

## Key points

- **White is still translucent and won't hold its shape**: the starting water temperature was low, the egg was too large, or putting in many eggs at once dropped the water temperature too much. [Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) isn't finished, so keep heating; next time put in fewer per pot and hold a bare simmer.
- **Yolk is completely hard**: it cooked too long, or wasn't cooled promptly after lifting out, so [residual heat](/principles/heat-transfer/) kept travelling in. Cool it as soon as the time is up, and shorten the cooking slightly next time under the same conditions.
- **Treating a runny yolk as safe**: [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) shows up as texture and does not guarantee sterilization. An undercooked yolk still carries a risk of Salmonella; young children, pregnant women, older adults and people with weaker immune systems should eat eggs with both white and yolk fully cooked.
