*Đồ chua* is the sweet and sour pickle of the Vietnamese table. This daikon and carrot version is often tucked into a [Bánh Mì Thịt](/recipes/banh-mi-thit/), and served with [Cơm Tấm Sườn Nướng](/recipes/com-tam-suon-nuong/). Daikon is the main vegetable, and carrot adds sweetness and colour. Take only a small pinch each time, whether tucked into a sandwich or tossed into rice noodles; the sourness makes rich meat and sauces taste fresher (see [acid](/principles/acid/)).

The daikon and carrot sticks are first rubbed with salt and a little sugar to draw out water, then soaked in the mixed vinegar brine. The vegetables lose some water and become bendable but keep a crisp bite (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). The sourness comes straight from the white vinegar. Once made, keep it in the refrigerator; after 1 hour you can taste the sweet and sour flavour.

## Steps

1. Cut into thick sticks. Peel the daikon and carrot and cut them into matchsticks about 5 cm long and 3 mm thick. Cut even slices first, then stack the slices and cut into strips. Wash a glass jar of about 1000 ml and its lid, and dry them.
2. Draw out the water. Put the sticks in a large bowl, add 1 tsp fine salt and 10 g sugar, and toss and rub by hand for about 3 minutes, until liquid collects in the bottom of the bowl and a daikon stick can bend until its two ends touch without breaking. The volume of the vegetables will shrink; if they snap when bent, rub for another 1 minute, but don't rub until the edges break apart.
3. Rinse off the salt and sugar. Tip the vegetables into a colander and rinse under cold water for about 30 seconds, until the surface no longer tastes clearly salty or sweet. Press lightly to remove extra water and drain for about 3 minutes, until no water drips when you lift the sticks, then pack them loosely into the jar, without pressing them too tight.
4. Mix the brine. Put 100 g sugar, 300 ml white vinegar and 240 ml lukewarm water in a bowl and stir for about 1–2 minutes, until no sugar grains are visible at the bottom.
5. Soak to flavour. Pour the brine into the jar and use a clean spoon to nudge the sticks so the liquid covers all the vegetables. Cover and refrigerate for at least 1 hour, until the daikon sticks taste sweet and sour from the outside to the centre and are still crisp when bitten. If the vegetables float, press them back below the liquid; if the container is so wide that the brine can't cover them, switch to a narrower jar.
6. Serve and store. Use clean chopsticks to lift out the amount you need, drain for about 1 minute, until it won't soak the bread or rice noodles, then serve. Keep the rest soaking in the brine in the refrigerator and use it within 4 weeks; the vegetables get softer over time, so use the crisper batch first.

## Key points

- **The brine quickly turns weak**: not enough water was drawn out, or too much water was carried in after rinsing (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Rub until the daikon bends without breaking, then press lightly after rinsing and drain thoroughly.
- **Thin sticks go limp with no crunch**: they were cut too thin and are easily damaged by rubbing (see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Cut matchsticks of even thickness and don't grate them into fine shreds.
- **Tastes clearly sweet but still very sour**: sugar only changes the balance of sweet and sour on the tongue, and the acid is still in the brine (see [acid](/principles/acid/)). Measure the vinegar, water and sugar in the same ratio, and taste with vegetables that have been drained of brine, served with the main dish.
