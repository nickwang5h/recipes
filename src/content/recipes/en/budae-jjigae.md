*Budae jjigae* cooks luncheon meat, hot dogs and kimchi in one shallow pot. It is a home-style hot pot that came out of post-war Korea. The finished pot has enough broth to move between the ingredients, while the meat, tofu and noodles each keep their shape, and it is eaten with white rice.

The fermented sourness of ripe kimchi goes with the fatty savour of the processed meat (see [Fermented Seasonings](/principles/fermentation/)). The luncheon meat, sausage, chili paste and soy sauce all carry their own salt, so measure the seasoning paste first, cook in a low-sodium stock, and judge the saltiness only at the end (see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/)). When noodles are heated, their [starch gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) and they keep absorbing broth; parboiling them separately and combining them at the end makes it easier to keep enough broth.

## Steps

1. Cut the kimchi into bite-sized pieces, the luncheon meat into 5 mm slices, the hot dogs on the diagonal, and the bacon into lengths. Slice the tofu, onion and shiitake, and cut the green onion into lengths; pull the bacon slices apart so they do not stack into thick clumps.
2. Measure out 30 ml from the chicken stock and mix it with the chili flakes, chili paste, soup soy sauce, garlic and black pepper into an even seasoning paste. Divide the rest of the stock into 720 ml for the base and 250 ml held back for topping up.
3. In a shallow pot about 26 cm wide, lay the onion, kimchi and bacon, and arrange the luncheon meat, hot dogs, tofu and shiitake around the edges. Put the seasoning paste in the middle and pour in 720 ml of the chicken stock so the bottom layer sits in liquid.
4. Heat over medium-high heat for about 10 minutes, until the centre of the pot is also bubbling steadily. Use a spoon to loosen the seasoning paste gently, turn to medium heat and cook for 10–12 minutes, until the thick stems of the kimchi are soft, the thickest part of the bacon reaches 71°C (160°F), and the centre of the tofu is hot.
5. If using instant noodles, boil a separate pot of water while the pot ingredients cook, which takes about 5 minutes to reach a boil. Add the noodle blocks and parboil for 2 minutes, until the noodles can just be pulled apart and the centre is still firm, then lift out at once and drain.
6. Put the green onion and noodles into the pot and cook over medium heat for 2–3 minutes, until the white parts of the green onion are soft and the noodles have no hard core when bitten. If the broth has fallen below the bottom layer of ingredients, top it up with 100 ml at a time from the held-back stock; at the end you can add the last 50 ml, so there is always broth in the bottom of the pot.
7. If adding cheese, lay it on the surface and cook over medium heat for 1 more minute, until the cheese softens, clings to the noodles and melts at the edges into the broth. Ladle out some broth, let it cool and taste it, and add salt only if it is still bland. Serve in the pot, giving out the noodles first, then the meat, vegetables and broth.

## Key points

- **The broth is both salty and strong**: the salt from the processed meat and the sauces adds up, and boiling evaporates water. Use a low-sodium stock, leave out the seasoning packet from the noodles, and add salt only at the end (see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/)).
- **The noodles are mushy and there is almost no broth**: the noodles went in too early and kept absorbing water. Parboil them in a separate pot, combine them with the pot, and serve as soon as they are done; use the held-back stock to put the liquid back (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)).
- **Only the saltiness of the meat, with very little kimchi flavour**: the kimchi was not yet sour and ripe, or its juice was washed off. Choose napa cabbage kimchi that is already clearly sour, and keep the fermented juice that clings to it (see [Fermented Seasonings](/principles/fermentation/)).
