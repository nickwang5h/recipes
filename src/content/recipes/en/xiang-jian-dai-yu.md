Pan-fried belt fish is seasoned with salt, ginger and Shaoxing wine and goes straight into the pan, giving fine, soft flesh and a golden surface. This is a common home-style dish in Zhejiang. Belt fish is flat and thin, so salt gets in easily; curing it in the fridge and then drying the surface spreads the saltiness evenly and sets up the frying; see [salt](/principles/salt/).

The surface moisture has to go before the browning and aroma of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) can happen when frying fish. Leaving space in the pan and waiting for the fish to set before turning protects the pieces better than moving them around. The finished dish keeps the taste of the fish with the salt and ginger, and goes well with rice or congee.

## Steps

1. Start with thawed belt fish sections. Trim off the dorsal and belly fins, clear out the black membrane inside the belly and the blood along the bone, and rinse gently. Cut into sections about 5 cm long and blot off the water with paper towels. Do not scrape hard at the silvery skin, which would tear it.
2. Sprinkle the salt evenly over both sides of the fish, add the Shaoxing wine and ginger shreds and toss. Spread the pieces in a single layer on a shallow plate and cure in the fridge for 3 hours, until the flesh firms slightly and the surface gradually dries. Keep the plate in the raw-food area, away from ready-to-eat foods.
3. Pick out the ginger and pour off the liquid on the plate. Press each piece dry with paper towels, especially the belly cavity and the cut ends, until the paper shows no clear wet marks, then fry. Wet ginger left in the pan scorches first, and the curing liquid should not go into the hot oil.
4. Preheat a heavy flat-bottomed pan over medium heat for about 2 minutes, until the 0.5 tbsp of oil you add spreads quickly and shimmers slightly. Add half the fish, leaving about 2 cm of space between pieces and keeping them in a single layer.
5. Fry the first side over medium heat for about 5–6 minutes, until the edges are golden and the fish moves when a spatula is pushed gently underneath. Do not turn it yet; if the bottom still sticks, keep frying about 1 more minute and try again. When dark brown spots appear, turn the heat down.
6. Support each whole piece with a thin spatula to turn it, then fry for about 5–6 minutes more, until both sides are golden, the flesh at the thickest part is opaque, and chopsticks easily lift it off the central bone. Thick pieces need a little longer; take them out only when cooked through.
7. Wipe burnt bits out of the pan with paper towels held in tongs, add the remaining 0.5 tbsp of oil, and fry the second batch the same way for about 10–12 minutes, until both sides are golden and the flesh comes off the bone. Lay the pieces flat on the plate so they do not crush each other.

## Key points

- **Skin sticks and the fish breaks when turned**: the surface was not dried, or the fish was moved before the bottom set. Allow time for the water to evaporate and for [surface browning](/principles/maillard-reaction/), then lift each whole piece with a thin spatula.
- **Dark brown outside but the flesh by the bone is still translucent**: the heat is too high, so the surface and centre are not heating in step (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Turn the heat down and keep frying, and judge by the colour and bone release at the thickest point; do not turn the heat up to rush it.
- **Fish is dry and hard in a lunch box**: cooking and reheating added up to too much heat. Before boxing, go by the flesh being just cooked through, without aiming for a hard, crisp crust. Put the pieces in a shallow box in a single layer, refrigerate promptly once they stop steaming, within 2 hours of leaving the pan, and cover tightly once fully cold. The next day, reheat covered with a vent until the centre reaches 74°C (165°F); watch for fine bones when eating bone-in.
