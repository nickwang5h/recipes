*Purée de pommes de terre* is a basic potato side in French cooking. Cooked potatoes are pressed fine, butter is mixed in first, and then hot milk loosens the mash. The smoothness comes from finely broken potato tissue, butter and the right amount of liquid; spooned onto a plate it should pile up softly, and a fork drawn across leaves shallow lines.

When potatoes cook, the starch in the cells takes up water and [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), and the tissue softens with it. A potato ricer breaks cooked potato into fine particles, but a high-speed blade ruptures many more cells and lets a lot of sticky starch out, so the mash easily turns gluey. Yukon Gold, common in Canada, suits a smooth mash; choose potatoes of the same variety and the same size to cook together.

## Steps

1. Cut the peeled potatoes into large pieces of similar size. Bring enough water to cover the potatoes to a boil, add salt, put in the potatoes, and once it returns to a boil keep a steady boil.
2. Cook for about 15–20 minutes, then check the largest piece: a knife tip should pass through the centre and a light press should split it, with no hard core. When it reaches this state lift the potatoes out and drain; do not keep cooking until the corners fall apart in the water.
3. While hot, press the potatoes through a ricer into a dry pot, or put them through a hand-cranked food mill. If you have neither, mash carefully with a hand masher, then press through a sieve with the back of a spoon; do not use a blender or an immersion blender.
4. Set the pot over medium-low heat and stir with a wooden spoon to let the extra steam escape. As soon as the mash gathers together and a thin film has just formed on the bottom of the pot, take it off the heat; do not let it brown or form a crust.
5. Mix in the soft butter in several batches, mixing each in evenly before the next. Heat the milk in another pot until just boiling, then add it to the mash in small amounts several times; wait for each to be absorbed before continuing, and slow down as it nears the thickness you want.
6. Season with salt and white pepper and fold in gently. Lift the wooden spoon: the mash should fall softly, with no dry lumps and no loose milk; you need not add all the milk. If it has cooled a little, warm it gently until hot through, avoiding a boil, then spoon onto warm plates and eat at once.

## Key points

- **Sticky and stringy, like paste**: high-speed beating ruptured too many cells, and the released starch made the mash gummy. Switch to a ricer and stop as soon as it is mixed (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)).
- **Hard bits in the mash**: the centre of the potatoes was not cooked through, or the potatoes had cooled before pressing. Cook until the centre of the largest piece is soft, and press while hot.
- **So thin it spreads out, or milk seeps around the edge**: the potatoes were not drained well, or too much milk went in at once. Let the steam escape before adding butter, add hot milk in batches, and stop when it is soft and piles up; once the dairy is in, do not rely on hard boiling to dry it out.
