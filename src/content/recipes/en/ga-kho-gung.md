*Gà kho gừng* is a Vietnamese everyday ginger braised chicken to eat with rice. The legs are skin-on and bone-in, the ginger is cut into strands, fish sauce gives savouriness, and caramel gives the red-brown colour and a toasty aroma. The sugar first goes through [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/) in the pot. Then the chicken and water go in, which carries the colour into the braising liquid.

The ginger, garlic and shallot go into the pot with the chicken, and their aroma is carried by the fat that renders from the skin (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Once the chicken is cooked, it keeps braising gently so that the meat near the bone and the skin turn soft. At the end the sauce is reduced until it thinly coats the pieces, still leaving some thick sauce to spoon over rice.

## Steps

1. Marinate the chicken. Pat the pieces dry with paper towels. Mix them with the ginger, garlic, shallot, salt, the 25 g of sugar for marinating, the optional bouillon powder, fish sauce and pepper. Cover and refrigerate for 30 minutes, until the seasonings coat the pieces evenly. If the legs haven't been cut into pieces, ask the meat counter to do it, and avoid hacking the leg bones at home.
2. Make the caramel. Put the oil and the other 25 g of sugar in a heavy-bottomed pot about 24 cm wide. Heat over medium-low for 3–5 minutes, swirling the pot gently, until the sugar melts into a deep amber and smells of caramel. As it nears deep amber, move the pot off the burner, so the leftover heat doesn't burn the sugar black.
3. Coat with caramel. Tip the chicken, ginger and all the marinade into the pot, put it back over medium-high heat and stir-fry for about 5 minutes, until the skin tightens, the surfaces of the pieces are no longer pink and the caramel clings evenly.
4. Add water and braise. Pour in 360 ml of hot water and cook over medium heat for about 3 minutes, until the pot returns to a boil. Skim off the foam and excess floating fat. Turn to medium-low and cook uncovered for 30 minutes, until the liquid has reduced by about a third and the meat near the bone can be pulled apart with chopsticks. Insert a thermometer into the thickest piece, away from the bone. At 74°C (165°F) the chicken is done; if not, cook 5 more minutes and measure again.
5. Thicken the sauce. If the chicken is already tender but the sauce is still thin, take the pieces out first, reduce the sauce over medium heat for 3–5 minutes until it thinly coats the back of a spoon, then return the chicken and toss for 1 minute, until every piece is shiny. If the sauce has already reached this state when the braise is done, simply turn off the heat and serve.

## Key points

- **The whole pot is black and tastes burnt and bitter**: the caramel went too far; see [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/). Take the pot off the heat and add the chicken when the sugar is deep amber. If it gives off a sharp, acrid smoke, throw it out and start again.
- **The breast is dry while the leg meat isn't tender yet**: breast cooks fast and its water is squeezed out as it cooks on, while leg meat needs longer braising to soften (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). This dish uses legs. Once the chicken is cooked and tender, take it out and reduce the sauce on its own.
- **The sauce is so salty it turns bitter**: as water evaporates, the fish sauce and salt become more concentrated (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Stop the long cooking once the meat is tender, and keep a sauce that still flows. Don't reduce it until only oil is left in the pot.
- **The ginger is tough and coarse to chew**: it was cut too thick or from old, fibrous ginger, and its aroma is released slowly (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Cut thin slices against the ginger's fibres, then cut them into fine strips, and braise them with the chicken.
