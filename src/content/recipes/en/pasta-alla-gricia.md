*Pasta alla gricia* is the Lazio pasta of cured pork jowl and sheep's milk cheese. The noodles are coated in a thin, glossy sauce, with pork flavour, the savouriness of the cheese and the sharpness of black pepper arriving together. The pork renders its fat first, the pasta goes into the pan with some water, and the sheep's milk cheese and black pepper are stirred in at the end. Around Amatrice a semi-aged sheep's milk cheese is used; it is hard to find in Canada, so you can substitute the saltier, harder Pecorino Romano.

The sauce depends on [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/): tossing breaks the fat into small droplets, the starchy pasta cooking water helps keep them suspended in the water, and the cheese adds body and flavour. If the pan is too hot, the proteins in the cheese clump together (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). So use heat to cook the pork, and turn the heat off to stir in the cheese, so the sauce only thinly coats the pasta.

## Steps

1. Cut the skinned pork jowl into strips of even thickness and grate the cheese into fine crumbs. Each strip should have both fat and lean. Discard the hard rind you trim off.
2. Put the pork jowl in a skillet and cook over medium-low heat for about 10 minutes, turning occasionally. When the fat is translucent and the edges are golden and rendered fat has collected in the pan, turn off the heat. Do not wait until the lean meat turns dark brown and hard.
3. Put the spaghetti into lightly salted boiling water and cook for about 9–10 minutes, until it still has bite in the centre and a cut strand shows no dry, white, hard core. Before draining, set aside a bowl of the cooking water.
4. Put the pasta in the pan with the pork, add a little pasta cooking water, and heat and toss briefly. There should be a flowing layer of liquid in the pan, and the oil should no longer all float on top. If the liquid is nearly gone, add a little more water.
5. Take the pan off the heat and wait until the vigorous bubbling stops. Sprinkle in the cheese in batches, tossing constantly, and add the next batch only once the previous one has dispersed. If the sauce is too thick, add pasta cooking water a spoonful at a time, and toss until every strand is coated in a glossy, thin sauce.
6. Grind in black pepper and divide immediately between two plates. Taste before deciding whether to add salt, since the sauce will thicken slowly once it leaves the pan.

## Key points

- **The cheese forms a sticky clump**: the pan is too hot, so the proteins bunch together, and there is too little water to keep them apart (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Take the pan off the heat before adding the cheese. If it starts to form grains, stir in a little warm water to loosen it; once it has pulled into a tough clump it is hard to rescue.
- **Oil pools under the pasta and it tastes greasy**: there is too little water, so the oil droplets were not broken up (see [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)). Add pasta cooking water and keep tossing until the oil and water become one coating sauce on the pasta.
- **The dish is too salty**: the salt from the cured pork and the sheep's milk cheese adds up, and reducing the sauce concentrates the salt further (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Salt the pasta water only lightly, especially when using Pecorino Romano, and taste at the end.
