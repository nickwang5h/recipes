Simmered kabocha (*kabocha no nimono*) is skin-on kabocha cooked into small floury pieces in a lightly sweet, soy-flavoured dashi. The green skin stays on the outside, the orange flesh holds together when picked up, and it bites like a cooked chestnut. It is a side dish for white rice, and the liquid only needs to carry out the squash's own sweetness.

Kabocha is starchy, and when it absorbs water and heats, its starch [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), which is what makes the flesh floury and soft. Once it is soft, continued boiling or stirring with a spatula makes the edges fall away easily. Laying the pieces skin-side down in a single layer and using a drop lid to circulate the liquid heats the pieces gently (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Shaving off the sharp corners keeps the edges from breaking up first.

## Steps

1. Wash the squash skin, scoop out the seeds and stringy pulp, and weigh out 450 g. Set a flat cut face on the cutting board, cut into wedges first, then into pieces about 4–5 cm long and of similar thickness; shave off sharp corners and a narrow strip of hard skin at the edges, leaving most of the green skin.
2. Choose a shallow pot in which the squash fits snugly in one layer, and place the pieces skin-side down. Pour in 400 ml of unsalted [dashi](/recipes/dashi/), add the sake and sugar, and swirl the pot gently to dissolve the sugar; the liquid should come to about two thirds of the height of the pieces.
3. Heat over medium heat for about 4 minutes, until it bubbles steadily at the edge of the pot and the sugar has completely dissolved, then add 2 tsp of soy sauce. Taste a small spoonful of the liquid and add salt if it needs more; it should be slightly sweet with a clear dashi flavour, and the soy sauce should not cover the squash's aroma.
4. Set a drop lid, slightly smaller than the pot opening, on top, or a round of parchment paper with a hole in the middle, so that it rests on the pieces. Turn to low heat and cook for 10 minutes, until the edges of the pieces begin to soften and the liquid is still bubbling slowly.
5. Check the amount of liquid, and if it is dropping quickly, put the outer lid on to reduce evaporation. Keep cooking over low heat for 10–20 minutes, until a bamboo skewer slides easily into the centre of the thickest piece, fine cracks show near the skin, and the pieces can still be picked up whole. Don't turn them with a spatula at any point, and don't reduce the liquid to a syrup.
6. Turn off the heat and move the pot off the burner, leave the drop lid in place and put on the outer lid, and rest for 30 minutes, until it no longer gives off much steam and the surface of the pieces is evenly coated with the liquid. Lift the pieces from the skin side with a wide spoon onto a plate with a little of the liquid, and serve warm.

## Key points

- **Squash falls apart into mash**: the flesh is already soft all the way through and is still boiling or being moved often. Shave the corners, keep the skin as a support, stop the heat as soon as a skewer goes through, and lift from underneath when plating. See [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **Soft outside but the centre near the skin is still hard**: the pieces are too thick, or vary too much in thickness. Cut to similar thickness, judge by the thickest piece, and keep cooking over low heat until the centre is soft all the way through. See [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **Squash very salty, with a layer of thick liquid on the bottom**: the pot is too wide or the heat too high, so too much water evaporated before the squash was soft. Pack the pieces in one tight layer, put on the outer lid if the liquid level drops too fast, and stop reducing once the saltiness is enough. See [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/).
