*Kai jeow* is beaten egg with fish sauce, fried in hot oil into a golden omelette. The fish sauce brings saltiness and savoury depth at once, and the edges are puffy and crisp while the centre is cooked through and soft. In Thailand it is often eaten with white rice and soup, and beyond breakfast it can turn up in an everyday meal.

When the egg hits the pan, its water quickly turns to steam and the edges puff up; the proteins then set and fix the shape (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Oil covering the bottom of the pan carries heat evenly to the surface of the egg (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). The home method uses a small flat pan, where the thin edges colour quickly and the centre still needs a flip to heat through. To keep it dry, take it out of the pan as soon as it is done and drain off the surface oil (see [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)).

## Steps

1. Crack two large eggs into a bowl and add 1 tsp of fish sauce. Beat quickly with a fork for about 30–60 seconds, until the white and yolk are fully mixed, there are no stringy clear strands of white in the bowl, and fine bubbles appear on the surface.
2. Pour 3 tbsp of oil into a flat-bottomed pan about 20 cm wide and swirl the pan to spread the oil across the bottom. Heat over medium-high heat for 1–2 minutes, until the oil flows easily and shows fine ripples. Drop in a tiny bit of egg to test; it should bubble and puff up immediately. If it just spreads quietly, heat 30 seconds longer and try again.
3. Pour the egg into the pan in one go, keeping the rim of the bowl close to the pan to avoid splashing the oil. Fry for about 30 seconds first, until the outer ring puffs up and the bottom starts to set, then use a spatula to push the set edges gently toward the middle.
4. Tilt the pan slightly so the raw egg on top flows to the exposed bottom of the pan. Keep frying for about 1 minute, until most of the egg has set, the edges are golden, and the spatula can lift the whole omelette from underneath. Push only the edges; don't scramble the whole thing into pieces.
5. Flip with a wide spatula and fry another 15–30 seconds, until the other side is set with no liquid egg, and the centre is done at 74°C (165°F). If it isn't there yet, turn to medium heat and fry in 15-second increments so the edges don't burn first.
6. Lift the omelette and let the oil drain back into the pan, then put it on a plate lined with paper towels to drain for 30 seconds, until there are no obvious beads of oil on the surface. Move it to a serving plate and serve while the edges are still crisp and the centre still soft.

## Key points

- **Egg spreads into a thin sheet with limp edges**: the oil is not hot enough, or the pan is too big. Confirm with a drop of egg that it bubbles immediately, and use a small pan for two eggs. See [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/).
- **Burnt bottom, wet top**: the egg collects in the middle and the bottom keeps heating. Push the set edges in and let the raw egg flow to the pan surface. See [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **Hard centre and a burnt, bitter egg flavour**: it was heated for a long time after it had set. Lift it from the pan once it reaches doneness, because residual heat will keep tightening the egg. See [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
