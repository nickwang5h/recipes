Blanched choy sum has crisp stems and tender leaves, dressed with a little savoury sauce with garlic and a touch of heat, so what you taste is the clean sweetness of the vegetable. The method is to cook the vegetable in boiling water, drain it onto a plate and then pour over the sauce, without leaving the cooked vegetable soaking in hot water; see [blanching and removing off-odours](/principles/blanching/). The sauce here has garlic and bird's eye chili, with a little sweetness and heat inside the savoury flavour.

The stems are thicker than the leaves, so the stems go into the boiling water first and the leaves after, so the two parts finish at nearly the same time; see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/). Light soy sauce and oyster sauce are themselves salty, so the salt in the blanching water and the sauce are used separately and no salt is added to the sauce, so the leaves are not coated in a salty dressing.

## Steps

1. Wash the choy sum and cut off the tough root ends. If the outer layer of the thick stems has a tough skin, peel it off thinly, and split especially thick stems lengthwise, keeping the leaves whole; get the minced garlic and chopped chili ready.
2. Stir the light soy sauce, oyster sauce, sugar and water together into a sauce, stirring until the sugar dissolves. Prepare the salt and oil for blanching separately; the salt goes only into the blanching water.
3. Put the blanching water, salt and oil in a pot and bring to a boil over high heat. There must be enough water to submerge the leaves later; if the pot is small, blanch in batches, and do not crowd the choy sum into a clump soaking in lukewarm water.
4. Hold the choy sum by the leafy end with tongs and put the root and stem into the boiling water first for about 1 minute. When the colour of the stems deepens and they begin to soften, lower in the whole plant and blanch for about 1 minute. Thin stems with small leaves can be lifted out sooner. It is done when the thickest stems have no hard core and the leaves have only just cooked.
5. Lift out the choy sum, drain it well and arrange it on a plate. If blanching water collects at the bottom of the plate, pour it off, so the hot water does not keep softening the leaves and does not dilute the sauce.
6. In a small pot, bring the sauce to a boil over low heat, add half the garlic and chili, and cook until fragrant, reducing slightly. Take it off the heat and stir in the remaining garlic, then pour a thin layer over the choy sum; keep the extra sauce for dipping and do not pour it all onto the plate.

## Key points

- **The leaves are mushy and the stems are still hard**: the whole plant went into the water at once, and stems and leaves do not cook at the same speed (see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Put the thick stems in first, then blanch the leaves briefly, and cut off the tough root.
- **The colour is dull and the texture has collapsed**: the vegetable went in before the water boiled, or it stayed in the hot water after being lifted out (see [blanching and removing off-odours](/principles/blanching/)). Keep the water boiling, blanch in batches, and drain and plate right after blanching.
- **The leaves are too salty and there is a lot of liquid on the plate**: the blanching salt was added to the sauce, or all of the sauce was poured onto the plate (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Throw away the blanching water when you are done, and let the sauce coat the vegetable in only a thin layer.
