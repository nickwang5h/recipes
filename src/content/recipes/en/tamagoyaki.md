Tamagoyaki is a rolled omelette made by cooking a thin layer of seasoned egg, rolling it up, and wrapping it in the next layer. This version is the classic **dashimaki tamago**: with kombu and bonito dashi added it is savoury, soft and lightly sweet. The dashi dilutes the egg, so the coagulated protein network is looser and holds more water (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).

The thickness of the roll comes from building up layers, each of which is poured into the pan very thin. A thin layer sets quickly and sticks to the next layer while its surface is still moist; if each layer is cooked until dry and firm, the cut face tends to fall apart. A rectangular tamagoyaki pan works best, and a wide spatula holds the first roll more easily than chopsticks alone. Adjust the heat to how much heat the pan holds (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

## Steps

1. Stir together the cooled dashi, salt, mirin and usukuchi soy sauce. Separately, break up the eggs gently and mix them into the seasoned dashi only until the mixture pours evenly, without beating it into foam. Divide the mixture roughly into three portions.
2. Preheat a rectangular tamagoyaki pan over medium heat. Hold a folded paper towel dipped in oil with tongs and wipe a thin layer of oil. Test with a drop of egg: it should sizzle gently and start to set; if it browns immediately, take the pan off the heat to cool it first.
3. Pour in the first portion and tilt the pan so it covers the bottom; pop any large bubbles. When the bottom layer can be lifted and the surface is still moist, fold and roll it with a spatula from one end to the other to make the core of the roll.
4. Push the roll to one end of the pan and wipe the freed-up pan bottom lightly with oil. Pour in the second portion, lifting the edge of the roll so the new egg runs underneath; when the bottom layer can be lifted, roll the original roll back over the new sheet of egg.
5. Wipe with oil, pour in the third portion and roll in the same way. Turn to low heat and turn the whole roll gently so every side heats evenly; insert a thermometer into the centre and remove from the pan at 74°C (165°F). If the outer layer browns too quickly, take it off the heat briefly and continue.
6. While hot, shape it gently into a long bar with a clean bamboo mat and let it rest for about 1 minute, without pressing hard. Cut into thick slices and serve with the drained grated daikon.

## Key points

- **The egg sheet tears as soon as you roll it**: it was poured too thick, or the bottom layer couldn't support the egg yet. Wait until the bottom has set, then support it with a wide spatula; it's fine if the first layer is folded untidily, since the later thin layers will wrap it. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **Burnt outside, still wet and cold in the centre**: the pan is too hot and the outer layer hardened first. Keep the layers thin, turn to low heat toward the end and turn the whole roll, and check the centre with a thermometer. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **Layers separate when cut, and it tastes dry**: every layer was cooked until the surface was completely dry before rolling. Roll while the surface is still moist, and take it out once the whole roll is cooked through; don't press hard on the bamboo mat to stick dry egg sheets back together.
