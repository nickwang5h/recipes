Ginger pork (*buta no shogayaki*) is a staple of Japanese home cooking and set meals. Thin slices of pork loin with a little fat on the edge are pan-fried, then coated in a sweet-salty sauce with ginger, and a pile of raw shredded cabbage goes beside them. The ginger juice disperses into the sauce, and a little grated ginger left in gives a sharper spice; the sauce brushes over the meat and a little still pools at the bottom of the plate to toss with the cabbage.

Thin slices shorten the distance heat has to travel to the centre, and even slicing lets everything cook at the same time (see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Spread the meat out and fry it first, because the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) only happens when the surface is fairly dry. The ginger juice goes in last with the wet sauce, so the grated ginger does not burn on the dry hot bottom of the pan (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). The meat is already very thin, so reducing the sauce takes only a short cook.

## Steps

1. Cut the cabbage into fine shreds, wash and shake dry, and divide between two plates. Grate out 1 tbsp of juicy onion purée and slice the rest thin. Grate the ginger and squeeze out 1 tbsp of ginger juice, divided into two portions, and measure out 2 tsp of grated ginger separately.
2. Toss half the ginger juice and 1 tbsp of the sake with the pork, and let it stand in the refrigerator for 5 minutes, until every slice is moistened with marinade. Drain lightly before it goes into the pan; the slices should come apart from each other and carry no pool of liquid.
3. Stir the soy sauce, mirin, the remaining 2 tbsp sake, sugar, the other half of the ginger juice, 2 tsp grated ginger and the onion purée together, until there are no clumps of sugar at the bottom of the bowl.
4. Preheat a frying pan over medium-high heat for 1–2 minutes, until the oil added to it flows into a thin layer. Use half the oil first, and spread the pork in a single layer in two batches. Fry the first side of each batch for about 1 minute, until the edges turn white and the underside has a few golden spots, then flip and fry for 30–60 seconds more, until both sides have changed colour, and set aside for the moment.
5. Turn to medium heat, add the remaining oil and the sliced onion, and stir-fry for 6–8 minutes, until the onion is translucent and soft with lightly golden edges. The brown meat juices left on the bottom of the pan can be carried into the onion, but wipe out any burnt residue first.
6. Return the pork and the juices on its plate to the pan, stir the ginger sauce again and pour it in. Cook over medium heat for about 2 minutes, turning the slices gently and spooning the sauce over the surface, until the liquid is glossy and thinly coats the meat. Insert a thermometer into the thickest slice; it is cooked at 71°C (160°F), and if not, cook 30 seconds longer each time.
7. Serve the pork and onion at once beside the cabbage, and divide the sauce from the pan over the meat. The sauce should still flow, and the shreds stay crisp, to be tossed as you eat.

## Key points

- **The slices curl and heat unevenly**: the slices vary in thickness, or the connective tissue at the edge shrinks. Cut evenly across the grain, and make shallow cuts at obvious seams of fat, lean and connective tissue (see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/)).
- **Liquid pools in the pan and the meat has no fried flavour**: the slices are piled up or too much marinade went in. Drain off the loose liquid and fry in a single layer in batches (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).
- **The ginger tastes bitter and the sauce is too dry**: the grated ginger was fried too long in a dry pan, or the sauce was reduced too long. Put the ginger into the wet sauce and take the pan off the heat once the sauce coats the meat (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
