*Gỏi cuốn* are Vietnamese fresh rice paper rolls, classically filled with cooked shrimp, pork, rice noodles, lettuce and herbs. The wrapper is thin and translucent, and the fillings are each cooked separately, then rolled and eaten as they are. The starch in rice paper was already heated and set when it was made (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)); when rolling, it only needs to take up water again so the dry sheet turns pliable.

Rice paper keeps softening after it leaves the water. If you soak it until it collapses completely before lifting it out, it sticks or tears while you roll. Slice the pork thin and strip the tough stems from the leaves, so the fillings bend and sit snugly (see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). The dipping sauce is built on hoisin and chicken liver, with chopped peanuts added at the end for nutty flavour and texture.

## Steps

1. Cook the shrimp and pork. Cook the shrimp in salted water for about 90–150 seconds, until opaque in the centre, then lift out and let cool. Cook the pork separately in gently simmering water until the centre reaches 71°C (160°F), then cover and let cool.
2. Prepare the fillings. Halve the shrimp lengthwise into two pieces and remove any remaining vein. Slice the pork against the grain about 3 mm thick, then cut into narrow strips. Remove the tough ribs from the lettuce, and wash and drain the herbs and chives.
3. Cook the noodles. Follow the package directions (dried fine rice noodles usually take about 3–5 minutes) until soft with no hard core, rinse with cold water and drain well. Divide the pork, shrimp, noodles and lettuce into 16 portions each, so you do not have to hunt for ingredients while rolling.
4. Make the dipping sauce. Blend the chicken liver with 30 ml of water until fine. Over low heat, cook the garlic and chili flakes in the oil until fragrant, stir in the tomato paste, then pour in 240 ml of water, the liver purée and the hoisin sauce and cook together until the sauce keeps bubbling for about 1 minute and reaches 74°C (165°F). Mix the cornstarch with 1.5 tsp of water, stir it in and cook for about 30 seconds, until the sauce thinly coats a spoon. Let it cool to warm, then add the peanuts and sesame seeds. Add the optional fish sauce if you want it saltier and more savoury.
5. Soften a sheet of rice paper. Give the sheet a quick turn in room-temperature drinking water and lay it flat on a clean plate. Start rolling when it can bend and the surface is slightly tacky but it still has a little firmness.
6. Roll the rolls. Put the lettuce, noodles, pork and herbs on the lower part, fold the bottom edge up over the filling, then line up the shrimp pieces in front, orange-red side against the rice paper. Fold in both sides, add the chives, and roll forward into a tight cylinder with the seam pressed on the bottom.
7. Plate them one by one. Leave space between the finished rolls and cover them so they do not dry out. Roll and eat right away, served with the dipping sauce. Refrigerate any filling and finished rolls you are not eating yet.

## Key points

- **Rice paper sticks to your hands and tears when pulled**: it took up too much water (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Shorten the dip for the next sheet and let it keep softening on the plate. Drain the fillings first.
- **Bulging, filling spilling out, roll won't tighten**: too much filling, or tough stems are holding the wrapper out (see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Use less, slice the pork thin, remove the stems and gather the filling into a narrow line before folding the bottom edge.
- **Shrimp rubbery and hard, pork dry**: they were overcooked, so the protein contracted and squeezed the water out (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Check each for doneness separately, lift it out as soon as it is cooked, and slice only after it has cooled.
