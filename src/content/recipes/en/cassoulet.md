*Cassoulet* is a pot of white beans and meat from Languedoc in France: beans and meats are each cooked through, then layered in a clay pot and baked slowly. The meats vary by region; here I use the classic combination of lamb, pork belly and garlic sausage. The liquid comes from the bean cooking water, and long-stewed pork rind gives off [gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), which binds the liquid between the beans and meats.

The beans must be cooked until soft in the centre before they go in the oven. The starch in the beans [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), so they eat soft and creamy, and the surface slowly loses water and colours in the oven, with the roasted aroma of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). A finished cassoulet can be spooned out with whole beans and tender meat, moist inside with a thin crisp top. The beans must be soaked overnight, and cooking and baking also need half a day.

## Steps

1. Discard the soaking water and rinse the beans. Tie the blanched pork rind into a bundle and bring it to a boil in one pot with the beans, 1500 ml of clean water, the carrot, the half onion studded with clove, 3 cloves of garlic and the herb bundle. Keep it boiling for at least 30 minutes, then turn to low heat.
2. After cooking the beans over low heat for about 1 hour, add the pork belly and garlic sausage and keep simmering gently until a bean breaks at a pinch and the centre is no longer chalky and hard. Top up with hot water partway so the beans stay under the water. If you use fresh pork sausage, the centre must reach 71°C (160°F).
3. In another pot, melt the lard and brown the lamb shoulder and lamb breast in batches until the surface is light brown. Pour off half the floating fat, add the chopped onion and the remaining garlic and stir-fry until lightly yellow, stir in the tomato purée, then ladle in bean cooking liquid until it just covers the meat.
4. Cover the lamb pot and stew it in a 160°C (325°F) oven for about 1.5–2 hours, until a fork pulls the meat fibres apart easily. Keep the pot at a light bubble; once the bean pot is done, turn off the heat and keep all of the bean liquid.
5. Lift out the pork rind and cut it into small pieces, and lay them in the bottom of a heatproof clay pot of about 3 L. Discard the bean herb bundle, the clove-studded onion and the carrot. Cut the pork belly into pieces and the garlic sausage into thick slices, and layer them in alternating with the lamb and white beans.
6. Add the lamb juices and bean liquid to nearly the level of the top beans, and sprinkle on a thin layer of bread crumbs. Bake at 160°C (325°F) for about 1 hour, until the top is golden brown and the edges of the pot bubble slowly. If the beans on top look dry while it bakes, add hot bean liquid down the side of the pot.
7. Take it out and rest for about 10 minutes so the liquid sets a little, then serve in the pot. Pack the leftovers into shallow containers and refrigerate promptly; when reheating, add a little water and bring the centre to 74°C (165°F).

## Key points

- **Meat is tender but the centre of the beans is hard**: the beans were baked with acidic tomato before they were cooked through, and the cell walls would not soften. Cook the bean liquid on its own until there is no hard core, and put tomato only in the meat pot. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **Inner layers dry and loose, meat tough**: the liquid level was too low, and the liquid kept evaporating during the slow bake. Top up with hot bean liquid down the side, so the gelatin stays in a moist liquid. See [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).
- **Bean skins break and the whole pot turns to mush**: it was cooked too hard, or stirred too often. After the initial boil, switch to a gentle simmer, and spoon the layers in when assembling instead of stirring back and forth. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
