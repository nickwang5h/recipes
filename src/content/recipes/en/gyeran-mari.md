*Gyeran mari* is the Korean home-style rolled omelette, often a side dish with rice and also packed in lunch boxes. The green onion and carrot are cut very fine and mixed into the eggs, which are fried in several thin layers, so the inside has tiny red and green flecks. An ordinary round nonstick pan will do; the thickness of the layers matters more than the shape of the pan.

When egg is heated, its proteins unfold and link up into a thin sheet that can be rolled (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). When you roll, the bottom should be firm enough to lift with a spatula while the surface still looks wet and glossy; that layer keeps cooking and bonds with the egg poured in next. If the heat is too high, the thin sheet dries and hardens first and is hard to bend. See [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/).

## Steps

1. Chop the green onion and carrot into fine bits. Crack the eggs into a bowl, add the salt and black pepper, and beat until no streaks of egg white remain, then stir in the vegetables. Large pieces of carrot would poke through the thin egg sheet, so cut them fine enough to scatter evenly through the eggs.
2. Pour the oil into a nonstick pan about 23–25 cm in diameter and spread it into a thin film with a paper towel. Preheat over medium-low heat for about 1 minute, until the oil flows easily and the bottom of the pan is not smoking.
3. Pour in half of the egg mixture and tilt the pan to spread it into a thin layer. Fry over low heat for about 30–60 seconds, until the edge is set and a spatula can slide underneath, with the surface still wet. Fold one end inward by about 3 cm with the spatula, then roll along, leaving a small piece of egg sheet at the far end lying flat on the pan.
4. Push the roll to the edge of the pan and pour in half of the remaining egg mixture, letting it fill the empty space and join the egg sheet you left. Fry over low heat for about 30–60 seconds, until the new sheet can be lifted, then roll the existing roll over it. Add the last of the egg mixture, fry it the same way for about 30–60 seconds, and roll it up once the edge is set.
5. With the seam side down, fry over low heat for another 1–2 minutes, turning gently onto each side so the seam bonds and the centre sets. Insert a probe from the end into the centre, and take it out at 74°C (165°F); if it hasn't reached that, fry another 30 seconds, without letting the outside turn dark brown.
6. Rest on a cutting board for 5 minutes, until the roll holds its shape and won't be squashed when cut, then cut into pieces about 2 cm thick. The cut faces should show joined layers and no runny raw egg.

## Key points

- **Cracks when rolled**: the egg sheet has fried dry, or the vegetable pieces are too big. Cut the add-ins finer and start rolling while the surface is still wet and glossy. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **Brown outside, wet in the centre**: the bottom of the pan heats too fast and the heat has not reached the middle of the roll. Switch to low heat, spread the eggs in thin layers, and check the centre at the end. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **Layers come apart**: the end of the old egg sheet was rolled all the way up and the new egg did not join it. Leave a small wet piece of sheet each time, so old and new layers set together. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
