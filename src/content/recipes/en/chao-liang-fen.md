Stir-fried liangfen is coated in garlic, doubanjiang and chili, and bites soft, slippery and hot all the way through. Liangfen is a gel formed when starch is heated, gelatinizes and then cools (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). What goes into the pan is already cooked liangfen, so the job is to heat the centre through, soften the surface and coat it in sauce.

Warm liangfen breaks easily, so stir with pushing and lifting motions. Add a little water and cover to heat it through, then uncover to thicken, and you do not have to toss hard while waiting for the centre to warm. Doubanjiang and both soy sauces carry salt, so taste at the end before deciding whether to add more; it is enough for the sauce to thicken, and there is no need to cook it dry.

## Steps

1. Cut the liangfen into blocks of about 2 cm, as evenly as you can, and lay them gently on a plate. Mince the garlic, slice the green onion and finely chop the doubanjiang, and keep the seasonings within reach.
2. Put the oil in a frying pan or wok and heat over low heat. Add the garlic and stir-fry until tiny bubbles form and you smell garlic. Add the doubanjiang and stir gently until the oil turns slightly red, without letting the garlic blacken.
3. Gently add the liangfen, lift it from the bottom with the spatula and toss for about 10 seconds, so the surface of the blocks picks up the oil and doubanjiang first.
4. Add the light soy sauce, dark soy sauce and chili flakes and push them together along the bottom of the pan, then add the thirteen-spice blend and toss gently for about 10 seconds. The powder should land on the wet liangfen and sauce, not stay on the dry edge of the pan to scorch.
5. Add 20 ml water along the edge of the pan and gently push to mix the seasoning liquid. Cover and braise over low heat until the centre of the liangfen is hot and the blocks are soft; a few quick tosses will not heat them through.
6. Partway through, check the bottom of the pan: there should still be a layer of moist sauce, and if it is too dry add a little hot water. Take a block with clean chopsticks; once the centre and the edges are both hot with no cold, firm core, uncover and reduce the sauce.
7. Push gently over low heat until the sauce thickens enough to cling to the liangfen, with a little sauce left at the bottom of the pan. Taste, add a little salt if needed, turn off the heat, plate and sprinkle with green onion.

## Key points

- **Liangfen turns to crumbled mush**: hot gel is soft and falls apart if stirred hard (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Lift or push with a wide spatula instead of repeatedly cutting and pressing.
- **Scalding outside, still cold inside**: the centres of the blocks heat slowly, and a few 10-second tosses will not heat them through (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Add a little water and braise covered, then check the centre.
- **Sauce is too strong and gets saltier as it reduces**: the salt in the doubanjiang and soy sauces concentrates as the sauce reduces (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Leave salt for last and stop heating as soon as the sauce clings to the liangfen; if it is too salty, add water to dilute it.
