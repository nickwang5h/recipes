*Arista alla fiorentina* is a Tuscan roast of pork loin: the outside is scented with garlic and rosemary, and the meat is juicy when sliced. The classic seasoning is garlic, rosemary, salt and pepper, and Artusi's version also adds a little clove. It uses loin with the rib bones; when you shop, look for *bone-in pork loin roast*. The long thin *pork tenderloin* is a different cut, with a different thickness and fat.

A whole roast goes in the oven, and the surface browns first while the centre heats later; see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/). The fat on the outside brings richness and aroma, but it cannot stop the lean meat from losing moisture. Pork loin has little connective tissue, so if you keep roasting after it is done, [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) only tightens the meat further and squeezes the water out. So measuring the centre with a probe is more reliable than roasting by the clock.

## Steps

1. Preheat the oven to 180°C (350°F). Pat the surface of the meat dry and keep the fat layer; if the bones have been separated, put the meat back on the bones and tie it with kitchen twine into a roast of fairly even thickness, fat side up.
2. Finely chop the garlic and rosemary and mix them with salt and black pepper. Cut a few shallow slits in the surface with a small knife, push the seasoning into them and rub the rest over the meat; push the cloves into shallow slits and remember where they are so you can remove them.
3. Put the meat in a roasting pan of a suitable size, bone side down and fat side up, and put it on the middle rack. Roast until fat begins to render from the outside and the surface is pale gold, then spoon the rendered fat from the bottom of the pan back over the surface.
4. Keep roasting, and start taking the temperature after about one hour. Insert the probe from the side into the centre of the thickest part, avoiding bone and large pockets of fat; if the surface is already deep golden and the centre is not yet done, loosely cover with a layer of aluminum foil.
5. Measure several of the thickest parts of the lean meat, and take the roast out when it reaches 71°C (160°F). The total time varies with the shape of the meat, so do not judge by the colour of the juices.
6. Move the meat to a platter, loosely cover with foil and rest for about 15 minutes. Remove the cloves and twine, cut the meat from the bones along the bone, and slice it against the grain of the muscle.
7. Pour off the excess floating fat from the roasting pan, add water and scrape up the brown bits, heating briefly until it makes a sauce you can spoon over the meat. Do not scrape in the blackened parts. Mix in the juices that ran out during resting and spoon over the slices.

## Key points

- **The outside is very dark and the centre is still cool**: the roast is too thick and the outer layer takes in heat faster than the inside. Shield the surface and keep measuring the thickest part; if the probe touches bone, the reading is not the temperature of the lean centre. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **It is cooked but dry and tough**: the loin was already done but kept roasting by the timer. Take it out at 71°C (160°F) and slice after resting; this cut is not suited to long roasting until it turns soft. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **The pan juices are bitter and the garlic tastes burnt**: the chopped garlic on the surface or the liquid in the pan has burned black. Push the seasoning into the shallow slits as much as you can, and do not use a roasting pan that is too large; the light brown bits have a roasted flavour, but black burnt bits should be thrown away. See [the Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).
