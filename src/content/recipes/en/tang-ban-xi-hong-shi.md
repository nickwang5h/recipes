The sweet-sour taste of sugared tomatoes comes from ripe fruit and white sugar. The sugar dissolves in the juice that runs from the cut surfaces and coats every piece; the sweetness softens the acidity, while the tomato's own tartness stays in the dish (see [acid](/principles/acid/)). After cutting, don't throw away the seeds and juice, which are the base of the dressing.

Peeling removes a tough layer of skin from each bite. Briefly [blanch](/principles/blanching/) the whole tomatoes, and cool them once the skin lifts at the score; the flesh should still hold its shape. Choose ripe tomatoes that are slightly springy; tomatoes that are already soft lose too much juice when peeled and cut.

## Steps

1. Wash the tomatoes under running water to remove any dirt from the surface. Score a shallow cross in the bottom, cutting only through the skin and not deep into the flesh.
2. Put the tomatoes in boiling water for about 30 seconds and watch whether the score curls back. Lift them out once the skin has loosened; if it still clings to the flesh, dip them in briefly again.
3. Cool them in drinkable cold water until you can hold them firmly, and peel along the score. Lift the thin skin gently, so you don't tear off soft flesh with it; drain any water left on the surface.
4. Cut off the hard stem and cut each tomato into about eight wedges. Cut with a sharp knife and try to keep the wedges intact; collect the clean juice on the cutting board into the plate.
5. Lay one layer of tomatoes on a shallow plate and sprinkle on part of the sugar evenly, then add the next layer. Divide the 20 g of sugar between the layers rather than piling it all on top, and don't add any water.
6. Cover and refrigerate for 10 minutes, until clear juice appears at the bottom of the plate and some of the sugar has dissolved. When serving, spoon the juice from the bottom back over the top; leaving a few sugar grains is fine. Serve at once.

## Key points

- **Still sharply sour**: the ripeness of the fruit decides the sweet-sour balance, and sugar can only change how the balance tastes (see [acid](/principles/acid/)). Choose ripe tomatoes first, taste a piece of flesh together with the juice, and then decide whether to add sugar.
- **Flesh is mushy after peeling**: it stayed in the hot water too long and the flesh softened with the heat (see [blanching and removing off-odours](/principles/blanching/)). Lift the tomatoes out to cool as soon as the skin curls, and score more shallowly next time.
- **A puddle of water on the plate, and it tastes flat**: the blanching water wasn't drained and diluted the juice and sugar (see [acid](/principles/acid/)). Drain the surface water before cutting, and keep the juice that comes out when you cut.
