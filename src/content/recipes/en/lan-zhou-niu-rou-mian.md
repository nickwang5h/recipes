Lanzhou beef noodle soup is a bowl of clear beef broth, daikon, hand-pulled noodles, chili oil and cilantro, with a broth that is clear and rich on the tongue. Simmer the broth gently. The collagen in the meat and bones slowly turns to gelatin, which gives the broth its smooth mouthfeel; see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).

In the classic method the noodles are pulled just before cooking. The cook stretches the dough long, folds it, works it into an even rope, then folds and pulls it thinner again for each bowl, shaping it by the dough's extensibility; see [Gluten](/principles/gluten/). Noodle shops use an edible plant-ash lye (*penghui*) to help the dough stretch. This is hard to reproduce at home, so plain fresh wheat noodles can stand in. Cook the noodles in a separate pot, or the starch they shed will cloud the clear broth.

## Steps

1. Put the beef, bones and 3000 ml cold water in a heavy pot. Heat over medium-high for 15–20 minutes, until the broth starts to boil and foam gathers. Turn to medium and skim for 10 minutes, until the foam clearly lessens and the surface is fairly clean.
2. Put the cloves, bay leaves, Sichuan peppercorns, fennel seeds, nutmeg, star anise and cassia in a spice bag. Add it to the pot with the green onion whites, ginger, garlic and dried chilies. Cover with a small gap and simmer on low for about 2 hours, until chopsticks pierce the beef fairly easily and the connective tissue is soft but the piece still lifts out whole. Check every 1 hour; the surface should show only scattered small bubbles.
3. Lift out the beef. Put 300 ml of the broth in a shallow container to cover the meat, and set the container in a bath of cold water for 20 minutes, until it stops giving off much steam. Then refrigerate overnight, until the meat is firm enough to slice. Take the bones and spices out of the rest of the broth, pass it through a fine sieve, skim off most of the surface fat and season with salt.
4. Put the daikon in the strained broth and simmer on low for 10–15 minutes, until the slices are translucent at the edges and chopsticks cut through easily. Slice the beef thinly against the grain and return it to the broth. Heat on low for 3–5 minutes, until the centre of the slices reaches 74°C (165°F) and the broth is simmering again.
5. In another pot, heat water over medium-high for 8–10 minutes, until it is at a full boil. Cook the freshly pulled noodles bowl by bowl for 1 minute, until they stretch, have no raw-flour taste at the centre and are still springy. Cook store-bought fresh noodles for the time on the package, to the same state. Lift them into each bowl and add broth right away.
6. Divide the sliced beef, daikon and clear broth among the bowls. Scatter cilantro and the green onion greens, and drizzle 1 tbsp chili oil on each. There should be enough broth for the noodles to loosen, with the chili oil floating on top. Serve as soon as the bowls are filled.

## Key points

- **Cloudy white broth with a heavy spice flavour**: a hard boil breaks the fat into the broth, and a long cook keeps drawing out the spices. Keep the boil gentle and take out the spice bag when the simmer is done (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).
- **The beef falls apart and won't slice**: it was cooked too long, or cut while hot. Lift it out once [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/) have softened it enough for chopsticks to pass through, and slice thinly against the grain after it has cooled and firmed.
- **Noodles break and the broth turns sticky**: the dough was not stretched enough, or the noodles were cooked too long. Pulled noodles need a continuous [gluten](/principles/gluten/) network first. Keep the noodle water and the clear broth apart, and cook and eat bowl by bowl.
