Steamed pumpkin uses only pumpkin and water, and the sweetness comes from the flesh itself. Steam condenses on the cooler pieces and releases heat, which then travels from the surface to the centre (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). The thicker a piece, the more slowly the centre keeps up with the surface; cutting slices of even thickness helps more than simply turning up the heat.

Heat softens the pumpkin's tissue, and varieties with more starch also undergo [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), which gives a floury, mealy bite. Varieties with more water are softer and moister, and the same steaming gives a different texture. Choose by variety and flesh, and judge doneness by how soft the thickest part is once steamed; the time is only a starting point.

## Steps

1. Wash the skin of the pumpkin, cut it open, and remove the pulp and seeds. If the skin is hard, peel it first; if it is thin and you plan to eat it, you can keep it, but wash off the soil and grit from the surface.
2. Cut the flesh into slices about 2 cm thick, keeping the thickness as even as you can. Set especially thin trimmings aside, so thin pieces are not steamed to pieces while waiting for the thick ones.
3. Add about 1000 ml water to the steamer, with the water level below the rack. There must be enough water to steam through to the end, and the plate must not sit in the water; bring it to a boil over high heat until steam rises steadily.
4. Arrange the pumpkin slices evenly on a heatproof plate, spread in a single layer as far as possible. Leave a gap around the plate for steam to pass, set it into the steamer that is already boiling, and cover.
5. Keep the water boiling steadily and steam for about 15–20 minutes. Start checking from the thickest slice: it is done when a chopstick goes through to the centre easily, the flesh has no hard core and parts when pressed lightly. If there is still resistance, keep steaming; thin slices that are already soft can come out first.
6. Turn off the heat, lift the lid so that steam escapes away from you, and take the plate out with a plate gripper or heatproof gloves. The small amount of pumpkin juice on the plate can be eaten too; if there is a lot of condensed water, pour off the excess so the slices do not sit soaking.

## Key points

- **Edges mushy, centre still hard**: the slices are uneven in thickness or stacked too tightly (see [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/)). Arrange them apart by thickness and check the thickest slice; do not judge doneness by the colour of the surface alone.
- **Still not soft at the stated time**: the water was not boiling steadily, or the plate blocked the steam (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Restore steady steam first, then keep steaming; add hot water if it runs short midway, and never let the pot boil dry.
- **Cooked through but not floury**: varieties differ in water and starch content (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Slices with no hard core need no more long steaming; for a floury, glutinous bite, switch to a variety with starchier flesh.
