*Caponata* is a Sicilian sweet-and-sour vegetable dish, rich with oil, savoury and a little sharp: fried eggplant is combined with tomato, celery, green olives and capers, and sugar and wine vinegar bring out the sweet-sour flavour. The eggplant must be fried until soft before the acid goes in, because vegetables are very hard to soften again in acid; see [acid](/principles/acid/).

Frying first colours the outside of the eggplant and leaves the inside soft; see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/). The other ingredients are cooked into a thick sauce, and the eggplant is folded in gently at the very end, so the pieces do not break. Once made, let it cool and rest in the refrigerator for about 2 hours, so the richness, savouriness and sweet-sour flavour can be tasted distinctly in a single bite.

## Steps

1. Cut the eggplant into pieces with the skin on, sprinkle with a little salt and toss, and leave in a colander for about 30–40 minutes. When water beads on the surface, squeeze lightly, rinse off the extra salt if needed, then dry thoroughly with paper towels, especially the cut surfaces.
2. Thinly slice the red onion; remove the loose seeds and pulp from the tomatoes and cut the flesh into strips; thinly slice the tender celery stalks and chop the tender leaves. Pit the green olives, and rinse and drain the capers. Have all of these ready before you start frying.
3. Put the olive oil for cooking in a wide pan and stir-fry the onion over low heat for about 10 minutes, until soft and slightly translucent. Add the tomato and celery and cook for about 5 minutes, then add the olives, capers and pine nuts and keep cooking until the thin juices of the tomato have clearly reduced.
4. In a separate deep pot, heat the frying oil, with the oil no more than half the height of the pot. Test with one piece of eggplant first; when it keeps bubbling and slowly colours on the surface without the oil smoking, add the rest in batches. Fry each batch for about 7–8 minutes, until the edges of the pieces are golden and they are soft when pressed lightly with a spoon, then lift out and drain.
5. Put the fried eggplant into the thick sauce and fold gently for about 2–3 minutes. Add the torn basil, white wine vinegar and brown sugar, toss to combine, and briefly raise the heat to let the sharp vinegar smell go off; turn off the heat when the sauce looks glossy and clings to the eggplant.
6. Taste a bite with eggplant and olive, then decide whether to add salt. Move the dish to a shallow container, let the obvious steam escape, then refrigerate for about 2 hours. Take it out shortly before eating to come back toward room temperature, and fold once gently so the thick sauce at the bottom coats the vegetables again.

## Key points

- **Eggplant is soft outside and firm inside, and the sourness is already strong**: the eggplant went into the sweet-sour sauce before it had softened. Fry it separately until the centre is soft when pressed, then combine; see [acid](/principles/acid/).
- **Oil pools on the plate, and the eggplant surface looks wet and pale**: too much went in per batch, the oil recovered too slowly, or it was not drained after coming out. Fry in batches, reheat the oil before the next batch, and drain off the surface oil after lifting out; see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/).
- **The sweet-sour sauce tastes right but each bite is very salty**: the salt from the capers, olives and salted eggplant has added up. Rinse the salt off the eggplant first, and taste before adding salt at the end; see [salt](/principles/salt/).
- **Eggplant falls apart into mush while the sauce is still thin**: the tomato was not reduced thick, and the whole pot was rescued by long cooking. Make the thick sauce first and fold in the eggplant last; after the sugar and vinegar go in, cook only briefly; see [acid](/principles/acid/).
