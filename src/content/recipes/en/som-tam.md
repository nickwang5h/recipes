*Som tam* is a salad pounded and tossed at the same time. It is crunchy, and sour, salty, sweet and hot all at once. This version is **som tam Thai**, with peanuts and dried shrimp: green papaya shreds, yardlong beans and tomatoes, tossed in a sour, salty, sweet and hot dressing. The papaya must be green and firm; when cut, the flesh is pale and the seeds are not yet ripe. Soft, sweet ripe papaya can't give this crunch. The fish sauce and dried shrimp give it a touch of the sea.

The light pounding is meant to crack the surface of the papaya shreds so they take up flavour, while the fibres stay crisp enough to bite through. The thickness comes from [knife work](/principles/knife-work/); if you pound too hard, the papaya turns into wet, soft crumbs. Taste the dressing together with the vegetables and fruit: [acid](/principles/acid/) makes the flavour bright, and the [salt](/principles/salt/) in the fish sauce slowly draws water out of the vegetables and fruit, so serve it as soon as it is mixed.

## Steps

1. Peel the green papaya, halve it and remove the seeds, then cut the clean flesh into thin, long shreds. Use a shredder, or cut thin slices first and then shred them. Keep the papaya steady on the cutting board; don't hold it in your hand and chop at it with a knife.
2. Put the garlic and chilies in a deep mortar and pound to a coarse crush. Add the grated palm sugar and press a few times to mix the sugar with the garlic and chilies. A stone mortar also works, but pound the vegetables and fruit lightly later.
3. Add the dried shrimp and yardlong beans, and pound until the shrimp break up slightly and the bean pieces crack. If using green beans instead, cook and cool them first, drain them and then add; they can't be eaten raw.
4. Pour in the fish sauce and lime juice and stir until no obvious lumps of sugar remain. Add the papaya shreds and tomatoes. Hold a spoon in one hand and lift from the bottom while pounding lightly with the other, so every layer is coated in the dressing. If the mortar is too small, do it in two batches.
5. Stop pounding when the papaya shreds have softened slightly but are still crisp, and the tomatoes have just burst a little and their juice has mixed into the dressing. Taste a piece of papaya and tomato together: the sourness should be bright, and the salt and sweetness should both be noticeable.
6. Toss in the coarsely crushed peanuts, plate with a little of the juices, and eat at once. If you don't have a deep mortar, use a sturdy large bowl and press the vegetables and fruit lightly with one end of a rolling pin, then toss with a spoon.

## Key points

- **Papaya is mushy with no crunch**: the papaya is too ripe, or it was pounded too hard. Choose firm green papaya, pound the garlic and chilies first, and once the vegetables and fruit are in the mortar, switch to light pressing and tossing. See [knife work and cooking](/principles/knife-work/).
- **A lot of water on the plate and the flavour gets weaker**: it was mixed ahead and left standing, and the salt keeps drawing water out of the vegetables and fruit. The ingredients can be cut ahead, but save the fish sauce and the sour dressing for just before serving, and add the peanuts last. See [salt](/principles/salt/).
- **Sweetness covers the sourness, or only a sharp sourness is left**: the sugar and lime are not balanced. Mix by the amounts given first, then taste it on a piece of papaya and add a little of whichever side is missing; don't taste only the dressing at the bottom of the mortar. See [acid](/principles/acid/).
