*Jok* is a common Thai breakfast congee, cooked until the grains are very fine and smooth in the mouth, with pork meatballs in it and ginger strips, chopped green onion and white pepper on top. As the jasmine rice heats in water, its starch absorbs water and swells, the grains gradually split open, and the liquid thickens with them (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Cooking the congee base in plain water first and adding the pork stock afterwards makes the final thickness easier to control.

The tenderness of the meatballs comes from meat with a little fat, a small amount of ice water, and thorough beating. Salt helps the proteins in the meat become sticky, so the water stays held in the mixture (see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/)). When they go into the broth, just scoop the mixture into small balls of similar size so they cook through together. Add the ginger strips when filling the bowls, and their sharpness comes through more clearly.

## Steps

1. Put the ground pork, light soy sauce, fish sauce, sugar, white pepper and ice water in a food processor and blend for about 1 minute, until the mixture is smooth and sticks together. Return it to the refrigerator and take it out when you are ready to cook the meatballs.
2. Rinse the jasmine rice and drain. Add 1200 ml of water to a pot and heat over medium-high heat for about 5–8 minutes, until the water is at a steady rolling boil; add the rice and stir it apart to keep it from sticking to the bottom.
3. Turn to medium-low heat, leave the lid ajar, and cook for about 45–60 minutes, until the grains bloom and fall apart when pressed with the back of a spoon, and the congee base is fine and thick. Stir every 5 minutes at first; once it thickens, keep stirring gently across the bottom. If the grains are still hard and the congee is already too dry, add 60 ml at a time from the reserved hot water to avoid it boiling dry.
4. In another pot, combine the pork stock and the 4 tsp of light soy sauce and heat over medium heat for about 4–6 minutes, until it boils. Using two teaspoons, scoop the meat mixture into balls about 2 cm across and drop them in, nudge them apart gently, and cook for about 2 minutes, until the surface sets and the colour turns lighter.
5. Ladle the congee base into the meatball broth and stir to break up any lumps, then cook over low heat for another 4–6 minutes, until the centre of the meatballs reaches 71°C (160°F) and the congee runs continuously off the spoon. If it is too thick, loosen it with the rest of the reserved hot water, 60 ml at a time, and cook 1 minute after each addition, until the whole pot is bubbling gently again.
6. Taste and adjust with the 1 tsp of salt, then divide among four bowls. Sprinkle each with a quarter of the ginger strips and green onion, finish with white pepper, and serve hot.

## Key points

- **Grains still hard but the congee base is sticking**: water is evaporating fast and the bottom isn't being stirred. Keep it at a gentle bubble, stir gently across the bottom once it thickens, and add the measured hot water in time; starch needs water to keep [gelatinizing](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **Meatballs loose and coarse**: the meat is too lean, or went into the broth before it became sticky. Choose ground pork with some fat and beat it with the seasonings and ice water into a sticky paste first; the stickiness comes from [salt](/principles/salt/), so develop it before it goes into the pot.
- **Meatballs hard while the congee is just right**: the meatballs were simmered from the time the rice was cooking all the way to the end, and cooked so long the meat tightened and went hard. Cook the congee base first, then add the small meatballs and take them out when they are done (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
