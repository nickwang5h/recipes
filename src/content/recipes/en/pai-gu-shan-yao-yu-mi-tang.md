Pork rib, Chinese yam and corn soup puts the meaty flavour and the sweetness of vegetables in one pot. The yam is soft and starchy, the carrot and corn keep their shape, and the soup keeps a little of the fragrance of fried ribs. Blanching the ribs first and then simmering gently is the starting point for a soup with less grey scum (see [Blanching and Removing Off-Odours](/principles/blanching/)).

The connective tissue on the ribs needs moist heat and time to soften gradually (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). The starch in the yam absorbs water and cooks, and with long simmering it breaks into the soup more easily. For a clear soup and yam in pieces, stir little. If you like a thick, floury soup, let the yam simmer with the ribs from the beginning.

## Steps

1. Peel the carrot and cut it into rolling-cut chunks about 3 cm. Cut the corn into sections about 4 cm long. Wear gloves to peel the yam and cut it into sections about 4 cm long, and keep it in plain water if you will not use it right away.
2. Put the ribs in a pot of cold water so the water covers them, and add 10 g of the ginger slices, the knotted white part of the green onion and 15 ml of Shaoxing wine. Bring to a boil, then cook for 3 minutes more. Skim the scum, lift out the ribs, rinse off surface particles, and drain well.
3. Heat the pot, add 10 ml of oil, and stir-fry the remaining 10 g of ginger slices until fragrant. Add the ribs and stir-fry over medium heat for about 1 minute, until the surface moisture has gone and there are a few pale gold sear marks. They do not need to turn deep brown.
4. Add 1500 ml of water and the carrot, corn and drained yam. Bring to a boil and skim any new scum. Turn to low, cover and simmer for 40 minutes, keeping the surface lightly bubbling and avoiding a constant hard boil.
5. Check the thickest part of a rib. A chopstick should go in fairly easily, and the join between meat and bone should have softened. Check that the centre of the yam has no hard core. If the ribs are still tough, keep simmering gently until soft, and add hot water when the level in the pot drops.
6. Add part of the salt, stir, and simmer on low for 2 more minutes so the salt spreads evenly. Taste and add more if needed, sprinkle with the minced green part of the onion, and serve the ribs and vegetable pieces together with plenty of broth.

## Key points

- **Grey bits in the soup and an off taste**: scum after blanching was still stuck to the ribs (see [Blanching and Removing Off-Odours](/principles/blanching/)). Rinse off the particles and simmer in fresh water, drain well before frying, and skim new scum when the soup comes to a boil again.
- **The ribs are still tough after 40 minutes**: cooked meat does not mean the connective tissue has softened (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Treat the time as a checkpoint and keep simmering gently until the meat leaves the bone fairly easily.
- **The yam breaks apart and the soup turns thick**: the yam was cooked, then simmered longer, boiled hard or broken up (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). To keep its shape, add the yam after the ribs have simmered for 20 minutes, and check the softness at the end.
- **The pot will not bubble again after adding water**: the new water lowered the temperature of the whole pot. Hot water recovers a gentle simmer faster. Wait until small bubbles return before checking whether the ribs are soft (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)).
