Egg fried rice brings cooked rice and eggs together again in a hot pan, with separate grains, soft pieces of egg and green onion fragrance that lasts to the end. The rice already went through [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) when it was cooked: the starch absorbed water and swelled, and the grains cooked through. After cooling, part of the starch rearranges (retrogradation) and the grains firm up; spreading the rice out also lets steam escape from the surface. The two together help the rice separate in the pan.

Freshly cooked rice can also be fried, but its surface has to dry out first. When wet rice hits the pan, the heat goes into evaporating water first and the rice easily sticks into clumps; piling too much into the pan causes the same problem. See [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/).

## Steps

1. Loosen the rice. Gently break up large clumps of refrigerated rice, keeping the grains whole. If you use freshly cooked rice, spread it in a thin layer and let it sit for at least 15 minutes, until the visible steam is gone and the surface is no longer wet and sticky, then fry. Do not leave rice out at room temperature for a long time waiting for it to firm up.
2. Get everything ready. Beat the eggs, and keep the green onion and light soy sauce close at hand. Once the rice is in the pan you have to keep turning it, so divide the oil and seasoning in advance, so you can take it off the heat in time when the rice is hot through.
3. Cook the eggs. Heat a carbon steel wok first and then add 1 tbsp of oil; if using a non-stick skillet, add the oil first and then heat until the surface of the oil shimmers. Pour in the eggs and wait about 30 seconds, until the egg puffs up and the bottom has set, then fold it into soft pieces and push it to one side.
4. Separate the rice. Add another 1 tbsp of oil to the pan and add the rice, then turn the egg pieces onto the rice. Scoop the spatula from the bottom and fold the rice over, breaking apart the remaining clumps and cutting the egg into small pieces; do not keep pressing down, which crushes the grains.
5. Stir-fry until hot through. Let the rice touch the surface of the pan again and again and stir-fry for about 2–3 minutes, until every grain is separate, the whole pan is giving off steam and a spoonful scooped up has no cold, hard clumps. If the pile of rice is thick and hard to turn, fry in batches, bringing each batch to this state.
6. Season and finish. Add the remaining oil and the light soy sauce and toss until the colour is even and there is no liquid at the bottom of the pan. Sprinkle in the green onion and toss until the aroma comes out, then plate, keeping a little chew in the grains.

## Key points

- **The rice turns pasty and gets wetter as you fry**: the surface of the rice has too much water, and gelatinized starch tends to stick together. Spread the rice out and let it dry before frying (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)); wet, soft rice is better made into a rice soup.
- **Scorched outside and still cold inside**: the pan is piled too thick and the pan temperature drops (see [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)), so the rice has too little contact with the pan. Break up large clumps, stir-fry in batches and make sure the inside is hot too.
- **How to store leftover rice**: when rice sits at room temperature for a long time, *Bacillus cereus* multiplies and produces heat-resistant toxins that re-frying does not remove. To use rice the next day, spread it out to cool and refrigerate it within 1 hour of cooking, and fry it within 24 hours; throw out rice that has stayed out overnight at room temperature (NHS advice).
