*Chaoshou* is the Sichuan word for wontons. Wontons in chili oil wrap pork filling in thin square wrappers, and after cooking they are tossed in a sauce of chili oil, mashed garlic and soy sauce. The wrappers are slippery, the filling soft, savoury with a touch of sweet, and the vinegar leaves only a light sourness.

The water goes into the pork filling in portions. [Salt](/principles/salt/) and stirring help the proteins in the meat become sticky, holding the water first and then setting during cooking (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). The chili oil sauce is mixed in the bowls separately, keeping the [aroma](/principles/aromatics/) of the chili and garlic, rather than being cooked for a long time with the wontons.

## Steps

1. Chop the white parts of the green onions and put them with the minced ginger into the 80 ml of warm water. Soak for 5 minutes, until the water clearly smells of green onion and ginger. Strain out the green onion and ginger and keep the soaking water; mince the green parts.
2. Add the egg, salt and white pepper to the ground pork and stir in one direction for 2 minutes, until the filling is sticky. Add the green onion and ginger water in three portions and stir another 3–4 minutes; let each portion be absorbed before adding the next, until the filling holds together in a mass with no loose water at the bottom of the bowl.
3. Put about 1 tsp of filling in the centre of each wrapper. Moisten two adjacent edges with the sealing water, fold in half into a triangle, press out the air next to the filling and press tight. Then overlap the two pointed corners and stick them together with water. Cover the wrappers you are not using with a clean cloth so the edges do not dry and crack.
4. Divide all the chili oil sauce ingredients evenly among four bowls and stir until the sugar crystals dissolve. Each bowl should have a layer of chili oil with some fine chili flakes, with the garlic spread evenly.
5. Heat the water for cooking the wontons over high heat for about 8–10 minutes, until at a steady rolling boil. Add the wontons in two batches and nudge them off the bottom with the back of a spoon; cook each batch for 4–5 minutes, and after they have all floated, cook 1 minute more. Pick the largest one, insert a thermometer into the centre of the filling; when it reads 74°C (165°F) and the wrapper has turned translucent, they are ready to lift out.
6. Drain off the obvious dripping water with a slotted spoon, put the wontons straight into the sauce bowls, and toss gently so the thin wrappers are all coated with the chili oil sauce. Sprinkle with the green parts and eat hot; after draining, do not pile them up and leave them waiting, since the thin wrappers stick together easily.

## Key points

- **Filling weeps water and won't stay wrapped**: the water went in too fast, before the meat had become sticky. Stir the meat, egg and salt first, then add the water in portions, waiting each time until no water is left at the bottom of the bowl. See [salt](/principles/salt/).
- **Wrappers tear and filling leaks out**: they were overfilled, filling got caught in the seal, or they were stirred hard after going in the pot. Wrap only a small ball of filling in the thin wrapper, press the edges tight, and push the water with the back of a spoon to move the wontons. See [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).
- **Sauce is bland, only heat is left**: too much of the cooking water came along into the bowl, or the chili oil lacked fragrance. Drain before tossing, choose a Sichuan-style chili oil with a full aroma, and leave the mixed sauce in the bowl to finish. See [spices and aroma extraction](/principles/aromatics/).
