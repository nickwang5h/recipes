Sweet and sour pork tenderloin is battered strips of fried pork, finished with a coat of tomato sweet-and-sour sauce. Tenderloin has little connective tissue, so it cooks quickly. The starch coating first absorbs water and [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), then dries out in the hot oil, which is how the crisp shell forms. The first fry cooks the meat through, and the brief second fry dries the shell (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)).

Have the sauce ready first, so that the pork can be tossed in it as soon as it is fried. The sauce is watery and will soften the crisp shell; cook it thick and toss only a few times to keep the contrast between crisp and tender. The sugar and ketchup soften the sharpness of the vinegar, and the type of vinegar also changes the aroma.

## Steps

1. Trim the white silverskin from the tenderloin and cut into even strips about 1 cm thick. Mix with the beaten egg, Shaoxing wine, light soy sauce, oyster sauce, salt and white pepper by hand, then cover and refrigerate to marinate for at least 20 minutes.
2. Stir the ketchup, vinegar, sugar and 150 ml of water into a sauce until there are no obvious sugar grains, and leave it beside the stove. Before frying, set out a draining rack and a plate for cooked food, and keep the plate that held the raw meat separate.
3. Drain the excess egg from the bottom of the bowl of meat. Sprinkle the starch over the strips in several additions, mixing them one by one so the surface gets a thin, continuous layer of starch. The strips should be separate from each other, with no big lumps of dry starch; fry only after they are coated.
4. Heat the oil to 160°C (325°F), then add the strips in batches, one at a time, and gently separate them once they set. Fry until the shell is slightly yellow and the strips are cooked through; test the centre of the thickest strip, which should reach 71°C (160°F), then lift out and drain.
5. Once the oil has recovered, raise it to about 190°C (375°F) and fry the strips again in batches for about 40 seconds, until the shell is dry and crisp, then lift out immediately and spread out to let the steam escape. The oil should not smoke; take out any batch that has already darkened early.
6. In a fresh pot, heat the oil for the sauce, pour in the sauce and bring to a boil, then reduce until it coats a spatula and the mark left when the spatula is dragged across the bottom of the pot stays briefly. Add the fried pork and toss quickly until every strip is thinly coated, then turn off the heat and plate at once.

## Key points

- **Shell is yellow but the centre is not cooked**: the strips are too thick or the oil too hot, so the surface browns first (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Cut even strips, and cook the centre in the first fry before going on to the second.
- **Coating clumps and the shell is thick and hard**: too much egg in the bowl clumps the dry starch into thick lumps (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Drain off the extra liquid, coat in several additions, and separate strips that are stuck together.
- **Goes soft as soon as it is coated**: the sauce is still thin and the shell takes water back (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Reduce the sauce thick before adding the pork, spread it out when it comes off the heat, and eat while hot.
- **Saltiness and pepper overpower the sweet and sour**: soy sauce and oyster sauce already contain salt, and pepper stands out in a small batch (see [salt](/principles/salt/)). Start with small amounts in the marinade, and add no salt to the sweet-sour sauce.
