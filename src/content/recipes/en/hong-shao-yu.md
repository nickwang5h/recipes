The skin of red-braised fish is fried to a pale gold, the sauce is savoury with a hint of sweet and sour, and the flesh is tender. Dry the fish first and then fry it so the skin colours easily; the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) gives the fried aroma, and the dark soy sauce deepens the colour of the sauce. The sugar goes straight into the liquid for seasoning, without making a separate caramel.

The tenderness of the fish depends on stopping the heat in time once it is cooked. While braising, keep the sauce at a gentle bubble, turn the fish as little as possible and spoon sauce over it, so the whole fish heats evenly and is less likely to break (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Crucian carp has many fine bones, so pick out the bones carefully when eating.

## Steps

1. Rinse the prepared crucian carp, making sure the gills and the blood membrane in the belly cavity are cleaned out, and cut a few slashes in the thick part on both sides. Pat the skin, belly cavity and slashes dry with paper towels. Shred the ginger, smash the garlic and cut the chilies into sections.
2. Heat 40 ml of oil in a snug shallow pan, add the fish over medium heat, then turn to medium-low so the underside gradually sets. Keep the bottom of the pan at a steady sizzle, and nudge the fish gently once the skin shows a pale gold; flip only when it releases from the pan, and do not force it with the spatula.
3. Support the body and flip the fish, and fry until that skin sets too. Push the fish to one side, stir-fry the ginger, garlic and dried chilies briefly in the cleared oil, and as soon as it smells fragrant, drizzle in the Shaoxing wine, then add the vinegar, sugar and 5 ml of dark soy sauce.
4. Add about 400 ml of water, to about halfway up the fish. Bring to a boil, then turn to a gentle simmer and cover to braise; at about 1 minute check whether it sticks to the bottom, and if it moves freely, support it and flip once; after that move the fish as little as possible, and every so often spoon the sauce over the back facing up.
5. After braising for about 3–4 minutes, add the optional oyster sauce and Thai chili, and keep simmering gently until the flesh at the thickest part is completely opaque and comes away from the bone. If the fish is thick, braise a little longer, and do not judge only by how much liquid is left.
6. Lift the cooked fish onto a plate first, and reduce the sauce in the pan on its own until it thinly coats the back of a spoon; taste and add a little salt. Turn to low heat, sprinkle in the green onion and optional cilantro, cover for about 20 seconds, then turn off the heat and pour the sauce back over the fish.

## Key points

- **Skin sticks to the pan and the fish breaks when flipped**: the surface was wet, or it was scraped before the skin had set, so it neither colours nor develops the fried aroma (see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Pat dry before it goes in the pan, and flip only when it releases naturally.
- **Little liquid left but the back of the fish is undercooked**: the liquid reducing fast does not mean the fish is cooking fast (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Check the thick back, and if the liquid runs dry first, add a little hot water and keep braising gently.
- **Dry fish, body falling apart**: the boil was too hard, the fish was turned too often, or the cooked fish stayed in the pan while the sauce reduced (see [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Simmer gently, spoon the sauce over, and take the cooked fish out first.
- **Thick sauce too salty**: the dark soy sauce and the optional oyster sauce already carry salt, and the saltiness grows heavier as the sauce thickens. Do not over-reduce (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)), and add salt only after a final taste.
