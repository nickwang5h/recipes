Hot and sour soup gets its sourness from vinegar and its heat from white pepper. Tofu, shredded pork, wood ear and daylily buds give it texture. The soup should be slightly thick but still pour smoothly off a spoon. Each spoonful lifts some of the ingredients, and no thick paste coats your mouth afterwards.

That smooth feel comes from [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Disperse the starch in cold water first, then stir it into the hot soup so it thickens evenly. The egg then goes into a soup that already has a light thickening, which makes separate egg ribbons more likely. The vinegar and white pepper go in last, so long cooking does not carry off their aroma; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).

## Steps

1. Rinse the dried shiitake, wood ear and daylily buds separately. Soak each in 480 ml of warm water for 30–60 minutes, until the shiitake are soft in the centre, the wood ear is fully open and the daylily buds bend. Snip the hard base off the wood ear, remove the shiitake stems and snip the hard ends off the daylily buds. Cut everything into short strips. Strain and keep 500 ml of the daylily soaking water; discard the rest.
2. Toss the pork with the Shaoxing wine, the marinade salt and 0.5 tbsp of the starch. Refrigerate for 10–15 minutes, until the surface is evenly coated in a thin layer. Stir the vinegar and white pepper together in a small bowl and set aside for just before serving.
3. Put the chicken stock, daylily soaking water, ginger and the white part of the green onion in a pot. Heat over medium-high for 5–7 minutes, until the soup starts to bubble. Add the shiitake, wood ear, daylily buds, tofu and light soy sauce. Simmer over low heat for 5 minutes, until the ingredients are heated through and the shiitake strips are soft.
4. Stir the thickening starch and 60 ml of cold water together. Pour it in a few additions while stirring the soup, and cook over low heat for 1 minute, until the soup turns glossy and lightly coats a spoon. Stir up the starch from the bottom of the bowl before each pour, so the last pour is not too thick.
5. Add the pork in separate pieces and nudge them apart. Cook over low heat for 2–3 minutes, until all the pork has turned white and the thickest piece reaches 71°C (160°F). Keep the soup at a gentle bubble so the pork does not clump.
6. Pour in the egg in a thin stream. Leave it for 20 seconds so the ribbons set, then push them gently apart. Cook for another 30–60 seconds, until the egg is fully cooked and the soup reaches 74°C (165°F). Turn off the heat and stir in the vinegar and white pepper. Taste and adjust the salt, drizzle with toasted sesame oil, scatter the green part of the green onion and serve right away.

## Key points

- **White lumps of starch in the soup**: dry starch hit by heat directly gels on the outside first and traps the inside (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Mix it with cold water and keep stirring the soup as it goes in.
- **Egg ribbons are fine and broken, and the soup is cloudy**: the egg was stirred apart as soon as it hit the pot. [Protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) takes time. Let the egg set first, then push it gently apart.
- **Heat is still there but the sour fragrance is faint**: the vinegar and pepper went in too early and their volatile aromas left with the steam. Add them after turning off the heat; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).
