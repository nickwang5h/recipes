The "tiger skin" of tiger-skin green peppers is the wrinkled, blistered, brown-spotted skin that forms where the pepper skin presses against a hot pan. Oil fills the gaps between the skin and the pan, and light pressing and turning let every part touch the pan; the flesh softens while the surface picks up a fried fragrance (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Thin-skinned long green peppers make it easier to get soft, tender flesh.

The sauce goes in after the tiger skin has formed, so water does not enter the pan too early and stew the peppers in liquid. Sugar, light soy sauce and black vinegar make a sweet-sour sauce, and the vinegar gets only a short toss at the end so its sourness stays bright (see [Acid: Balance, Softening and Stability](/principles/acid/)). The garlic also goes in only after the peppers are fried, and the sauce follows as soon as you smell it (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).

## Steps

1. Remove the stems from the peppers, wash them and wipe the surface dry. Cut each one lengthwise into three or four strips. To make them less hot, remove the white membrane and seeds inside. After cutting, wipe out any water in the hollows.
2. Mince the garlic finely. In a small bowl, stir the sugar, light soy sauce and black vinegar together, breaking up the sugar grains as much as you can. Do not add extra salt yet.
3. Pour the vegetable oil into the pan and heat over medium heat until the oil flows freely but has not started to smoke. Add the peppers, skin side down where possible, spread in a single layer so every piece touches the pan.
4. Press down the curling pieces gently with a spatula, and turn them over and move them around from time to time. Fry for about 2 minutes, until the skin has brown spots and wrinkles and the flesh begins to soften. If the wrinkling is not obvious, fry a few minutes more; if any spots turn black, lower the heat.
5. Push the fried peppers to the edge of the pan, turn the heat to low and use the oil in the pan to stir-fry the garlic until fragrant. As soon as the garlic smells fragrant and is still pale, toss the peppers back in.
6. Pour in the sweet-sour sauce, turn the heat to medium-high and stir-fry for about 30 seconds. Once the sauce bubbles, the sugar has dissolved and the peppers are coated in a glossy layer of sauce, stop; do not reduce it until the sugar strings or the bottom of the pan is dry and sticky.
7. Taste a small piece of pepper, and only if it really tastes under-seasoned add a little salt, toss and plate. The peppers should bend easily, with wrinkled skin rather than large black patches.

## Key points

- **Soft peppers but no tiger skin**: the pieces were wet, overlapping, or sauced too early, so the surface could not heat up (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Wipe them dry, fry them in a single layer, and season only after the wrinkles and brown spots appear.
- **Garlic burnt, peppers not yet fried**: finely minced garlic cannot survive the time the peppers need (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Fry the peppers first and the garlic after.
- **Sauce salty and sticky, sour aroma weak**: the seasoning sauce was cooked too long, so the water is gone and the vinegar's fragrance has dispersed (see [Acid: Balance, Softening and Stability](/principles/acid/)). Finish with a short toss and plate; taste before deciding on extra salt.
