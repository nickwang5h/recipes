Longjing shrimp is a famous Hangzhou dish: separate, white, tender shrimp with the fresh scent of green tea and only a thin glossy film of sauce on the plate. It rests on two things: a thin coating that protects the shrimp, and oil that is not very hot, so the shrimp cook gently.

The egg white and starch in the coating set and [gelatinize](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) as soon as they heat, forming a slippery skin around each shrimp. With the oil held at 120–130°C, the shrimp's [proteins](/principles/protein-coagulation/) set slowly instead of tightening all at once and squeezing out water; above about 140°C the shrimp start to yellow and toughen. The tea goes in last, so its aroma spends little time in the hot wok (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)).

## Steps

1. Rinse the shrimp and drain them, spread them on paper towels and press another layer of towels on top to dry them. The drier the surface, the better the coating holds.
2. Put the shrimp in a bowl with the salt and egg white and stir by hand in one direction for about 1 minute, until the shrimp feel slightly tacky and springy. Sprinkle in the 3 tbsp starch and mix until every shrimp has a thin coat. Cover and refrigerate for at least 2 hours so the coating clings.
3. About 10 minutes before cooking, make the tea: put the leaves in a cup, pour on 60 ml of water at about 85°C and steep for 1 minute, until the leaves unfurl and the liquid is pale green. Strain, keeping the leaves and the tea separately.
4. Stir 2 tsp of the oil into the shrimp so they separate easily in the wok. Heat the rest of the oil in a wok over medium heat to 120–130°C on a thermometer.
5. Add the shrimp and separate them at once, gently, with chopsticks. Cook for about 1 minute, until they curl and turn white and opaque all through, then lift them out with a slotted spoon to drain. The oil should only bubble finely, not crackle; if it heads towards 140°C, turn the heat down.
6. Pour off the oil, leaving about 1 tbsp, and turn the heat to high. Return the shrimp, pour the Shaoxing wine down the side of the wok, add the tea leaves and 2 tbsp of the tea, and toss quickly. Stir the slurry, pour it in and toss for about 30 seconds, until the juices thicken and coat the shrimp with a gloss. Push a thermometer into the centre of the largest shrimp; at 74°C (165°F) take it off the heat, and serve with a small dish of vinegar.

## Key points

- **Shrimp turn yellow, hard and shrunken**: the oil was too hot, so the proteins set too far and squeezed out water (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Watch the thermometer while passing the shrimp through oil and keep it under 140°C; the return to the wok is only to combine the flavours, so toss a few times and serve.
- **Coating falls off, shrimp stick together**: the oil was too cool, so the coating washed off before it set; shrimp that were not dried or chilled also hold their coating poorly (see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Heat the oil to 120°C before adding the shrimp and separate them straight away.
- **Tea tastes bitter, or there is hardly any tea scent**: water that is too hot or steeping too long draws out bitterness; tea added too early loses its aroma in the hot wok (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Steep for 1 minute in water below boiling, and add the leaves and tea only in the last step.
- **A pool of water on the plate**: the shrimp were not dried, or too much tea went in. The tea only needs to moisten the shrimp; the slurry takes up the rest of the liquid.
