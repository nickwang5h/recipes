Millet congee needs only grain and water to turn silky. The grains absorb water and soften, the starch granules swell with heat, and some of the starch escapes into the liquid so it slowly thickens (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). 100 g of millet to 2000 ml of water is a fairly thin starting ratio. In an ordinary pot, simmer with the lid ajar and check the thickness as it cooks.

The bottom of the pot overheats more easily than the surface, so stir up the grains that settle every so often (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). When the grains are soft and a ladle lifts the grains and liquid together, it is done.

## Steps

1. Put 100 g of millet in a bowl, add clean water, stir gently once and pour off the dusty water. Pick out any debris. Don't rub the grains or rinse until the water runs fully clear.
2. Choose a larger pot and add 2000 ml of drinking water, leaving room below the rim because the foam will rise later. Bring to a steady boil over high heat.
3. Add the drained millet and push a spoon around the bottom once to spread the grains. When the liquid returns to a boil, keep boiling over high heat for 6–10 minutes, nudging along the bottom a few times so the grains don't settle into a layer.
4. Turn the heat to medium-low or low and cook for 15–20 minutes with the lid set ajar. Keep small bubbles on the surface. If the foam climbs close to the rim, lower the heat or take the lid off. Stir the bottom now and then, but don't stir hard all the time.
5. Scoop out a few grains to check: they should have swollen, fall apart under light pressure and have no hard centre. If they are still hard, keep cooking gently. If the congee is already thick but the grains aren't soft, add a little hot water, stir and cook on.
6. Once the grains are soft, check the liquid: when you ladle it up, grains and liquid should flow down together, and it shouldn't separate into grains with clear water floating on top after a moment. If it seems thin, uncover and cook a little longer. When it reaches the thickness you like, turn off the heat and let it cool slightly in the bowl.

## Key points

- **Scorched bottom, burnt smell**: the grains sink and the bottom stays pressed against the hot pot (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Keep a gentle simmer and stir the bottom now and then. If it has already scorched, pour the congee from the top into a clean pot and don't scrape the burnt layer.
- **Foam lifts the lid and the congee boils over**: starchy liquid builds up more and more foam, and high heat with a tight lid makes it harder to settle (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Use a larger pot, lower the heat and leave the lid ajar. No oil is needed to break the foam.
- **Grains still have a hard centre but the liquid has thickened first**: the water evaporated faster than the grains absorbed it and softened (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Add hot water and keep cooking until the grains are soft. Judge by the grains. The congee also thickens as it cools, so leave a little fluidity when you take it off the heat.
