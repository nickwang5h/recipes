Hollandaise (*Sauce hollandaise*) is a warm egg yolk and butter sauce, often served with poached eggs, asparagus or fish. It should be thick yet pourable, spreading into a smooth layer when poured, with acidity that brightens the heaviness of the butter.

It is an [oil-in-water emulsion](/principles/emulsification/): the butterfat is whisked into fine droplets dispersed in a water-based liquid, and the lecithin and proteins in the yolks help keep the droplets stable. Whisking over gentle heat thickens the yolk base; heat it further, until [proteins coagulate extensively](/principles/protein-coagulation/), and grains appear and the sauce is also prone to separating.

## Steps

1. Warm the clarified butter. Keep it fully liquid at about 50°C (120°F); do not use butter that has just come off the heat. Set out a metal mixing bowl and a whisk, with a small bowl of cold water beside them.
2. Set up the water bath. Heat water in a small pot until it bubbles gently, then turn the heat to low and set the mixing bowl on top, with the bottom of the bowl not touching the water; the bowl must be easy to lift away at any moment.
3. Thicken the yolk base. Put the yolks, the water in the recipe and the vinegar in the bowl and whisk continuously for about 1–2 minutes, sweeping the bottom and sides of the bowl. When the mixture has turned paler and the liquid falling from the lifted whisk briefly leaves a ribbon trail, move the bowl off the water bath.
4. Start the emulsion. Drip in only a little warm butter at first, whisking nonstop as you add it; once the butter has fully blended in and the surface regains an even sheen, continue adding. You can steady the bowl on a damp cloth so that one hand can pour while the other whisks.
5. Add the rest of the butter. Once the sauce is stable, switch to a thin stream, still whisking constantly. Let each addition of butter blend in before adding the next; if the sauce gets stiffer and stiffer and the groove left by the whisk does not close, whisk in a little water until smooth before adding more butter.
6. Season and serve. Add the salt, white pepper and a little cayenne, then add the lemon juice a little at a time and stop when the acidity is clear on the tongue. Adjust with a little water to a consistency that flows off a spoon and pour it at once over hot food.

## Key points

- **Oil on the surface, sauce separated in layers**: the butter went in too fast, or there was too little water phase, and the [emulsion](/principles/emulsification/) broke. Stop adding butter, put a little warm water in a clean bowl, whisk the broken sauce into it drop by drop, and speed up again once it is smooth.
- **Grains like scrambled egg appear**: the yolks overheated in one spot and [protein coagulated](/principles/protein-coagulation/) too far. As soon as small grains appear, take it off the water bath and add a little cold water to cool it; once there are clear egg curds, start over, since straining out the grains will not restore the original texture.
- **Sauce too thick to pour**: the oil droplets are crowded, or the butter has started to set as it cooled. Loosen it with a little warm water; if it has already turned cold and firm, stir it gently back to liquid over warm water to keep the [emulsion](/principles/emulsification/), and do not put it directly on the stove.
- **The yolks are not fully cooked**: for pregnant women, older people and children, use pasteurized yolks. Use the sauce as soon as it is made; do not hold it warm for a long time.
