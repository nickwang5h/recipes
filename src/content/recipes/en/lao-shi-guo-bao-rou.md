Old-style sweet and sour crispy pork has a crisp crust and a clear sauce, and it comes off the heat with a sharp vinegar fragrance. The sauce is made from white vinegar and sugar. Potato starch is settled into a wet batter that coats the pork; in the heat it first gelatinizes ([starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)) and then keeps drying out into a crisp shell. The pork is sliced thin and even so the coating and the centre are done together.

The point of this dish is a thin sauce and a fast toss. How sour it tastes depends on the acidity of the vinegar (see [Acid](/principles/acid/)): 55 g of 5% white vinegar and 55 g of 9% white vinegar bring in very different amounts of acid. Add the pork as soon as the sauce is thick enough to cling, so the crust has little time to soak up liquid again.

## Steps

1. Stir the potato starch with the soaking water and leave it for about 60 minutes, until a thick white layer has settled at the bottom and the water above is clear. Slowly pour off the clear water and keep the wet starch at the bottom.
2. Trim the membrane from the pork loin and cut it into even, large slices about 3 mm thick. Toss with the marinating salt and the Shaoxing wine, cover and refrigerate for 15 minutes. Drain off any extra liquid when you take it out.
3. Mix the sugar, white vinegar, light soy sauce and the sauce salt. Cut the green onion, ginger and optional carrot into fine shreds, slice the garlic and cut the cilantro into sections. Set them out in the order they go into the pan.
4. Mix the pork slices with the wet starch, 5 ml water and 3 ml oil, and spread them out piece by piece. The batter should coat the pork thickly and flow down slowly when you lift a slice. If it is too dry, add a little water. If it is too thin, let it settle again and pour off the extra water.
5. Heat the frying oil to about 150°C (300°F) and add the pork slice by slice. Fry in batches for about 1–2 minutes, until the crust has set and the pork is cooked through, then lift out. Before lifting, check the centre of the thickest slice; if it is still raw, fry a little longer. A set crust does not mean the pork is cooked.
6. Once the oil is back to about 150°C (300°F), return the pork in batches for about 20–30 seconds, until the crust is dry and crisp and a pale golden colour, then lift out. Spread the pieces out to drain and let the steam escape; do not pile the hot pork in a heap.
7. In a clean pan, heat the oil for the sauce and stir-fry the ginger and garlic briefly until fragrant. Pour in the sweet and sour sauce and bring it to a boil, reducing until it is clear and just coats a spatula. Add the pork, green onion, carrot and cilantro and toss quickly, about three turns, until thinly coated, then plate. Scatter with sesame seeds if you like.

## Key points

- **The wet batter slides off the pork**: the water on top was not fully poured off, so the batter is too thin (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Let it settle before pouring off the water, and don't leave a pool of marinade on the pork.
- **The crust has colour but the pork is raw**: the slices are too thick or too many went in at once (see [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/)). Slice thin, fry in batches, cook the pork through in the first fry, then return it briefly.
- **The crust goes soft and sauce pools on the plate**: too much sauce, or the sauce is too thin, so the crust soaks up liquid again (see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Reduce the sauce until thick, coat with only a thin layer and serve at once.
- **The sourness is weak, or so sharp it is hard to eat**: the vinegar has a different acidity but the same weight was used (see [Acid](/principles/acid/)). Check the acidity on the vinegar label first. If you switch to a lower-acid vinegar, also allow for the extra water it brings.
