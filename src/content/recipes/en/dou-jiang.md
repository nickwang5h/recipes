Soy milk is a milky white drink ground from soybeans, often served at breakfast with [youtiao](/recipes/you-tiao/). The soaked beans are ground up, so protein and fine particles go into the water, and the oil is also dispersed into tiny droplets, which is why it looks like milk (see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)). Strain out the coarse bean pulp, and the milk that is left is fine and smooth.

Grinding and cooking are two separate things. The blender only breaks up the beans; the raw milk still has to be heated steadily before it is safe to drink. Heating also changes the structure of the soy proteins (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). When the foam on top of the pot rises, check whether the liquid under the foam is really boiling before you start counting the cooking time.

## Steps

1. Pick out any soybeans with mould spots or insect holes, wash the rest and put them in 600 ml of soaking water. Soak them in the refrigerator for 12 hours, until the beans are clearly swollen and there is no dry, hard core in the centre when you split one. Pour off the soaking water and rinse once more.
2. Put the soaked soybeans and 700 ml of grinding water in a blender and blend for 2–3 minutes, until the beans are completely broken up and the milk is an even milky white. If the blender is too small, blend in batches, leaving room in each batch for the liquid to move.
3. Set a sieve over a large bowl and line it with clean fine cheesecloth. Pour in the soy milk and strain for about 3–5 minutes, until most of the liquid has run through, then gather the corners of the cloth and press gently. Rinse the pulp with the remaining 700 ml of grinding water in two portions, pressing each time until the bag no longer drips steadily, and combine all the filtered liquid.
4. Pour the filtered liquid into a heavy-bottomed pot of at least 3 L and heat over medium heat for about 8–10 minutes. Stir slowly with a spoon along the bottom of the pot until the milk under the foam is also boiling steadily. When the foam rises close to the rim, lower the heat first to leave room for boiling.
5. From the moment it really boils, turn to low heat and cook for 20 minutes, keeping the milk at a gentle bubble and watching the bottom of the pot. Cooked soy milk has a clear bean flavour with no raw bean taste, and there are no burnt bits stuck to the spoon.
6. If you are using sugar, add it and stir for about 1 minute, until the sugar has completely dissolved, then turn off the heat. Divide into bowls to drink. Pour what you are saving for the next meal into a shallow container and cool it in a cold water bath for about 20–30 minutes, until it no longer gives off much steam, then refrigerate; the whole cooling process should take no more than 2 hours.

## Key points

- **It feels gritty when you drink it**: the coarse particles were not strained out. Grind until no whole beans remain and then strain through fine cheesecloth; the oil droplets dispersing in the soy milk is [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/), but coarse pulp can only be removed by straining.
- **The bottom is scorched and the whole pot smells burnt**: the milk overheated locally at the bottom (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Use a heavy-bottomed pot and stir along the bottom while heating; if it has already scorched, switch pots and pour out the upper layer of milk without scraping the bottom.
- **You turned off the heat at the first bubbles and it still tastes of raw beans**: the foam arrives earlier than a full boil, and if you go by the foam alone, [protein denaturation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) is not finished. Wait until the liquid is rolling, then cook it on low heat for the full 20 minutes.
