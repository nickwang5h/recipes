Congee needs only rice and water to turn out soft and thick. The starch in the rice absorbs water in the hot liquid and gelatinizes (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)): the grains swell and burst open, and some of the starch moves into the water, joining rice and water that were separate. For a plain white congee, leave out the oil mixed into the rice.

The ratio of rice to water is calculated by the volume of dry rice. 160 ml of rice with 1600 ml of water is a starting point of about 1 to 10. A wide pot or a lid left open speeds evaporation and makes the congee thicker; if you double the amount, switch to a pot large enough to hold it. How long it takes to come to a boil depends on the pot, the heat and the starting water temperature (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

## Steps

1. Measure the dry rice and rinse it, draining off the rinsing water. If using vegetable oil, mix the oil into the rice so each grain has a thin coating, and put it in the pot straight away.
2. Put the rice and 1600 ml of water in a heavy-bottomed pot, leaving enough room in the pot for the boiling foam. Push gently along the bottom with a spoon to spread apart any grains that have gathered in one place, and heat over medium-high heat.
3. When the water starts to roll and white foam rises, turn to low and leave the lid ajar or off. Keep fine bubbles rising steadily and do not let the rice liquid surge over the edge of the pot. If the foam pushes the lid up, lift the lid and lower the heat first.
4. Keep cooking for about 40–60 minutes, stirring the bottom now and then, and checking more often later as the congee thickens. Push the spoon gently along the bottom to bring up rice that has settled. If the water clearly drops while the centre of the rice is still hard, add boiling water and keep cooking.
5. Scoop out a few grains and press them open. When they crush easily with no hard core, the liquid is milky white and cohesive, and there is no chalky hardness of raw rice in the mouth, turn off the heat. If you like the grains to hold their shape, this state is enough; if you like it softer, cook longer and stir gently.
6. Turn off the heat and stir evenly, then check the final thickness. A spoonful should flow back into the pot in a continuous stream and level out quickly. If it is too thick, stir in a little boiling water; if too thin, cook gently a while longer. Let the bowl cool a little, since the congee holds its heat and loses it slowly; try a small spoonful before eating.

## Key points

- **Scorched at the bottom, still thin on top**: after the rice settles, one spot is heated too long. [Heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/) is uneven; a heavy-bottomed pot, low heat and stirring along the bottom reduce scorching. If it has scorched, move the upper layer to a clean pot and do not scrape the scorched layer.
- **Boils over as soon as it rolls**: gelatinized rice liquid foams easily and there is not enough room in the pot. Watch the foam: if it rises high, lower the heat and lift the lid, and do not go by the clock alone.
- **Too thick after standing**: the rice keeps absorbing water, and cooling also tightens the structure of the [gelatinized starch](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Leave a little room to flow when serving, and thin it back with water to the thickness you like when eating.
