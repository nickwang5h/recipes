Butter-seared shrimp are salty and sweet, with a strong peppery bite and the milky flavour of butter clinging to the shells. Light soy sauce, rice wine and sugar make a thin sauce, and the butter goes in last. With the back of the shrimp cut open, the sauce reaches the meat easily; keeping the shells on keeps the fragrant oil and pepper on the shells.

Sear in vegetable oil first, and add the butter only once the shrimp start to change colour, so the milk solids spend little time in the hot pan and are less likely to burn bitter. Shrimp that are dried on the surface sear more fragrantly (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)); once the meat goes from translucent to opaque it cooks very quickly, and if you cook it longer the [proteins](/principles/protein-coagulation/) set too far and the shrimp turn dry and hard.

## Steps

1. Remove the heads from the shrimp, cut through the back shell with scissors and pull out the vein, leaving the shells on. Thaw frozen shrimp in the refrigerator first, and after preparing them, blot them dry with paper towels and wipe away any water pooled in the cut backs.
2. In a small bowl, mix the light soy sauce, rice wine and sugar, stirring until the sugar has mostly dissolved. Add only a small part of the salt for now, and taste once the sauce has thickened; cut the butter into small pieces and have the black pepper freshly ground.
3. Heat the pan, add 45 ml of vegetable oil, and when the oil spreads freely turn the heat to medium-high and add the shrimp. Spread them in a single layer with the bellies against the pan; if the pan is small, cook in batches, but still divide the oil across the whole dish.
4. Sprinkle in the black pepper, using half and saving the rest for when you taste. Turn the shrimp to the other side and cook until the shells on both sides have changed colour, the meat along the cut backs starts to turn white and there is no large pool of water at the bottom of the pan.
5. Turn the heat to medium, add 7 g of butter, and turn gently until it melts, then pour in the sauce right away. Make sure every shrimp is coated with the sauce, and begin reducing only once the meat at the thickest part of the cut back is opaque.
6. Turn the heat to high for about 15 seconds and reduce the sauce until it thinly coats the shells, with a little flowing sauce left at the bottom of the pan. Insert a thermometer into the centre of the shrimp; at 74°C (165°F) they are done, and if not, keep heating. Once there, taste, add more pepper or salt, and plate right away.

## Key points

- **Butter turns black and the sauce is bitter**: the butter went in too early and stayed in hot oil too long (see [The Maillard Reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Sear the shrimp first and pour in the sauce as soon as the butter melts; throw out burnt black butter and start over.
- **Shells are red but parts of the meat are still translucent**: the shell changes colour first and the centre has not caught up (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Do not go by colour alone; measure the temperature at the thickest part of the cut back. The 15 seconds of reducing is only a guide.
- **Pepper overpowers the shrimp**: freshly ground pepper releases its aroma quickly (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). One tablespoon is already a heavy dose of pepper, so add it in portions; stop when the sauce is clearly peppery.
