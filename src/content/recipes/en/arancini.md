*Arancini* are Sicilian fried rice balls, with a crisp shell, soft saffron rice, and a filling of meat sauce and peas in the centre; this is the common traditional version. One batch makes about 12 large rice balls, two per person.

The rice balls hold their shape because of the stickiness released as the rice cooks; see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Cooled rice packs more easily, and the meat sauce also needs to be reduced to a consistency that piles on a spoon. In the deep fryer, the outer layer loses water and becomes a crisp shell, while heat slowly reaches the centre; see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/).

## Steps

1. Soften the onion, carrot and celery in the olive oil, add the beef, and break it up and stir-fry until it changes colour. Pour in the wine and cook until the smell of alcohol fades, then add the passata, tomato paste, peas, salt and pepper, and simmer over low heat for about 40 minutes, reducing until a line drawn through it does not close up right away. The beef must be cooked through to 71°C (160°F).
2. Spread the meat sauce in a shallow container, let it cool a little and refrigerate; when it is fully cold, mix in the diced cheese. The filling should pile up, with no thin liquid at the bottom of the container.
3. Cook the rice in boiling salted water until it is done but still has some bite, and drain well. Mix in the butter, grated cheese and saffron water, spread it thin to cool, and when it is warm, mix in the whole egg and the egg yolk; keep the white. Spread it in a shallow dish and refrigerate until the rice is completely cold and can be pressed together.
4. Divide the rice and the filling into about 12 portions each. With wet hands, take a portion of rice and press it into a bowl shape, put in a portion of filling, close the rice over it and shape it into a ball about the size of a tennis ball. The seal must be continuous, with no meat sauce or cheese visible on the surface.
5. Beat the two whole eggs with the reserved egg white. Roll the rice balls in the egg, then coat them in fine bread crumbs, pressing lightly so the crumbs stick, and check the seal in particular.
6. Heat the oil in a deep pot to 170°C (340°F) and fry the rice balls in batches, turning gently, until golden all over and the shell is hard; test the centre of one, which should reach 74°C (165°F). Lift out and drain, then let them steam off on a rack and rest a little before eating.

## Key points

- **They crack and leak filling when they go into the oil**: the rice or meat sauce was too wet, or the seal was too thin. Wrap only when cold, keep the filling in the centre, and check for cracks after wrapping. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **The shell has browned but the centre is still cold**: the rice balls are thick and the inside heats slowly. Hold the oil at 170°C (340°F), fry in batches, and judge by a centre temperature of 74°C (165°F); if the colour is right but the centre is not hot through, finish them in the oven. See [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).
- **The shell is greasy and soft**: too many went into one batch and the oil temperature dropped, or they were stacked when they came out and trapped steam. Leave space in each batch and wait for the oil to recover; spread them out to drain after lifting. See [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/).
- **Cooked rice has sat out on the counter for a long time**: the rice balls must be cold to wrap, but cooked rice left out for too long is not safe. Spread the rice and meat sauce thin and refrigerate them promptly, with no more than two hours total at room temperature; wrap and fry on the same day. For why cold rice shapes better, see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
