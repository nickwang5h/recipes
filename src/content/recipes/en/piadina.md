*Piadina romagnola* is a flatbread from Romagna, traditionally cooked on a clay plate called a *testo*. Lard, flour and water are the base, and some home recipes add a little milk and baking soda. Rolled thin, the lard version cooks up pale on the surface with scattered dark spots. The bread is pliable and a little chewy, and is eaten hot with ham, cheese or sautéed greens.

The dough has to be rolled thin without springing back the moment you let go (see [gluten](/principles/gluten/)). Lard makes the texture softer and richer, and after even kneading, a rest lets the dough stretch more easily. Cooking relies on direct contact with the pan, so a thin round must set quickly without the surface scorching before the inside is done (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). A heavy cast-iron pan at home can stand in for the clay plate.

## Steps

1. Mix the flour, fine salt and baking soda. Add the cooled melted lard, the milk and the warm water, and mix by hand into a mass. Do not rush to add water: knead until the dry flour is slowly absorbed, and if dry crumbs still will not stick, add water one teaspoon at a time.
2. Knead the dough for about 10 minutes, until the surface is smooth, a small piece can be pulled out without tearing at once, and there is no thick sticky dough left on your hands. Cover and rest for about 20 minutes so the surface does not dry and crack.
3. Divide the dough into 6 portions and shape each into a ball. Take one at a time and keep the others covered. Dust the board with only a thin layer of flour and roll into a round about 1.5–2 mm thick, as even as you can from centre to edge, and sized to lie flat in the pan.
4. Preheat a dry, heavy cast-iron pan over medium heat. Lay in the first round, flip after about 10 seconds, and cook another 10 seconds or so. A few scattered dark spots are right; if large black patches appear at once, lower the heat.
5. Prick any large bubbles with a fork and keep cooking until both sides are spotted, turning and flipping as you go so every part of the bread takes a turn against the pan. Each round takes about 3–4 minutes. Take it out when the centre is no longer sticky and soft, and the surface is dry but the bread can still bend.
6. Cook the remaining rounds the same way, and lower the heat as the pan gets hotter and hotter. Cut into pieces while hot, or fill with the prepared fillings. If you have to wait, cover loosely with a clean cloth so the bread does not turn hard.

## Key points

- **It shrinks back as soon as it is rolled and will not go thin**: the gluten has not relaxed, and forcing it only tightens it more (see [gluten](/principles/gluten/)). Cover and rest a little longer, roll from the centre outward, and use little dusting flour.
- **The surface is charred but the centre is still wet and sticky**: the pan is too hot, or the round is uneven in thickness (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Lower the heat, roll the remaining dough evenly, and turn the bread often so one spot does not stay on the pan.
- **The whole round is hard and dry, like a cracker**: the pan is too cool and the bread cooked too long, so water keeps escaping; too much flour taken in while rolling also makes it hard (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Preheat the pan fully before the bread goes in, take it out as soon as it is done, and eat it quickly.
