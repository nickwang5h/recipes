The lightness of *mousse au chocolat* comes from air beaten into it. Whisking makes protein unfold at the surface of the bubbles and wrap around the air, and as the chocolate and butter cool they firm up and hold those bubbles in the mousse. The mechanism is covered in [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).

This is the classic rich version with plenty of egg yolk and butter, aerated with egg white foam, and the coffee and orange liqueur set off the chocolate. The yolks bring together the water and fat in the chocolate base (see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)). The finished mousse should be spoonable, with fine bubbles still in the mouth, and it must chill for at least 2 hours.

## Steps

1. Whisk the egg yolks with 150 g of sugar until pale and thick enough that a ribbon falls from the whisk and leaves a trail, then stir in the orange liqueur.
2. Set the bowl over gently steaming water, with the bottom of the bowl not touching the water, and keep whisking the yolk mixture until it thickens and a thermometer reads 74°C (165°F). Move the bowl into cold water and keep whisking until it has cooled and a trail on the surface holds briefly.
3. Melt the chocolate with the strong coffee over hot water, stirring gently until no solid bits remain. Take it off the heat and stir in the soft butter a little at a time, to make a smooth, glossy chocolate mixture.
4. Mix the chocolate mixture into the yolk mixture until the colour is even. It should be soft and pourable, and only slightly warm when you touch the bowl. If it is too hot, the foam added later will deflate easily; if too cool, it will not mix evenly.
5. In a clean, grease-free bowl, whip the pasteurized liquid egg whites and a pinch of salt to soft peaks, sprinkle in 1 tbsp of sugar, and whip until the peaks stand while the foam is still fine and glossy. If the package says it is not suitable for whipping, choose another product that is.
6. First fold a quarter of the egg white foam into the chocolate base to lighten it. Add the rest in two parts, folding with a spatula from the bottom of the bowl upward and turning the bowl, and stop as soon as no white streaks are visible.
7. Divide among six small cups, cover and refrigerate for at least 2 hours, until the mousse no longer flows when you tilt the cup gently. Keep it chilled and take it out just before eating, so the finished mousse does not sit long at room temperature.

## Key points

- **The egg whites will not whip**: fat or egg yolk got into the whites and stopped bubbles forming, and some liquid egg whites are not meant for whipping. Wash and dry the bowl and whisk well, and check what the package says it is for (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **Heavy mousse with few bubbles**: the chocolate base was too thick, or the mixture was stirred hard and repeatedly so the foam broke. Loosen the base with a little foam first, then fold gently; chilling only sets the shape and cannot put the air back (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
- **Greasy, grainy chocolate base**: too much butter went in at once, or part of the yolk mixture overheated. Keep whisking over the water, add the butter a little at a time, and remake the base if hard bits like scrambled egg appear (see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/)).
- **The risk of raw egg**: once the mousse has set, raw egg is still raw, and neither chilling nor alcohol kills bacteria. Heat the yolk mixture to 74°C (165°F), and use pasteurized product for the egg whites, which are not heated again (see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).
