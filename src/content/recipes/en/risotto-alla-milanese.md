Risotto alla milanese is coloured golden with saffron and set on a base of beef marrow and butter. A good risotto spreads slowly when it is spooned onto the plate, with the grains still defined, soft to the bite with a little resistance.

The thick sauce comes from the rice itself. The grains absorb water in the hot stock and their outer layer undergoes [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/); some of the starch is released into the stock and thickens it, and gentle stirring helps the starch on the surface disperse. Risotto rice releases starch while keeping its grain, and butter and cheese make the texture rounder at the end.

## Steps

1. Cook the chopped onion in the butter for the onion and the beef marrow. Keep the heat medium-low and cook for about 5 minutes, until the onion is soft, translucent and slightly golden and the marrow has melted into the fat; don't let dark brown appear on the bottom.
2. Add the unrinsed rice and stir for 3–4 minutes. Coat the grains evenly in fat and cook until the surface is glossy and the edges look slightly translucent, keeping the grains whole and not browning them.
3. Pour in the white wine. Stir gently and cook until no liquid wine is left in the pot and the sharp smell of alcohol has clearly faded.
4. Add hot stock a ladle at a time (about 11–13 minutes). First let the liquid just cover the rice, keep a lively gentle boil, and push the bottom lightly with a wooden spoon; add more when the stock has reduced and the grains begin to show, so the bottom stays moist.
5. Add the saffron together with its steeping stock to the rice. Keep adding stock according to how much is absorbed, and cook for about 5 minutes, then taste a grain: the centre should have a slight bite with no hard white core; at this point the pot should still hold a thick, flowing sauce.
6. Off the heat, stir in the cold butter and cheese (*mantecatura*). Taste and add salt. When you shake the pot gently the rice should move in waves together with the sauce; if only a lump of rice shifts, stir in a little hot stock to loosen it, and plate in warm dishes right away.

## Key points

- **The rice is hard but the pot is already dry**: liquid was added too late and the [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) is not finished. Add hot stock to restore the gentle boil and keep tasting the rice to judge; before each addition just wait for the liquid level to drop, without reducing to a dry pot.
- **The rice is cooked but the sauce is thin**: too much liquid was added and the released starch is diluted. Near the end add stock in small amounts and reduce while pushing gently; with the right amount of liquid, the [gelatinized](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) starch forms a thick sauce that coats the grains.
- **The grains break and the whole pot looks like paste**: cooked too long or stirred too hard. The outer layer, after [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), is already soft and easily damaged, so use light pushing and shaking the pot instead, and take it off the heat while the centre of the grain still gives a little resistance; grains that have broken can't be restored.
- **It turns into a lump on the way to the table**: residual heat keeps the rice absorbing water, and the [gelatinized](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) sauce also thickens as it cools. Leave it a bit loose when taking it off the heat, warm the plates first, and serve as soon as it is stirred.
