*Polpette al sugo* are Italian home-style meatballs in tomato sauce, tender and juicy, usually served as a meat course with bread for the sauce. The milk-and-bread paste in the balls brings in moisture and also keeps the meat particles apart; the egg sets as it heats and holds the mixture together. Stop mixing once it is even. If the mixture is packed too tight or cooked too long, the protein network in the meat tightens and the texture turns hard (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).

Frying first and then simmering each do one job. Frying drives the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) on the drier surface, which brings out the meaty flavour. Then the tomato sauce heats gently and carries heat to the centre. The tomatoes cook in the same pan, so the brown bits left by the meat melt into the sauce too.

## Steps

1. Put the crustless bread and the milk in a small pot and heat over low heat until the bread is soft, mash into a paste with a fork, and let it cool off the heat. The paste must not be hot to the touch, or it will set the egg first.
2. Gently mix the ground beef, bread paste, onion, parsley, egg, olive oil, cheese, nutmeg, salt and black pepper. Stop when no lumps of bread paste can be seen and the ingredients are evenly spread; do not press it down or slap it repeatedly.
3. Gently roll into meatballs about 2.5 cm across, coat the surface lightly with bread crumbs, and pat off the extra crumbs. Meatballs of the same size cook through at the same time.
4. Heat the vegetable oil until a pinch of bread crumbs dropped in gives off fine bubbles at once, and add the meatballs in batches. Wait until the underside forms a thin brown crust and releases easily before turning, and fry until the sides are coloured. Do not crowd the pan.
5. Take the pan off the heat and spoon off most of the frying oil. Arrange the meatballs in one layer, and add the tomatoes with their juice and a little salt. Cover and simmer over low heat for about 20–25 minutes, turning gently halfway, until the sauce is thick and a little oil rises at the edges. Insert a thermometer sideways into the centre of a meatball and take them out at 71°C (160°F). If the sauce reduces away first, add a little hot water.

## Key points

- **The meatballs are rubbery and tight when cut**: too much kneading or too long on the heat joined the meat proteins too densely (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Mix only until even, do not squeeze when rolling, and stop the heat once cooked through.
- **They break apart when turned**: the surface had not set before they were moved, or the pan was too full and the balls sat in released water (see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)). Fry in batches, wait for the thin crust, and turn with a spatula supporting the ball.
- **Burnt outside, still raw in the centre**: frying only colours the surface, and the centre needs gentle moist heat to cook through (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)). Replace the burnt, bitter frying oil, then simmer in the tomato sauce over low heat until the centre temperature has been measured.
