Spaghetti aglio, olio e peperoncino (*Spaghetti aglio, olio e peperoncino*) seasons long pasta with garlic, chili and olive oil. The sauce is thin, the pasta is glossy, and a little rich, glossy sauce is left at the bottom of the plate; the first taste is garlic, and the heat comes after.

As the pasta cooks, the starch on its surface [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) and some of it dissolves into the water. When that water is added to the garlic oil and tossed with the pasta, the oil scatters into fine droplets spread through the water, and you have an [emulsified](/principles/emulsification/) sauce. The starch thickens the water, so the oil droplets do not easily merge again. The amount of water has to be just right: there must be water in the bottom of the pan for the oil to disperse, but not so much that it turns into a bowl of soup.

## Steps

1. Cook the spaghetti in boiling water for about 8 minutes. Salt the water, stir gently after the pasta goes in so the strands move freely, and when it is near the doneness on the package, taste a strand; once the centre still has a little resistance, get ready to lift it out.
2. Heat the olive oil, sliced garlic and chili over low heat for about 5 minutes. Gently swirl the pan so the garlic touches the oil evenly, and turn off the heat when the garlic smells fragrant and the edges of the slices have just turned pale gold, letting the residual heat keep releasing flavour.
3. Add a little pasta water to the slightly cooled garlic oil. Pour it in slowly to avoid splatter; a thin, cloudy layer of liquid appears at the bottom of the pan, and the colour of the garlic slices stops deepening.
4. Move the pasta into the pan of garlic oil. Set aside a portion of pasta water before you drain it, and use tongs to lift and drop the pasta so it mixes thoroughly with the oil and water.
5. Toss over medium heat for 1–2 minutes and adjust the amount of liquid. When the bottom of the pan looks dry and the pasta drags, add a little pasta water; if there is too much liquid, keep tossing to reduce it, until the pasta looks glossy, the liquid at the bottom is slightly cloudy and there are no large pools of oil on top.
6. Take the pan off the heat and toss in the chopped parsley. Taste and add salt, and plate while the sauce can still flow; the pasta should separate easily, with each strand coated in a thin layer of sauce.

## Key points

- **A pool of oil at the bottom of the plate and dry pasta**: the water phase is too small or the tossing was not enough, so the [emulsion](/principles/emulsification/) has not formed. Add a little hot pasta water and keep tossing, so the pooled oil disperses into the sauce; adding more oil on its own only makes the oil layer thicker.
- **The sauce is like thin broth and does not cling to the pasta**: too much pasta water went in, or it has too little starch (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Use the cloudier water from just before the pasta comes out, add it a little at a time, keep tossing in the pan to reduce it, and taste the pasta as you go to check it still has some bite.
- **It is glossy when it leaves the pan, then sticks after a while**: the pasta keeps absorbing water and the starch in the sauce (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)) thickens. Leave a little flowing sauce when you take it off the heat and eat as soon as it is tossed; after a short wait, add a little hot pasta water and toss again to loosen it.
