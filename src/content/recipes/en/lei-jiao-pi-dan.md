*Lei jiao pi dan* (also written with a different first character for *lei*) is a warm-to-cool side dish: pan-fried green peppers torn into strips and coated with soft century egg yolk, with sourness, saltiness and garlic mixed into the gaps. The peppers are fried soft first so they tear easily, and the century eggs are only pressed into coarse chunks, so there are strips of pepper and pieces of egg to chew, which has more texture than a mashed paste.

Peppers pressed flat sit more firmly against the pan and heat more evenly; see [Heat Transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/). The vinegar brings the sourness and the sugar rounds it off. The minced garlic, green onion greens and toasted sesame oil go in only after the heat is off, to keep their bright [aroma](/principles/aromatics/). Let the fried peppers cool a little before handling.

## Steps

1. Prepare the peppers. Wash the long green peppers, remove the stems, split them open from the side, remove the seeds and the harder inner ribs, and press them lightly flat on a board. Then dry the inside and outside with paper towels. Once split open the whole pepper doesn't puff up, and dried peppers splatter less when they go into the pan.
2. Prepare the mix-ins. Peel the century eggs and cut them into a few chunks. Cut the green onion greens into short sections of about half a centimetre, mince the garlic and cut the Thai chilies into short sections. Stir the light soy sauce, aged vinegar and sugar together in a small bowl until the sugar dissolves.
3. Fry the peppers. Add 15 ml oil to the pan, add the peppers and turn the heat to low. Press them lightly with a spatula so the skin lies against the bottom of the pan. Turn them over one by one and fry for about 8–10 minutes. Turn off the heat when they have collapsed, chopsticks go through easily and there are a few brown spots on the surface.
4. Cool the peppers. Lift them into a clean deep bowl and let them cool until you can handle them; don't tear peppers straight from the pan with your hands. If a lot of frying oil has come into the bowl, drain it off before pressing and mixing.
5. Press and mix the main ingredients. With two forks, tear the peppers into strips about half a centimetre wide, add the century egg and press lightly with the backs of the forks. Stop when the egg chunks are broken up and the yolk starts to coat the pepper strips, leaving some whole pepper strips and pieces of egg white.
6. Season. Pour in the dressing, add the green onion greens, garlic, Thai chilies and toasted sesame oil, and fold from the bottom with a fork so everything is coated. Taste a mouthful of egg with pepper strips; if the sour, sweet and salty are all distinct, it can go to the table.

## Key points

- **The peppers are burnt outside and still firm inside**: the heat was too high and the surface burnt first. Press the peppers flat as in [Heat Transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/), fry them soft over low heat, then brown them in a few spots.
- **Too sour or too sweet after mixing**: most of the dressing clings to the egg chunks, so tasting the dressing alone is misleading. Following the [balance of acid](/principles/acid/), start with the lower amounts and taste with the main ingredients; if it is overdone, dilute it with more cooked pepper or century egg.
- **Weak garlic and faint sesame oil**: when mixed in and cooked long with the peppers, the volatile [aromas](/principles/aromatics/) escape. Stir in the minced garlic, green onion greens and toasted sesame oil after the heat is off.
