*Polenta* is a common cornmeal staple in northern Italy. Fresh from the pot it is thick and soft, and can hold up stews and sauces; once cooled it can be sliced. This basic version uses only cornmeal, water and salt, and butter and cheese can be added to suit what it is served with. Served hot, it goes well with [Ragù alla Bolognese](/recipes/ragu-alla-bolognese/), letting the meat juices run into the porridge.

The corn grains take up water as they heat, the starch swells, and the water is slowly held in the thick porridge (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Thickening is only the start: the coarse meal keeps absorbing water and softening. Stirring breaks up the lumps that form when the meal goes in, and turns the porridge at the bottom up so one spot does not stay against the hot pot (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).

## Steps

1. Bring plain water to a boil in a deep heavy-bottomed pot large enough to hold it all, and add the salt. Leave headroom in the pot, because the thick porridge swells into large bubbles when it boils; do not fill it close to the rim.
2. Keep the water boiling and sprinkle in the cornmeal in a fine stream from your hand, while stirring constantly with a whisk in the other hand. Let only a small stream of meal fall into the water at a time, and do not dump in a whole bowl. After it is all in, keep stirring until no dry lumps are visible.
3. When the porridge thickens noticeably, switch to a long-handled wooden spoon, lower the heat, and keep it bubbling slowly. Keep stirring along the bottom, corners and sides of the pot, folding the thicker parts back into the whole; if the porridge spits violently, lower the heat further.
4. Cook for about 45–60 minutes, stirring often. If hard grains remain but the porridge is already too thick to push, add a little boiling water in batches, stir in and keep cooking.
5. Cool a small spoonful slightly and taste: the grains are soft, without the raw, rough taste of uncooked cornmeal. The wooden spoon should leave a groove for a moment, and the porridge should come together and pull away from the sides of the pot while staying soft enough to spoon. Once it looks like this, adjust the salt.
6. Spoon it hot into warmed plates, to go with a dish that has juices. To slice it, spread the part you will not serve into a shallow dish, cool, then refrigerate, and slice once the whole block has set; it turns hard as it cools.

## Key points

- **Dry lumps hidden inside**: the meal went in too fast, so the outside gelatinized first and wrapped dry meal inside (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Sprinkle it in finely while stirring constantly; press small lumps apart early, because you cannot count on long cooking to dissolve big ones.
- **It looks thick but tastes rough**: the coarse grains have not taken up enough water (see [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/)). Add boiling water and keep cooking until it is soft in the mouth; do not mix up the times for ordinary and instant meal.
- **Scorched and bitter on the bottom, while the top is fine**: the heat is too high, or the stirring does not reach the corners (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). If you smell scorching, spoon the top layer into another pot and do not scrape up the burnt bottom. Next time use a heavy-bottomed pot, low heat, and turn the bottom over.
