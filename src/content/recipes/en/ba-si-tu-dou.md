Candied potatoes have a crisp sugar shell with soft, fluffy potato inside, and when you lift a piece while hot, the sugar pulls into fine threads. It is two jobs: first fry the potatoes through and drive off the surface moisture, then cook the sugar syrup thick and coat them while hot. When the sugar has only just started to turn golden, [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/) adds a little aroma; cooked to a deep brown, the bitterness covers the sweet fluffy potato.

Whether the threads pull depends on the concentration and temperature of the syrup. While there is still a lot of water, the syrup just runs off the potatoes; once it thickens and has only just taken on a pale colour, take it off the heat and coat at once. The potatoes should stay warm and dry on the surface: if they are too cold, the sugar sets the moment it touches them; if the surface is wet, the water dilutes the reduced sugar again.

## Steps

1. Peel the potatoes and cut them into even small pieces, rinse the starch off the cut surfaces and drain. Blot off visible water, then toss with 30 g of dry starch until the surface is thinly white, and shake off the starch that did not stick.
2. Take a little of the prepared oil and wipe it over a dry plate, which you keep at hand. Have the sesame seeds ready too; once the sugar is coated, you must move in one go, and the sugar cannot be left in the hot pan waiting for the plate.
3. Add the frying oil to the pot, and when it is hot, add the potatoes in batches; the oil should bubble steadily without smoking. Turn slowly for about 5–7 minutes, until the surface is golden and a chopstick passes through the centre; if the large pieces are still hard, keep frying, and do not leave a raw core for the sake of appearance.
4. Lift out the potatoes, drain them well and spread them in a dry container to keep warm. Piled up, they steam each other wet; make sure no oil droplets or steam can be seen on the crisp shell.
5. In a separate clean pot, put 100 ml of water and the sugar and heat over low heat until the sugar has fully dissolved and the liquid is clear. Once it boils, stir as little as possible and swirl the pot gently so the heat is even; do not scrape the sugar grains on the pot wall back into the syrup.
6. Keep cooking the syrup thicker, and when the colour turns from clear to pale golden, the bubbles get fine and dense and it flows more slowly, take the pot off the heat. Do not wait for brown, and do not touch the syrup with your hand to test the thickness.
7. Pour in the warm potatoes and toss quickly for about 30 seconds, until each piece is coated in shiny sugar, then move them onto the oiled plate. Sprinkle with toasted sesame seeds; pick up pieces while hot, and when you gently pull them apart you will see fine threads. Let each piece cool a moment before eating.

## Key points

- **Golden outside but still hard inside**: the pieces were cut unevenly, or the [oil temperature](/principles/oil-temperature/) was too high. Cut the potatoes evenly, fry in batches, and lift them out only when a chopstick passes through the centre of the largest piece.
- **The sugar turns white and grainy**: undissolved sugar grains set off crystallization, and constant stirring after boiling makes it more likely (see [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/)). Dissolve the sugar completely first and stir little afterwards; for sugar that has recrystallized but is not burnt, take it off the heat, let it cool a little, then add water and dissolve it again.
- **No threads, or a bitter taste**: the syrup is still thin, or [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/) has gone too far. If it is thin, keep cooking it; if it has gone too far, make a fresh pot; take it off the heat at pale gold and use the residual heat to coat, and do not try to fix it with a thick layer of sugar.
