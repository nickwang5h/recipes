*Bò kho* is a Vietnamese spiced beef stew. The broth carries tomato, carrots, lemongrass and star anise, and it is often served with a baguette for dipping, or with rice noodles. Brisket has fine sinew, so after a slow stew the meat pulls apart along the fibres, and the broth clings slightly to the tongue because of the change from [collagen to gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).

Smashing the lemongrass and briefly stir-frying the whole spices lets their aroma go into the fat first and then spread through the broth; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). The carrots go in when the beef is nearly soft, so they do not cook to mush. The finished broth still flows, the tomato and meat juices have blended together, and the sinew in the beef breaks with one bite.

## Steps

1. Marinate the beef. Pat the beef dry, then toss it with the sugar, salt, the optional bouillon powder, pepper, five-spice powder, ginger, light soy sauce and 1 tbsp of the garlic. Cover and refrigerate for 1 hour, until the seasoning clings evenly to the chunks. Have the lemongrass, cassia, star anise and carrots ready separately.
2. Fry the spices. Heat the oil in a heavy 5 L stew pot over medium heat for about 1 minute, until the oil flows easily. Add the rest of the garlic, the lemongrass, star anise and cassia, and stir for 30–60 seconds, until the garlic is clearly fragrant and the spices have not blackened.
3. Brown the beef. Add the beef and its marinade and stir over medium-high heat for 6–8 minutes, until the surface of the chunks has changed colour and there are meat juices in the bottom of the pot. Stir in the tomato paste and cook for 1 minute, until the meat is evenly coated in red sauce.
4. Start the slow stew. Add the coconut water and beef stock and cook over medium heat for 8–10 minutes, until the surface is boiling, then skim off the foam. Cover, turn the heat to low and stew for 90–120 minutes, keeping small bubbles at the edge of the pot, until a fork goes into the meat and the fine sinew is nearly tender. Stir every 30 minutes, and if the liquid runs low, top it up with 50 ml from the held-back water.
5. Add the carrots. Stir the carrots into the broth, cover and cook over low heat for 20–30 minutes, until a chopstick pierces the carrot and the beef pulls apart with a light twist of a fork. If the sinew is still tough, cook 10 minutes longer at a time and check again, always keeping some broth in the bottom of the pot.
6. Adjust the broth. Lift out the lemongrass, star anise and cassia, then uncover and cook over medium-low heat for 5–10 minutes, until the broth is slightly thicker and coats the back of a spoon in a thin layer, yet can still be ladled for dipping bread. If it is already thick enough, skip the reducing and ladle it straight into deep bowls.

## Key points

- **The meat is cooked but the sinew is still hard to bite**: the collagen has not softened enough (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Keep stewing on low heat, and stop only when the sinew on the largest piece has softened.
- **The meat falls apart but the lean is still dry**: the boil was too hard and squeezed the water out of the meat (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Use brisket with its sinew and a little fat, keep the pot at a gentle bubble, and stop once the meat is tender.
- **The carrots break up in the broth**: they went in too early, or were stirred often after softening (see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Add them when the beef is nearly soft, and during the stew push them gently from the bottom of the pot.
- **The spices taste burnt and bitter**: the garlic and whole spices were fried too long (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Add the beef as soon as you smell the fragrance, and throw out any spices that have blackened and start again.
