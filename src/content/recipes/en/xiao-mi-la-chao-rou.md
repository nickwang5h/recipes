Pork with bird's eye chilies is directly spicy: thin, tender slices of pork, plenty of fresh chilies, and ginger, garlic and doubanjiang to lay a base flavour in the oil. The thin lean slices are cooked first and set aside, then the aromatics are fried, and finally everything is combined. That way the pork does not wait in the pan for the aromatics, which would let its [proteins denature and coagulate](/principles/protein-coagulation/) too tightly and turn dry and woody.

Fry the doubanjiang apart in the oil until the oil turns red before adding the fresh chilies, so the savoury paste and chili aroma join; see [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/). The light soy sauce, oyster sauce and doubanjiang are all salty, so taste only after everything is mixed. The finished dish is pork slices in a thin sauce, with the chilies just soft and little loose water at the bottom of the pan.

## Steps

1. Wash the bird's eye chilies, drain and cut on the diagonal into larger pieces. Cut 25 g each of ginger and garlic, 50 g in all; chop the doubanjiang fine, and put the sugar and chicken bouillon powder (optional) in a small bowl so they can go in quickly later.
2. Cut the lean pork against the grain into thin slices of similar thickness, add all the light soy sauce and oyster sauce, toss and marinate for 5 minutes. Do not add salt yet; use the marinade with its liquid, so you do not need to add more light soy sauce later.
3. Heat the pan, pour in 10 ml of oil, add the pork and spread it out; break it apart and stir-fry. When it changes colour, break open a thicker slice; once the centre is cooked and no longer looks raw, remove it to a plate and keep the meat juices on the plate.
4. Add the remaining 10 ml of oil to the pan, turn to medium heat and add the ginger and garlic. Stir-fry until there is less steam and the raw taste of the garlic has gone, then add the doubanjiang and fry over medium-low heat to loosen it, and move on when the oil turns red and the paste smells savoury.
5. Add the bird's eye chilies and stir-fry until the colour is bright and the cut edges are slightly soft. If the paste at the bottom begins to stick and burn, lower the heat first, and let the little water from the chilies carry the paste; do not keep heating an empty pan.
6. Return the pork and the juices on the plate to the pan, add the sugar and chicken bouillon powder (optional), turn to high heat and toss quickly. When the meat juices are hot again, the sugar has dissolved and the seasonings thinly coat the pork, taste a slice.
7. Add a little salt if needed, toss and serve. Do not keep stir-frying to get charred edges; lean pork that is already cooked dries out easily when heated again. The chilies should be cooked but still hold their shape, with the sauce staying on the slices.

## Key points

- **Pork is dry**: after being cooked it stayed in the pan while the ginger, garlic and doubanjiang were fried, and heating continued, so the [proteins denatured and coagulated](/principles/protein-coagulation/) and the meat lost its juices. Take it out first, and return it at the end only for a brief toss to season.
- **Doubanjiang is raw-tasting or burnt and bitter**: the pork went in before the paste had loosened, or the heat in the empty pan was too high (see [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/)). Wait for the oil to turn red and the paste to smell savoury before adding the fresh chilies and the meat juices.
- **Garlic is burnt, and it is hot without aroma**: the finely chopped ginger and garlic sat too long in hot oil (see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Add the doubanjiang as soon as they smell fragrant, combine as soon as the chilies are just soft, and add salt last.
