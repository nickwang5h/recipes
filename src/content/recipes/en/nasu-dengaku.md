Miso-glazed eggplant, *nasu dengaku*, pairs soft eggplant flesh with sweet and salty dengaku miso. The miso is first cooked with sugar, mirin and sake into a thick sauce that spreads easily. The eggplant is roasted soft separately, and only then glazed and broiled to brown the sauce. Dengaku miso is sweet by nature, and the sugar and mirin set off the salty savouriness of the miso. It suits a small serving with white rice.

The flesh softens as heat slowly reaches the centre, but a thick sauce browns quickly, so the two stages need separate heat (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)). Amino acids and sugar in miso take part in the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/), which gives the heated surface a roasted aroma. If the sauce goes on from the start, the outer layer often scorches and turns bitter before the flesh is soft. Shallow cross-hatching on the cut face lets the sauce cling, and the intact skin holds up the soft flesh.

## Steps

1. Set the oven to regular top-and-bottom heat at 200°C (400°F) and preheat for about 10 minutes, until the preheat indicator lights. Line a baking sheet with aluminum foil.
2. Trim the stems and halve the eggplants lengthwise. Score a shallow cross-hatch on the cut faces, with lines about 1 cm apart and about 5 mm deep, without cutting through the skin. Brush with oil right after cutting, spreading the whole 1 tbsp thinly over the cut faces and skins so that no oil pools in the cuts.
3. Place the eggplants skin side down on the baking sheet, apart from each other, on the middle rack. Roast for about 30 minutes, until the edges are slightly wrinkled, a bamboo skewer slides easily into the centre and the flesh collapses when pressed lightly. If the centre is still firm, roast for 5 more minutes at a time and test again. Get the flesh soft before you glaze it.
4. While the eggplants roast, stir the red miso, sugar, mirin and sake together in a small pot. Heat on the lowest setting, scraping and stirring constantly for 2–3 minutes, until the sugar dissolves and the sauce bubbles slowly in small bubbles. A spoon drawn through should leave a brief trail, and the sauce should still spread. Take it off the heat; do not cook it into a stiff lump.
5. Take out the soft eggplants and move the rack to about 15 cm from the top heating element. Turn on the broiler and preheat for 3 minutes so the top element is hot. Meanwhile, divide the dengaku miso among the eggplants and spread it over the cut faces in an even thin layer, not piled in the middle.
6. Return the eggplants to the oven and broil for 3–4 minutes, until the surface of the sauce bubbles and has scattered dark brown spots. Start watching the colour from the 2nd minute, and take them out as soon as spots appear. Sprinkle with toasted white sesame seeds and eat hot, scooping up the flesh and miso together with a spoon.

## Key points

- **The sauce is scorched but the flesh is still firm**: the sauce went on first, or the eggplant is too thick. In the first stage, go by a skewer sliding easily through the centre, and glaze only once the flesh is soft (see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/)).
- **The surface is black, hard and bitter**: the broiler is too close or the time too long, and the roasted aroma has turned to burnt bitterness. Take the eggplants out when you see scattered brown spots, without waiting for the whole surface to blacken (see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/)).
- **The miso clumps and the saltiness overpowers the eggplant**: the sauce was cooked too dry or spread too thick, so the concentrated salt gathers on the surface. Stop scraping and stirring on the lowest heat as soon as it spreads, divide it among all the eggplants, and add no extra soy sauce or salt (see [salt](/principles/salt/)).
