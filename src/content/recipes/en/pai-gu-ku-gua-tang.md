Pork rib and bitter melon soup sets the savoury ribs against the bitterness of the melon, and the dried shrimp add a flavour of the sea. The bitter melon goes in from the start and is simmered until soft, so its bitterness melts into the soup too. The ribs are simmered until the meat comes easily off the bone. Keep the heat low, with only small bubbles on the surface, and stir little so the soup stays fairly clear.

The ribs are first [blanched](/principles/blanching/) to clean the surface, then simmered in fresh water. The meat and the joints between meat and bone soften gradually because collagen turns into gelatin (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)), and that takes a long simmer. The dried shrimp provide [umami](/principles/umami/) and also salt, so at the end taste the soup before deciding how much to add.

## Steps

1. Chop the ribs into small pieces about 4 cm long and clear off bone chips. Put them in a narrow pot with 700 ml of cold water and the optional ginger slices. The water should cover the ribs. Heat until boiling and the scum gathers on top, then lift the ribs out and rinse off any particles.
2. Halve the bitter melon lengthwise, scoop out the seeds and the soft inner pith, and cut into strips about 0.5 cm thick. Rinse the dried shrimp quickly and drain.
3. Put the ribs and 1100 ml of water in the soup pot and bring to a boil over high heat. Add the bitter melon and dried shrimp, turn to low and cover. Keep small bubbles rising steadily at the edge of the pot, with the surface not boiling hard, and start timing the slow simmer.
4. At 120 minutes, lift out one piece of rib. If the meat comes away from the bone fairly easily, it is done. If it is still tough, keep simmering; the total time may reach 240 minutes. If the water has dropped noticeably, add hot water so it does not boil dry.
5. If you use an electric pressure cooker, use 1000 ml of water instead and put it in with the ribs, bitter melon and dried shrimp. Use the machine's soup program, and pressure-cook for about 45 minutes, then look at the meat. Let the pressure release naturally, and open the lid only once it has fully released. See whether the meat comes off the bone.
6. Prepare the salt and white pepper according to the soup left in the pot. Add a small amount of each, stir and taste, then add more gradually. If the dried shrimp taste clearly salty, you may add no salt. The bitter melon in the soup-pot version is already very soft, so lift it with a spoon when serving and stir little.

## Key points

- **Grey scum floats on the soup**: the surface was not cleaned in blanching, or the ribs lifted out were not rinsed well (see [Blanching and Removing Off-Odours](/principles/blanching/)). Change the water and simmer gently, and skim new scum promptly afterward.
- **The soup has simmered a long time but the ribs are still hard to bite**: the cut and the size of the pieces change how long it takes the connective tissue to soften (see [Collagen and Gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/)). Start looking at 120 minutes, and stop once the meat is soft; there is no need to fill four hours.
- **The dried shrimp are too strong and the soup too salty**: both the amount and the saltiness of the dried shrimp affect the soup (see [Umami](/principles/umami/)). Start with the middle of the range, and add salt according to the final soup volume and your tasting, rather than all at once from the starting water.
