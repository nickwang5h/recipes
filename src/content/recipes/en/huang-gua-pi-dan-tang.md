Cucumber and century egg soup is clean and fresh, with crisp cucumber slices and century egg carrying a little fried garlic aroma. The century egg and garlic are fried in oil until the edges dry out slightly and turn pale gold with a toasted fragrance, which is the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/); the thin cucumber slices go in last, and the heat is turned off as soon as the soup returns to a boil, so the crunch survives.

The garlic is smashed and cooked over low heat so its aroma can get into the oil (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). The thickness of the cucumber slices decides how long they need at the end (see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/)): a thick slice mixed in with thin ones is still raw when the soup returns to a boil, while the thin ones have been cooked soft waiting for it, so cut the slices evenly.

## Steps

1. Wash the cucumber, peel it if you like, and slice it into thin slices about 0.5–1.2 mm thick. You can shave the slices with a peeler; if cutting by hand, keep them as even as you can and do not mix a few thick pieces in with the thin ones.
2. Wash and chop the green onion. Smash the garlic, peel it and cut it in half. Leave the garlic pieces fairly large, so that frying slowly over low heat with the century egg brings out the aroma without easily burning black.
3. Peel the century eggs and cut each into 6–8 pieces. Blot the surface water off gently with paper towels. Keep the pieces as intact as possible, and keep any loose yolk to cook into the soup too.
4. Century egg is fragile, so after it goes in, wait until the underside has set before turning, so you do not crush it to a paste. Put the oil in the pan and heat over low heat, add the century egg and garlic, and turn gently for about 2 minutes, until parts of the surface are pale golden and you smell the garlic.
5. Add the water slowly along the side of the pan and bring to a boil over high heat. Push gently along the bottom to bring the pale juices stuck there into the soup; do not scrape in any burnt black, bitter bits.
6. Add the cucumber slices and nudge them apart so the thin slices separate. When the soup returns to a boil and the slices are hot through but still firm, turn off the heat right away; do not let the soup keep rolling.
7. Add part of the salt and the bouillon powder, stir, taste, and then add more salt if needed. Ladle the century egg, cucumber and soup into bowls together, sprinkle with the green onion and eat while the cucumber is still crisp.

## Key points

- **Garlic burnt black, soup bitter**: the garlic was cooked too long over low heat (see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/)). Add the water once it is pale gold; take out burnt black garlic, and start over if the base oil has also turned bitter.
- **Cucumber limp, slices unevenly cooked**: the slices are uneven, or they cooked too long after going in. Cut them to an even thickness according to [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/), and turn off the heat when the soup returns to a boil after the thin slices go in.
- **Only a boiled-egg taste, no fried aroma**: the century egg surface was too wet, or the water was added before it took on any colour, so the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) never happened. Blot off surface water first, fry over low heat to local pale gold, then add water to make the soup.
