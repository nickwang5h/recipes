Anhui cuisine here means the flavours of the Huizhou region, set against the mountains, hills and valleys of southern Anhui and neighbouring areas. Bamboo shoots, mushrooms and ham go into braises and stews. Cured fresh mandarin fish and hairy tofu take another route: the ingredient is fermented first, then fried, braised or steamed.

## Braises and stews: soft, but still whole

Anhui cooking often braises, stews and steams, and the "one-pot" dish (*yipinguo*) cooks many meats and vegetables slowly together in a single pot. The trouble is that each ingredient changes differently under heat: skin-on meat needs time, tofu breaks easily, and vegetables and bamboo shoots each have their own texture. So the cut, the preparation and the order into the pot decide whether everything ends up soft and unbroken at the same time.

Meat softens with moist heat. The connective tissue loosens slowly and [collagen turns into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), so the meat is tender and the broth silkier. Tender meat with little sinew only keeps losing water in a long stew, so decide when choosing which pieces can take it. Ham also brings its aged savoury saltiness into the broth; taste for salt once the broth has reduced. See [Umami](/principles/umami/).

At home this means plenty of liquid in the pot and steady heat; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). Bring it to a boil, then turn down to a gentle simmer; a heavy-bottomed pot is less likely to overheat in one spot on the bottom. When the ingredients are soft, lift them out with a spoon and stir as little as you can. If the skin-on meat is already soft but there is still a lot of sauce, take the meat out and reduce the sauce on its own. A home stove can do all of this; what matters is how you combine the ingredients and when you take it off the heat.

## Cured fresh mandarin fish: ripen first, then pan-fry and braise

Cured fresh mandarin fish, also called stinky mandarin fish, is salted and fermented first and then cooked. Its smell and taste have already changed before it goes in the pan, so the frying and braising that follow have three jobs: cook the fish through, fry in some fragrance, and coat it in the sauce. When learning it, it helps to treat "how the flavour forms" and "how the heat is handled" separately.

In [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/), microbes and enzymes break down proteins and flavour compounds, bringing new tastes and volatile aromas. A strong or faint smell alone doesn't tell you whether the fermentation went well. For home practice, start with a cured fresh fish product that comes with clear storage and cooking instructions, and put your effort into the frying and braising.

Drain the fish dry before frying, so the side touching the pan gradually loses water and develops the aroma of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). Turn it once the surface has set, then add the sauce and braise it through, without pushing it around with the spatula. Frying for fragrance and cooking the centre are two separate things: don't fry too dark at first, and at the end check that the thick part is opaque and flakes apart. An ordinary frying pan is enough to practise this. When reducing the sauce, spoon it over the fish; the fish stays whole more easily.

## Hairy tofu: fermented inside, fried outside

Hairy tofu is tofu whose surface is first allowed to grow fermenting mycelium, then fried in oil. During fermentation, enzymes gradually break down the soy protein, which changes the texture inside and adds taste compounds such as free amino acids; see [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/). Frying dries the surface and makes it fragrant, so the outside contrasts with the soft inside.

Tofu holds a lot of water. When it first touches the hot pan, the heat goes into evaporating water; once the surface has dried, it browns and fries to a fragrant crust more easily; see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). Lay the pieces too close together and the steam can't escape, so they colour more slowly. Turn them too often and you tear the outer layer before it has set, and the soft tofu falls apart.

At home, spread ready-made hairy tofu in a single layer in a frying pan. Turn it when the underside has formed a crust and the spatula can lift it, fry both sides golden, then season. A little sauce added for a short braise gives a moister outer layer; dipped straight in chili sauce, it keeps a drier surface. You can buy hairy tofu meant for frying and practise these two finishes. Making the ferment yourself takes separate control of the culture and the environment, so at home start with the frying.
