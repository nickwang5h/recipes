Anhui cuisine here means the flavours of the Huizhou region, set against the mountains, hills and valleys of southern Anhui and neighbouring areas. Bamboo shoots, mushrooms and ham go into braises and stews, while cured fresh mandarin fish and hairy tofu show another kind of change: the ingredient is transformed first, and then the heat for frying, braising and steaming is arranged.

## Slow-fire cooking and the combined pot: soft, yet keeping its shape

Anhui cooking often braises, stews and steams, and the "one-pot" dish (*yipinguo*) cooks many meats and vegetables together slowly in a single pot. The difficulty is that each ingredient changes differently under heat: skin-on meat needs time, tofu breaks easily, and vegetables and bamboo shoots each have their own texture. Cutting, preparation and the order of going into the pot decide whether you get several ingredients that are soft and still whole at the same time.

Moist heat gradually loosens the connective tissue in meat, and [collagen turns into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/): the meat softens and the broth becomes silkier. Tender meat with little sinew mostly just keeps losing water under long cooking, so you have to decide at the choosing stage which pieces suit a long stew. Ham also brings its aged savoury saltiness into the broth; judge the salt after the broth has reduced. See [Umami](/principles/umami/).

At home, slow-fire cooking means keeping enough liquid and steady heat in the pot; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). Bring it to a boil first, then turn down to maintain a gentle simmer; a heavy-bottomed pot more easily avoids local overheating on the bottom. Once the ingredients are soft, lift them out with a spoon and stir little; if the skin-on meat is already soft but there is still too much sauce, take the meat out and reduce the sauce. A home stove can do this work; what matters is the combination of ingredients and the state at which you stop the heat.

## Cured fresh mandarin fish: ripen first, then pan-fry and braise

Cured fresh mandarin fish, also called stinky mandarin fish, is salted and fermented first and then cooked. Its smell and taste have already changed before it goes in the pan, and the frying and braising that follow cook it through, add a fried fragrance and combine it with the seasoning sauce. When learning it, it helps to see separately "how the ingredient gets its flavour" and "how the finished dish is cooked".

In [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/), microbial activity and enzymes break down proteins and flavour compounds, bringing new tastes and volatile aromas. How strong or faint the smell is cannot by itself tell you whether the fermentation is sound; for home practice it is best to start with a cured fresh fish product that comes with clear storage and cooking instructions, and put your effort into the frying and braising that follow.

Pan-fry the fish after its surface has been drained dry, so the side touching the pan can gradually lose water and develop the aroma of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). Turn it once the surface has set, then add the sauce and braise it through, avoiding repeated pushing with the spatula. Frying for fragrance and cooking the centre are two separate stages: do not fry too dark in the first, and in the second check that the thick part is opaque and flakes apart. An ordinary frying pan is enough to practise this step; when reducing the sauce, spoon it over the fish, which makes it easier to keep the fish whole.

## Hairy tofu: a fermented inside, a fried outside

Hairy tofu is tofu whose surface is first allowed to grow fermenting mycelium, and is then fried in oil. In the fermentation, enzymes gradually break down soy protein, changing the interior texture and adding taste compounds such as free amino acids; see [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/). Frying makes the surface dry and fragrant, giving a contrast between the outer layer and the soft inside.

Tofu holds a lot of water, and when it first touches the hot pan the heat goes first into evaporating water; once the surface has dried, browning and fried aroma develop more easily; see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). If it is laid too close together in one go, the steam cannot escape and it takes longer to colour. Turning it often tears the outer layer, which has not yet set, and the soft tofu falls apart.

At home, spread ready-made hairy tofu in a single layer in a frying pan, and turn it when the underside has formed a crust and can be lifted by the spatula; fry both sides golden and then season. A little sauce added for a short braise gives a moister outer layer; served straight with chili sauce, it keeps a drier surface. If you buy hairy tofu meant for frying, you can focus your practice on these two finishes; making the fermented ingredient needs separate control of the culture and the environment, so for home cooking start with the frying.
