The fish and shellfish of the Fujian coast and the poultry and mushrooms of the interior give Fujian cooking several sources of savouriness. Methods differ by place; below, the stock-making, red yeast rice lees and sweet and sour fried dishes of Fuzhou cooking are the thread: savouriness needs a base, aroma needs layers, and texture comes from cutting and heat, each arranged on its own.

## Stock and quick poaching: make the base first, then protect the tenderness

Chicken stock poached sea clam pairs a pre-made stock with sea clam, and the heat for the stock and the heat for the clam are arranged separately. Poultry suits slowly drawing out flavour, but clam meat tightens under continued heat. Cook them together from start to end and the stock may turn rich while the clam is already tough. So the stock and the main ingredient each need their own heat.

The "clear" of a clear stock is only how it looks; the savouriness comes from taste compounds dissolved in the water. Glutamate and savoury nucleotides from different ingredients reinforce each other; see [Umami](/principles/umami/). Skin-on and sinewy ingredients also cook [gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/) into the stock and give it body. A stock can be clear and silky at once, so you can't tell from colour alone whether a pot of stock has flavour.

When making stock, keep it at a slight simmer and skim off the foam; it holds its clarity more easily. When poaching seafood, check whether the thickest part is cooked through; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). At home you can make a small pot of chicken stock first and then deal with the day's fish and shellfish. Buddha Jumps Over the Wall is the craft at the other end: many ingredients are prepared and then simmered together in a jar. At home, begin by learning how fast each ingredient cooks.

## Red yeast rice lees: bringing fermented aroma into braises

Red yeast rice lees come from rice wine brewed with red yeast rice; they smell of lees and are red. In dishes such as red lees eel and lees-braised pork belly, the lees are combined with the main ingredient and the liquid first, then braised until each reaches the texture it needs. What the lees add to the dish is brewed flavour that has already formed; once it is in the pot, the job is to spread it evenly.

During brewing the rice goes through saccharification and [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/), and the sugars, acids, alcohol and aromas together make up the taste of the lees. During braising, some of the volatile aroma leaves with the steam, and the proportions of the remaining flavours shift too; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). So take fish pieces off the heat as soon as they are cooked, and give pork belly enough time for the skin and connective tissue to soften.

A home cook can do the main process of lees braising; the hurdle is mostly the ingredient. Use red yeast rice lees meant for cooking, taste their saltiness, sourness and wine aroma first, then decide on salt and sugar. Fermented tofu of a similar red colour has the fermented base of a soy product, so if you substitute it, treat it as a different seasoning. When frying the lees, add the liquid as soon as you smell the aroma, so the fine bits of lees don't scorch in a dry pan.

## Scoring and fry-then-toss: shape, crisp shell and sauce controlled separately

One traditional mark of lychee pork is slices of pork scored and fried into a curled shape. The decorative cuts open the surface of the meat and change its thickness in places, so the meat shows its shape as it contracts under heat; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). Keep the depth of the cuts even and leave the bottom layer joined; cut all the way through and you get loose pieces that can't curl into a whole shape.

In frying, the surface dries into a shell while the centre of the meat slowly cooks; see [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/). Mix the sweet and sour sauce beforehand, and the meat goes back into the pan only for a brief toss. The water in the sauce moistens the crisp shell again, so as soon as a thin starch coating clings to the surface, the dish comes out of the wok; see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). This step depends entirely on getting the order of your prep right.

## Trying it at home

The site's [Lychee Pork with Pineapple](/recipes/li-zhi-rou/) is a home variation that pairs pineapple with pieces of pork; you can practise frying through, double-frying and coating with a thin starch sauce, while the traditional scored shaping has to be practised separately. [Drunken Pork Ribs](/recipes/zui-pai-gu/) fries the ribs hot and tosses them in a sweet, sour and garlicky sauce, which practises the finish. Both can be done on a home stove: put in less per batch, and the oil temperature holds better than it does by blindly turning up the heat.
