The fish and shellfish of the Fujian coast and the poultry and mushrooms of the interior give Fujian cooking different sources of savouriness. Methods differ by place; below, the stock-making, red yeast rice lees and sweet and sour fried dishes of Fuzhou cooking are the thread: savouriness needs a base, aroma needs layers, and texture is arranged separately through cutting and heat.

## Stock and quick poaching: make the base first, then protect tenderness

Chicken stock poached sea clam pairs a pre-made stock with sea clam, and the heat for the stock and for cooking the clam meat are arranged separately. Poultry suits slowly drawing out flavour, but clam meat tightens under continued heat. If you cook them together from start to end, the stock may become rich while the clam is already tough; the heat here has to look after the stock and the main ingredient separately.

The "clear" of a clear stock is how it looks; the savouriness comes from taste compounds dissolved in the water. Glutamate and savoury nucleotides from different ingredients can reinforce each other; see [Umami](/principles/umami/). Skin-on and sinewy ingredients also bring [gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/) into the stock and give it body. Clarity and silkiness can exist together; you cannot judge from colour alone whether a pot of stock has flavour.

When making stock, keep it at a slight simmer and skim off the foam, which makes it easier to hold a clear look; when poaching seafood, check whether the thickest part is cooked through; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). At home you can make a small pot of chicken stock first and then deal with the fish and shellfish of the day. Buddha Jumps Over the Wall shows the other end of the craft: many ingredients are prepared and then simmered together in a jar, and for home practice you can begin by learning to tell how fast the ingredients cook.

## Red yeast rice lees: bringing fermented aroma into braises

Red yeast rice lees come from rice wine brewing with red yeast rice, and carry the aroma of lees and a red colour. In dishes such as red lees eel and lees-braised pork belly, the lees are first combined with the main ingredient and the liquid, and then braised until each reaches the texture it needs. What the lees add to the dish is the brewed flavour already formed; the job once it is in the pot is to distribute it evenly.

During brewing the rice goes through saccharification and [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/), and the sugars, acids, alcohol and aromas together make up the taste of the lees. During braising, some of the volatile aroma leaves with the steam, and the proportions of the remaining flavours change too; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). So stop the heat promptly once the fish pieces are cooked, while pork belly needs enough time for the skin and connective tissue to soften.

A home cook can do the main process of lees braising; the hurdle is more the ingredient. Use red yeast rice lees that are meant for cooking, taste their saltiness, sourness and wine aroma first, and then decide on salt and sugar. Fermented tofu of a similar red colour has the fermented base of a soy product and, if substituted, should be treated as a different seasoning. When frying the lees, add the liquid once you smell the aroma, so the fine bits of lees do not scorch in a dry pan.

## Scoring and fry-then-toss: shape, crisp shell and sauce controlled separately

One traditional mark of lychee pork is the scored slices of pork fried into a curled shape. The decorative cuts open up the surface of the meat and change the local thickness, so that the meat shows its shape as it contracts under heat; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). The depth of the cuts must be even, leaving the bottom layer joined; if the cut goes through, you get loose pieces and it is hard to get a whole curled shape.

Frying dries the surface into a shell while the centre of the meat gradually cooks; see [Oil Temperature and Deep Frying](/principles/oil-temperature/). The sweet and sour sauce that follows is mixed first, and the meat goes back into the pan only for a brief toss. The water in the sauce will moisten the crisp shell again, so a thin starch coating clings to the surface and the dish comes out of the wok; see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). This step tests the order of your prep.

## Trying it at home

The site's [Lychee Pork with Pineapple](/recipes/li-zhi-rou/) is a home variation that pairs pineapple with pieces of pork; it lets you practise frying through, double-frying and coating with a thin starch sauce, while the traditional scored shaping has to be practised separately. [Drunken Pork Ribs](/recipes/zui-pai-gu/) practises the finish by tossing hot-fried ribs in a sweet, sour and garlicky sauce. Both can be done on a home stove, and cutting the amount per batch keeps the oil temperature up better than blindly adding more heat.
