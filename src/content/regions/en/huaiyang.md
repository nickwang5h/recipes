Jiangsu's network of waterways supplies fish, shrimp, crab, vegetables and soy products, all of them ready ingredients for fine preparation and light, fresh seasoning. This page covers the Huaiyang techniques of the Huai'an and Yangzhou area; Jiangsu cooking also has other local flavours, and the dishes of the whole province are not one taste.

## Knife work: cutting tofu into another texture

**Wensi tofu** is soft tofu cut first into slices and then into fine threads that spread gently apart in the soup. Here the knife work changes the texture in the mouth directly: whole tofu has to be bitten through, while fine threads are soft and loose in the soup, and one mouthful picks up tofu and broth together.

Cut fine, tofu has more surface and the heat reaches the middle faster; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). Soft tofu breaks easily, so the hard part is neat cuts and steady pressure, which let the threads keep a whole shape before they go into the pot. If the thickness is uneven, crumbs mix into the soup, and thick and thin threads feel different in the mouth.

Once the threads are in the soup, nudge them gently so they spread; stir hard and you wreck the shape you cut. The skill in Wensi tofu is in how fine the cut is, and also in getting the threads intact into the bowl.

At home you can start with coarser tofu threads, push-cutting steadily with more of the width of the blade, and keep the cut tofu supported in water so you handle it less. First get threads that don't break and are close in thickness, then cut finer. A professional chef's fine threads take repeated practice; at home the threads can be coarser, and pairing soup with tofu works just the same.

## Dasi gansi: letting the broth cling to the fine threads

**Dasi gansi** (*da zhu gan si*, shredded dried tofu simmered in stock) uses pressed dried tofu, sliced thin first and then cut into fine threads, and cooked with fresh stock and other ingredients. The ingredient differs from Wensi tofu: dried tofu is firmer, and its threads are resilient and cook through better in the soup.

Turning thick white dried tofu into thin threads gives the soup more surface to touch; in the mouth, both the gaps between the threads and their surfaces carry the seasoning. Even thickness also keeps the softness and firmness in one mouthful close; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). At home, ready-cut dried tofu threads save the slicing, but look at their thickness and firmness, because those decide how long to cook them.

Chicken stock, shrimp, ham and other ingredients together build a savoury base. The savoury nucleotides in the meats and the free glutamate in the other ingredients can produce [umami synergy](/principles/umami/); the exact taste varies with the stock and the ingredients. When the stock is already savoury enough, taste a mouthful with the threads in it before adding salt, so you don't taste only the soup and end up over-seasoning the dish.

For home practice, begin with evenly cut white dried tofu threads and a pot of chicken stock with a clear flavour. You want threads that are soft but not mushy, and a soup that holds up the soybean fragrance. There is no need to cram every strongly flavoured ingredient into the pot at once to look generous.

## Lion's head: fine cut, coarse chop, then gentle stewing to softness

Clear-stewed lion's head makes large meatballs of fatty and lean pork. **Fine cutting and coarse chopping** (*xi qie cu zhan*) means cutting the pork into small bits, then chopping it lightly a few times so it holds together while keeping some grain. Which meat you pick, how you cut it and how you mix it together decide whether the meatball is soft or won't hold its shape; beat it all into a fine, dense paste and you get a different, firmer texture.

When heated, the meat's [protein coagulates](/principles/protein-coagulation/) first and the meatball slowly sets; with continued moist-heat stewing, some of the [collagen in the meat turns into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), and it eats soft and smooth. The fat changes the overall texture as well, so not all the softness is thanks to gelatin.

Keep the soup at a slight simmer and a large meatball gets buffeted less, so it stews more easily until the inside is cooked through, soft and not falling apart. A hard boil and frequent turning easily damage the shape. At home you can manage fine cutting with coarse chopping and gentle stewing; first make the meatballs a similar size. The larger the meatball, the longer the centre takes to cook through, and you can't go by the surface having set.
