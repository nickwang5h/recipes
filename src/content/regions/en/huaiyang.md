The fish, shrimp, crab, vegetables and soy products of Jiangsu's water network provide ingredients for fine preparation and light, fresh seasoning. This page focuses on the Huaiyang techniques of the Huai'an and Yangzhou area; Jiangsu cooking also includes other local flavours, and the dishes of the whole province cannot be put under one taste.

## Knife work: cutting tofu into another texture

**Wensi tofu** cuts soft tofu first into slices and then into fine threads that spread gently apart in the soup. The knife work here directly changes the texture in the mouth: whole tofu has to be bitten through, while fine threads are soft and loose in the soup, and one mouthful picks up tofu and broth together.

Fine cutting raises the surface area and also shortens the distance heat has to travel inside; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). Soft tofu breaks easily; the difficulty also lies in neat cuts and steady pressure, so that the threads still hold their shape before going into the pot. If the thickness is uneven, crumbs mix into the soup, and thick and thin threads feel different in the mouth.

Once in the soup, nudge and stir gently so the threads spread; constant hard stirring destroys the shape you have cut. The technical value of Wensi tofu lies in the fineness of the cut and also in keeping it intact in the bowl afterwards.

At home you can start with coarser tofu threads, push-cutting steadily with more of the width of the blade, and support the cut tofu in water to cut down on handling. First get threads that do not break and are close in thickness, then cut finer. A professional chef's fine threads take repeated practice; a home version can keep coarser threads, and the direction of pairing the soup with the tofu still holds.

## Dasi gansi: letting the broth cling to the fine threads

**Dasi gansi** (*da zhu gan si*, shredded dried tofu simmered in stock) uses pressed dried tofu, first sliced thin and then cut into fine threads, cooked with fresh stock and other ingredients. Its ingredient structure differs from Wensi tofu: dried tofu is firmer, and the threads cut from it are resilient and can be heated more thoroughly in the soup.

Turning thick white dried tofu into thin threads increases the surface in contact with the soup; in the mouth, both between the threads and on their surface carries the seasoning. Even thickness also makes the softness and firmness in one mouthful close; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). At home, ready-cut dried tofu threads save the slicing, but watch their thickness and firmness, which decide the heating time that follows.

Chicken stock, shrimp, ham and other ingredients together build a savoury base. The savoury nucleotides in the meats and the free glutamate in the other ingredients can produce [umami synergy](/principles/umami/); the exact taste also varies with the stock and the ingredients. When the stock is already savoury enough, taste a mouthful with the threads before adding more salt, to avoid tasting only the soup and over-seasoning the dish.

Home practice can begin with even white dried tofu threads and a pot of chicken stock with a clear flavour. The goal is threads that are soft but not mushy, and a soup that can carry the soybean fragrance; there is no need to cram every strongly flavoured ingredient into the pot at once to look generous.

## Lion's head: fine cut, coarse chop, then gentle stewing to softness

Clear-stewed lion's head makes large meatballs of fatty and lean pork. **Fine cutting and coarse chopping** (*xi qie cu zhan*) first cuts the pork into small bits and then chops it lightly so it can bind, keeping some of the grain of the pieces. The choice of meat, the way it is cut and the mixing together decide whether the meatball is soft and still holds its shape; beating it all into a fine, dense paste gives a different, firmer texture.

Heating first makes the meat's [protein coagulate](/principles/protein-coagulation/), setting the meatball gradually; with continued moist-heat stewing, some of the [collagen in the meat turns into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), helping produce a soft, smooth texture. The fat also changes the overall texture, so not all the softness can be put down to gelatin.

Keeping the soup at a slight simmer reduces the buffeting on a large meatball and makes it easier to stew until the inside is cooked through, soft and not falling apart. A hard boil and frequent turning easily damage the shape. At home you can manage fine cutting with coarse chopping and gentle stewing; start by keeping the meatballs a similar size. The larger the meatball, the longer the centre needs to cook through, and you cannot go by the surface having set.
