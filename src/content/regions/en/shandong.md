The seafood of the Shandong coast and the river fish, poultry and livestock of the interior give Shandong cooking different ingredients. This page starts from the crisp tenderness of *bao* dishes, the clean savouriness of soups and the rich aroma of green-onion braising: three textures that rely on three different sets of heat.

## *Bao*: finish the knife work first, then win a short time

***Bao*** (a very hot, very quick fry) stresses that the ingredients cook fast and come out of the pan right away. Oil-quick-fried double crisp uses pork stomach tip and chicken gizzard, where choosing, removing the sinew and re-cutting are all part of the cooking; quick-fried kidney slices use scoring to divide thick tissue into small pieces that stay joined. The cuts shorten the distance heat travels inside and also increase the surface in contact with oil and sauce; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/).

Thin parts cook fast, while thick parts still need time. If thickness is uneven, the parts already cooked can only keep taking heat. The crisp tenderness of offal therefore comes first from trimming it uniformly and then from brief heat; the protein in meat that keeps contracting loses more water, see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). The "crisp" of double crisp is mainly the texture of the tissue; the finished dish does not have a crisp shell as a fried food does.

Dishes like this require the main ingredient, side ingredients and sauce all to be ready before the heat goes on. At home you can practise re-cutting and adding small amounts to the pan, and watch how the ingredients curl and set quickly; a professional stove holds high heat more easily after the food goes in. With a weak flame, reduce the amount in a batch and take the food out by doneness; you cannot replace the judgment of cooked through by shortening to some fixed number of seconds.

## Drawing stock: clear stock for clarity, milk-white stock for richness

Shandong cooking uses soup to complete the flavour of a dish. Soup-quick-fried double crisp sets off the main ingredient with clear stock, while milk-white stock cattail shoots puts tender cattail shoots into a milky stock. The difference between **clear stock and milk-white stock** lies first of all in how much the pot is agitated.

Clear stock is kept at a slight simmer, with foam and floating fat skimmed off promptly to reduce fine particles and fat being stirred into the stock. A refined clear stock can also be cleared with minced meat: the protein in the minced meat coagulates when heated and carries some of the suspended matter together, and then it is strained out. This process uses [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/); fine oil droplets already mixed into the stock are not easily removed by skimming alone.

Milk-white stock keeps a moderate amount of fat and uses a rolling boil to push the fat to disperse. Small oil droplets scatter light, which is why the stock looks milky white; see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/). The "milk" describes appearance, and no milk is needed. Whiteness alone also cannot tell you the savouriness; the ingredients and the amount of water still have to fit together.

At home you can treat the same ingredients separately with a gentle simmer and with a rolling boil, and compare clarity and body. Practise skimming, heat control and straining first, then try clearing with minced meat; a stock that has already boiled white is not a good starting point for your first clear stock. When boiling a milk-white stock, also watch the evaporation, so the stock does not turn white just as the pot is close to dry.

## Green-onion braising: the aroma is in the oil, the sauce clings to the outside

[Braised Sea Cucumber with Green Onions](/recipes/cong-shao-hai-shen/) lets the sea cucumber take on green onion oil and seasoning sauce. The green onion sections are first fried in oil to bring out their aroma, and the oil carries many of the aroma compounds through the main ingredient; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). When the green onion sections are slightly yellow and fragrant, watch the heat; frying on until they are black makes the oil bitter.

The sea cucumber should already be rehydrated, cleaned and cooked soft before the braising, and the final braise mainly heats it through and coats it with sauce. The thin starch sauce at the end uses [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) to raise the viscosity of the sauce, so the green onion fragrance and savoury saltiness cling to the surface. It is enough that the sauce flows and the main ingredient shines; if too thick, it wraps the soft sea cucumber in a mouthful of starch paste.

## Trying it at home

Start with the site's green onion braise, made with cooked sea cucumber and oyster sauce, to practise the green onion oil and thickening in stages. [Sweet and Sour Carp](/recipes/tang-cu-li-yu/) lets you go on to practise scoring, battering, and pouring the sauce over just before serving; the site's version uses ketchup. Frying a whole fish is also limited by the width of the pan, the amount of oil and room to work, and there is no need to force a big fish into a small pan.
