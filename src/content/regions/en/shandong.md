Shandong has seafood on the coast and river fish, poultry and livestock inland, so Shandong cooking draws on very different ingredients. This page covers three textures: crisp-tender *bao* dishes, clean-tasting soups and rich green-onion braises. They rely on three different kinds of heat.

## *Bao*: finish the knife work first, then the pan only needs a moment

***Bao*** (a very hot, very quick fry) wants the ingredients cooked fast and out of the pan right away. Oil-quick-fried double crisp uses pork stomach tip and chicken gizzard, and choosing, removing the sinew and re-cutting are all part of the cooking; quick-fried kidney slices are scored so the thick kidney divides into small pieces that stay joined. The cuts shorten the distance heat has to travel inside and put more surface in contact with oil and sauce; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/).

Thin parts cook fast, while thick parts still have to wait. If the thickness is uneven, the parts that are done can only keep taking heat in the pan. So for tender offal, trim everything to the same size first, then use brief heat. The protein in meat keeps contracting and squeezing out water, see [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). The "crisp" of double crisp is the texture of the tissue itself; the dish has no crisp shell like a fried food.

Before the heat goes on, have the main ingredient, side ingredients and sauce all ready. At home you can practise re-cutting and adding small amounts to the pan, and watch how the food curls and sets quickly; a professional stove holds high heat more easily after the food goes in. With a weak flame, cook less at a time and take the food out when it is done, rather than counting a fixed number of seconds.

## Drawing stock: clear stock should be clear, milk-white stock should be rich

Shandong cooking uses soup to set the flavour of a dish. Soup-quick-fried double crisp sets off the main ingredient with clear stock, while milk-white stock cattail shoots puts tender cattail shoots into a milky stock. The difference between **clear stock and milk-white stock** lies first in how much the pot is agitated.

Clear stock stays at a slight simmer, and the foam and floating fat are skimmed off all the time so fine particles and fat don't get stirred in. A refined clear stock is also cleared with minced meat: the protein in the minced meat coagulates in the heat, gathers up some of the suspended bits, and is then strained out. This works by [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/); fine oil droplets already mixed into the stock can't be removed by skimming alone.

Milk-white stock keeps a moderate amount of fat and uses a rolling boil to break it into droplets. Small droplets scatter light, which is why the stock looks milky white; see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/). The "milk" describes how it looks; no milk goes in. A white stock is not necessarily a savoury one either; the ingredients and the amount of water still have to be right.

At home, take the same ingredients and simmer one batch gently and boil the other hard, then compare clarity and body. Practise skimming, heat control and straining first, then try clearing with minced meat; a stock that has already boiled white is a poor starting point for your first clear stock. When boiling a milk-white stock, also watch the evaporation, so the stock does not turn white just as the pot is close to dry.

## Green-onion braising: the aroma is in the oil, the sauce clings to the outside

[Braised Sea Cucumber with Green Onions](/recipes/cong-shao-hai-shen/) lets the sea cucumber take in green onion oil and seasoning sauce. The green onion sections are fried in oil first to bring out their aroma, and the oil carries many of the aroma compounds through the main ingredient; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). When the green onion sections turn slightly yellow and fragrant, watch the heat; fry them on until black and the oil turns bitter.

The sea cucumber is already rehydrated, cleaned and cooked soft before it goes in, so the final braise only heats it through and coats it with sauce. The thin starch sauce at the end uses [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) to thicken the sauce, so the green onion fragrance and savoury saltiness cling to the surface. It is enough that the sauce flows and the sea cucumber shines; a thick sauce wraps the soft sea cucumber in a mouthful of starch paste.

## Trying it at home

Start with the site's green onion braise, made with cooked sea cucumber and oyster sauce, to practise green onion oil and thickening a little at a time. [Sweet and Sour Carp](/recipes/tang-cu-li-yu/) is the next step, for scoring, battering and pouring the sauce over just before serving; the site's version uses ketchup. Deep-frying a whole fish is also limited by the width of the pan, the amount of oil and room to work, so there is no need to force a big fish into a small pan.
