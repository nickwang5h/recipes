Guangdong has rivers and sea, and its markets are full of fresh goods every day, so Cantonese cooking starts from freshness and texture. This page covers three methods common in Guangfu cooking, poaching, steaming and high-heat quick stir-frying, and looks at how one kitchen keeps food tender and also builds a brief fried fragrance.

## Poaching and white cut: gentle water, and a cooked centre

**White cut chicken** is a chicken simmered and steeped in water until done, cooled, chopped into pieces and served with a ginger and green onion dip. A gentle water temperature controls how the meat takes heat, and the taste of the chicken and the taste of the dip each do their own work.

The outside of a whole chicken heats first, but the breast, thighs and the meat next to the bone are different thicknesses. When poaching, let hot water fill the cavity and surround every part, and the heat evens out; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). Keep the surface of the water gently moving and it is easier to control how much heat the outside takes. At a hard boil, by the time the thick parts are done the thin parts are long overcooked.

Whether the chicken is tender depends on how well you control doneness: if the meat's [protein](/principles/protein-coagulation/) keeps contracting, it keeps losing water. Cool the chicken right after it is done, so carry-over heat doesn't keep cooking it; this also changes the texture of the skin. Judge doneness at the centre of the meat. The colour next to the bone isn't reliable on its own.

At home, practise water temperature and centre doneness first with skin-on chicken thighs of similar size, which are easier to manage than a whole chicken. Use a probe to confirm it is cooked through. When to stop the heat depends on thickness, so the soaking time for one chicken can't be copied for another. A whole chicken cut into pieces adds one more bit of knife work: after cooking, cut along the joints.

## Steaming: cook the main ingredient first, add the aroma last

[Steamed Whole Fish with Ginger and Green Onion](/recipes/steamed-fish/) steams a fresh fish first, then finishes it with ginger, green onion, oil and seasoned soy sauce. Steam condenses on the cooler surface of the fish and gives up its heat, and the heat on the surface then passes inward; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). So leave room in the steamer for steam to pass, and leave space under the fish too; a plate packed full doesn't steam faster.

The thickest part sets the time. The fish is done when the flesh goes from translucent to opaque and flakes apart easily; take it out right away, before [the protein keeps contracting](/principles/protein-coagulation/) and turns it tough. Pour hot oil over the ginger and green onion laid on the fish, and the fresh aroma passes into the oil; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). The savoury saltiness comes from the seasoned soy sauce.

[Steamed Pork Ribs with Black Bean Sauce](/recipes/chi-zhi-pai-gu/) is another way of steaming: small pieces of rib get a thin coating and are laid in a single layer. A little starch [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) when heated, so the steaming juices on the surface turn slightly thicker and silky in the mouth. The thin coating only makes the surface slippery; whether the meat is tender still depends on even size and the right steaming time.

Both kinds of steaming work at home. A shallow plate, small portions and steady steam cook more evenly than piling everything into one deep bowl.

## *Wok hei*: drying the surface in a short time

Dry-fried beef chow fun stir-fries beef, rice noodles and vegetables briefly, and asks for whole noodles with a dry surface. It is the classic dish for watching *wok hei*. A professional kitchen uses strong heat to quickly make up the heat the wok loses, so the surface of the food heats up and loses water, and a fried fragrance develops.

Water soaks up a lot of heat. Once liquid collects in the wok, the food is mostly heated in moist heat; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). Only after the surface has gradually dried does the aroma of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) develop more easily. *Wok hei* also includes other aromas formed by fat and brief frying at high temperature, so "the wok was very hot" doesn't explain all of the flavour.

What you can practise at home is draining the ingredients, loosening cooked rice noodles, and stir-frying briefly in batches, so the finished dish has no pooled liquid and doesn't stick into clumps. The instant of aroma from a restaurant's strong fire is hard to reproduce fully, and there is no need to chase flames leaping into the wok. The site's stir-fried flat rice noodles contain cumin and gluten; that is a different home-style combination, not classic dry-fried beef chow fun.

## Trying it at home

Start with [Blanched Choy Sum with Oyster Sauce](/recipes/bai-zhuo-cai-xin/) to practise putting thick stems in first and tender leaves later, then do steamed fish and steamed pork ribs with black bean sauce. All three can be done on a home stove: first see whether the thickest part is cooked, then get it out of the heat on time and season it, and you can taste the difference in heat.
