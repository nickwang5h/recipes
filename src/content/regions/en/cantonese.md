The river and sea produce of Guangdong and the fresh goods of its markets make freshness and texture the starting point of Cantonese cooking. This page focuses on the poaching, steaming and high-heat quick stir-frying common in Guangfu cooking, and looks at how one kitchen both keeps food tender and builds a brief fried fragrance.

## Poaching and white cut: gentle heat, but the centre must keep up

**White cut chicken** cooks a chicken by simmering and steeping it in water, cools it, chops it into pieces and serves it with a ginger and green onion dip. The point of the method is to use a gentle water temperature to control how the meat takes heat, and to keep the taste of the chicken separate from the dip.

The outer layer of a whole chicken heats first, but the breast, thighs and the meat next to the bone are different thicknesses. When poaching, letting hot water get into the cavity and surround every part improves how it heats; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). A water surface that moves gently makes it easier to control how much heat the outer layer keeps taking; if you keep it at a hard boil, by the time the thick parts are done the thin parts have had far too much heat.

Tenderness comes from controlling doneness: if the meat's [protein](/principles/protein-coagulation/) keeps contracting, it loses more water. Cooling the chicken promptly once it is done slows the carry-over heat and changes the texture of the skin. Check doneness at the centre of the meat; the colour next to the bone should not be the only judge.

At home, practise water temperature and centre doneness first with skin-on chicken thighs of similar size, which are easier to manage than a whole chicken. Use a probe to confirm it is cooked through; the moment to stop the heat has to change with thickness, and the soaking time for one chicken cannot be copied for another. A whole chicken cut into pieces adds one more knife-work requirement: after cooking, cut along the joints.

## Steaming: cook the main ingredient, finish the aroma last

[Steamed Whole Fish with Ginger and Green Onion](/recipes/steamed-fish/) steams a fresh fish and then finishes it with ginger, green onion, oil and seasoned soy sauce. Steam condenses on the cooler surface of the fish and gives up its heat; the heat on the surface then passes inward; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). So the steamer needs room for steam to pass, and there should be space under the fish too; a plate packed full does not steam faster.

What decides the time is the thickest part. Check doneness when the flesh has gone from translucent to opaque and flakes apart easily, and take it out promptly, to avoid the toughness that comes when [the protein keeps contracting](/principles/protein-coagulation/). The ginger and green onion finish gets its heat from the oil, letting the fresh aroma pass into the oil; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). The seasoned soy sauce supplies the savoury saltiness.

[Steamed Pork Ribs with Black Bean Sauce](/recipes/chi-zhi-pai-gu/) shows another way of steaming: small pieces of rib are given a thin coating and laid in a single layer. A little starch [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/) when heated, making the steaming juices on the surface slightly thicker and silky in the mouth. The thin coating adds slipperiness to the surface; the tenderness of the meat still depends on even size and the right heating time.

Both kinds of steaming can be done at home. A shallow plate, small portions and steady steam give more even doneness than piling everything into one deep bowl.

## *Wok hei*: dry the surface in a short time

Dry-fried beef chow fun stir-fries beef, rice noodles and vegetables briefly, and asks for whole noodles with a dry surface. It is the classic dish for watching *wok hei*. A professional kitchen uses strong heat to quickly restore the heat the wok loses, so the surface of the food heats up and loses water, and a fried fragrance results.

Water takes up a lot of heat. Once liquid collects in the wok, the food is mostly heated in moist heat; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). Only after the surface has gradually dried does the aroma of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) develop more easily. *Wok hei* also includes other aromas formed by fat and brief frying at high temperature, so "the wok was very hot" cannot explain the whole flavour.

What you can practise at home is draining the ingredients, loosening cooked rice noodles, and stir-frying briefly in batches, so the finished dish has no pooled liquid and does not stick into clumps. The instant aroma from a restaurant's strong fire is hard to reproduce fully, and there is no need to chase flames leaping into the wok. The site's stir-fried flat rice noodles contain cumin and gluten, a different home-style combination, and cannot be taken directly as classic dry-fried beef chow fun.

## Trying it at home

Start with [Blanched Choy Sum with Oyster Sauce](/recipes/bai-zhuo-cai-xin/) to practise putting thick stems in first and tender leaves later, then do steamed fish and steamed pork ribs with black bean sauce. All three dishes can be done on a home stove; first look at whether the thickest part is cooked, then arrange timely removal and seasoning, and the difference in heat can be tasted.
