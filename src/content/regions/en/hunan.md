The Xiang River valley, the Dongting Lake area and the mountains of western Hunan bring the Hunan kitchen fresh fish, vegetables and smoked and cured goods. The heat of Hunan cooking also has layers: fresh chilies have a clean fragrance, pickled chilies bring sour saltiness, and dried chilies add dry aroma. Quick stir-fries and steamed dishes each arrange these flavours, and also look after the tenderness, crispness or softness of the main ingredient.

## Fresh-chili quick stir-fry: deal with water first, then combine in the pan

The skill in chili pork and Hunan-style stir-fried beef is cutting chili and meat into shapes suited to quick cooking. The meat is sliced thin and even, the green peppers are cut into pieces or strips by thickness, and side ingredients such as celery must match the cooking speed of the meat slices; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). If thick vegetables are stir-fried with thin meat, by the time the vegetables soften the meat has usually lost its water.

The green peppers can be fried first until the skin wrinkles and scattered brown spots appear, and then combined with the meat in the pan. Once the water has gone, the chili aroma and the roasted taste of the surface stand out more; the meat slices stay in the pan only until cooked through. Methods that use fatty meat can also first render the pork fat and use it to fry the chilies and garlic. Watch the water at the bottom of the pan: if the ingredients are piled in too full, the steam cannot escape and the surface has trouble forming [browned aroma](/principles/maillard-reaction/).

Cut chilies let the heat disperse more easily, and hot oil catches some of the aroma and capsaicin; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). Fresh chilies and pickled chilies each have their own taste, and if you swap one for the other the saltiness and sourness must be readjusted. On a home stove you can fry the chilies first, then the meat, and combine them last; small batches shorten how long each slice of meat waits to cook.

## Steaming under chopped chilies: let the sauce form against the fish

Fish head with chopped chilies spreads chopped pickled chilies over the surface of the fish head and steams it on the plate. Salted, fermented chilies already carry saltiness and mature chili aroma, and some products are also sour; taste the chopped chilies before using them to judge how much more salt is needed. See [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/). Bottled chopped chilies differ in sourness and saltiness, and the final taste should be judged together with the cooked fish.

Steam condenses on the surfaces of the fish head and the plate and delivers heat, while the inside still warms by conduction; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). Splitting the fish head and laying it flat reduces the gap in heating between thick and thin parts. The fish juices, the chili juices and the water condensed from the steam gather in the plate and form a sauce against the fish meat; dip the meat in the juices in the plate as you eat, and the flavour is complete.

The hurdles of this dish are mainly the capacity of the steamer and judging doneness. Leave room on the plate for the steam to flow, keep enough water in the pot, and take the dish out when the thickest part of the fish has turned opaque and lifts apart easily into flakes. Steaming longer makes the fish's [protein](/principles/protein-coagulation/) tighten and lose water. At home you can practise with a smaller fish head, and the thickness of the chilies spread should also be close everywhere.

## Steaming with cured meats: steaming the dry aroma moist again

Steamed mixed cured meats steams smoked and cured ingredients such as cured pork, cured chicken and cured fish on one plate. Curing, drying and smoking have already changed the flavour and moisture of the ingredients, and steaming them together heats them, softens them again and releases fat and juices. Different cured meats meet in the same plate sauce, and each mouthful carries both meat fragrance and smoke.

Steam supplies the heat, and the plate juices and the water in the ingredients help the dry, hard parts to soften; thickness decides when the centre is heated through; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). Cutting pieces to a similar thickness and spreading them moderately steams more evenly than piling the cured meats into a thick lump. The finished dish should be hot through and easy to bite apart while keeping each meat's own texture.

A home cook can begin with good cured meats bought ready-made, prepare them as the product directs, and after steaming taste the saltiness and decide whether to add seasoning. Choose a shallow plate that fits in your steamer and avoid turning up the heat repeatedly to save time. Making smoked and cured meat itself needs separate control of curing, drying and smoking conditions; learn the steaming first and you will already have a feel for this technique.

## Trying it at home

[Hunan-style Pork with Chili Peppers](/recipes/la-jiao-chao-rou/) uses lean pork to practise dealing with water in separate batches; [Hunan-style Stir-fried Beef with Celery and Chilies](/recipes/xiao-chao-huang-niu-rou/) practises matching the thickness of the meat slices and the celery. Getting the ingredients ready and making each batch smaller, then going for a short, quick combine, is the easiest step to carry out on a home stove.
