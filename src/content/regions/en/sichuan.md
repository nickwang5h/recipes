The vegetables and meats of the Sichuan Basin, together with fermented seasonings such as chili bean paste and pickles, make up the usual kitchen base of Sichuan cooking. The advanced part is getting different textures and flavours from the same ingredients: some are dry-fried to a dry fragrance, some are quickly stir-fried to stay tender, and chilies and Sichuan peppercorns each have their own moment to go in the pan.

## Dry-frying: let the water retreat and leave a dry fragrance

**Dry-frying** (*gan bian*) uses continuous stir-frying to reduce the water in the main ingredient and build a firm, dry-fragrant texture; dry-fried shredded beef is the classic. The "dry" here describes the state of the finished dish; the pan still needs oil, and the traditional stir-frying method and the restaurant method of passing the meat through oil first have different rhythms.

When the ingredient first goes in the pan, the heat first goes into warming it and evaporating water; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). As the liquid gradually disappears and the surface dries, it becomes easier to get the fried fragrance that comes from the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). If you keep adding stock while stir-frying, it is hard to get the same dry-fragrant texture.

Judge by moisture, colour and texture: the shreds of meat go from wet and soft to firm, no free liquid is left on the bottom of the pan, and the spices can cling evenly. Dry fragrance still has a stopping point; more heat makes the meat hard and the spices bitter and burnt. At home, stir-fry in small batches so the steam has somewhere to go; if the heat is low, give the dehydration more time, and add the spices only when the main ingredient is nearly there.

The site's [Dry-fried Chicken](/recipes/gan-bian-zi-ji/) is a variation in which chicken pieces and potato are fried first and then coated in a sweet and hot sauce. It lets you practise frying and reducing the sauce, but the texture of traditional dry-fried shredded beef should be understood separately, from the process of continuous dehydration.

## Quick stir-frying and the bowl sauce: finish tenderness and seasoning together

[Kung Pao Chicken](/recipes/kung-pao-chicken/) puts diced chicken, green onion sections, dried chilies and peanuts together, with a sweet, sour and salty thin sauce. The **bowl sauce** (*wanzhi*) is a seasoning sauce mixed before the heat goes on, prepared together with a little starch, so that the final seasoning and thickening are done in a short time.

This works first because the main ingredient is a consistent thickness and cooks at nearly the same time; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). Once the bowl sauce is in the pan, the starch absorbs water and swells, and the sauce goes from flowing to a thin coating on the ingredients; see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Before it goes in, stir up the starch that has settled to the bottom again, and finish when the sauce turns glossy and just coats the main ingredient.

[Fish-fragrant Shredded Pork](/recipes/yu-xiang-rou-si/) is also good practice for a bowl sauce, but the version on this site, with chili bean paste, carrot and green pepper, is the home-style version; classic fish-fragrant seasoning relies on pickled chilies combined with ginger, garlic, green onion and sweet-sour balance. When practising, first finish the chosen version completely, so you can tell the differences that proportions and ingredients make.

On a home stove you can handle the meat and the vegetables separately and combine them at the end. This makes doneness easier to control, but the rhythm differs from a quick one-pan stir-fry on a professional stove. Whichever way you cook, if you only start looking for the seasonings after the main ingredient is already cooked through, it keeps losing tenderness.

## Red oil and peppercorn fragrance: tell which layer needs longer

For [Mapo Tofu](/recipes/mapo-tofu/), first fry the chili bean paste until it opens up, so the oil turns red and the savoury aroma is released, then add stock and cook the tofu; the ground Sichuan peppercorns are sprinkled on at the end. For [Water-boiled Beef](/recipes/shui-zhu-niu-rou/), the red broth is built first with chili bean paste, and after the sliced beef has been poached, hot oil is poured over the chilies and Sichuan peppercorns on the surface.

Both dishes divide the flavour into an earlier stage and a later one. Oil can carry many fat-soluble flavour compounds, while volatile aromas are lost with continued heating; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). The chili bean paste is fried open first, and the finishing spices get only brief heat, each doing a different job; you can't cook all the seasonings together for a long time and expect the same layers.

At home, practise frying the chili bean paste over low heat first: when the oil starts to turn red and you smell the savoury aroma, move on to the next step. The hot-oil pour of a water-boiled dish can be done in stages, first using a small amount of oil on the spices, and continuing once you see normal bubbling and smell the aroma. Heavy smoke and black specks show it is too hot; "the hotter, the more fragrant" is not a way to judge the heat.
