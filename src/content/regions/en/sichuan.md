The Sichuan Basin has vegetables and meat, and fermented seasonings such as chili bean paste and pickles, which is the usual kitchen base of Sichuan cooking. Its skill lies in getting different textures and flavours from the same ingredients: some are dry-fried to a dry fragrance, some are stir-fried briefly to stay tender, and chilies and Sichuan peppercorns each have their own moment to go in the pan.

## Dry-frying: fry the water out and leave a dry fragrance

**Dry-frying** (*gan bian*) means stir-frying continuously to drive the water out of the main ingredient, which gives a firm, dry-fragrant texture; dry-fried shredded beef is the classic. The "dry" describes the finished dish; the pan still needs oil, and the traditional stir-frying method and the restaurant method of passing the meat through oil first have different rhythms.

When the ingredient first goes in the pan, the heat goes into warming it and evaporating water; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). Once the liquid is gone and the surface is dry, the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) can bring up the fried fragrance. If you keep adding stock while you fry, you won't get this dry-fragrant texture.

Here is how to tell it is done: the shreds of meat go from wet and soft to firm, no free liquid is left on the bottom of the pan, and the spices cling evenly to the meat. Fry further and the meat turns hard and the spices bitter and burnt, so even dry fragrance has a point to stop. At home, fry a small amount at a time so the steam has somewhere to go; if the heat is low, give the water more time to cook off, and add the spices when the main ingredient is nearly there.

The site's [Dry-fried Chicken](/recipes/gan-bian-zi-ji/) is a variation: chicken pieces and potato are fried first and then coated in a sweet and hot sauce. It lets you practise frying and reducing the sauce, but the texture of traditional dry-fried shredded beef comes from continuous dehydration.

## Quick stir-frying and the bowl sauce: finish tenderness and seasoning together

[Kung Pao Chicken](/recipes/kung-pao-chicken/) stir-fries diced chicken, green onion sections, dried chilies and peanuts together, with sugar, vinegar and salt making a thin sauce. The **bowl sauce** (*wanzhi*) is a seasoning sauce mixed before the heat goes on and prepared together with a little starch, so the final seasoning and thickening take only a short time.

For this to work, the main ingredient must be cut to an even thickness so it cooks at nearly the same time; see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/). When the bowl sauce goes in, the starch absorbs water and swells, and the sauce goes from flowing to a thin coating on the ingredients; see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/). Stir up the starch that has settled to the bottom before pouring, and take the pan off when the sauce turns glossy and just coats the main ingredient.

[Fish-fragrant Shredded Pork](/recipes/yu-xiang-rou-si/) is also good practice for a bowl sauce. The site's version, with chili bean paste, carrot and green pepper, is the home-style one; classic fish-fragrant seasoning pairs pickled chilies with ginger, garlic and green onion, plus sweet-sour balance. When practising, finish the chosen version completely first, and you can then tell what the proportions and the ingredients each change.

On a home stove you can cook the meat and the vegetables separately and combine them at the end. Doneness is easier to control that way, but the rhythm differs from a quick one-pan stir-fry on a professional stove. Whichever way you cook, if you only start looking for the seasonings after the main ingredient is cooked through, it keeps losing tenderness.

## Red oil and peppercorn fragrance: which layer needs longer, which shorter

For [Mapo Tofu](/recipes/mapo-tofu/), first fry the chili bean paste until the oil turns red and the savoury aroma is released, then add stock and cook the tofu; the ground Sichuan peppercorns go on at the end. For [Water-boiled Beef](/recipes/shui-zhu-niu-rou/), the red broth is built first with chili bean paste, and after the sliced beef has been poached, hot oil is poured over the chilies and Sichuan peppercorns on the surface.

Both dishes split the flavour into an earlier part and a later part. Oil holds many fat-soluble flavour compounds, while volatile aromas are lost with continued heating; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). So the chili bean paste is fried open first and the finishing spices get only brief heat, each doing its own job; cook all the seasonings together for a long time and you won't get the same layers.

At home, practise frying the chili bean paste over low heat first: when the oil starts to turn red and you smell the savoury aroma, go on to the next step. The hot-oil pour of a water-boiled dish can be done in several pours: pour a little oil over the spices first, and continue once it bubbles normally and smells fragrant. Heavy smoke and black specks mean the oil is too hot; "the hotter, the more fragrant" is not a way to judge the heat.
