Zhejiang has freshwater produce from rivers and lakes and fish from the coast, and tea, bamboo shoots and rice wine enter kitchens in different places. Zhejiang cooking has its own ways of being clean and fresh, and soft and glutinous; below, Hangzhou's Longjing tea shrimp, clear-soup fish balls and Dongpo pork serve as examples of how one kitchen makes shrimp tender, fish light and pork tender enough to fall apart.

## Smooth stir-fried shrimp: tenderness and tea aroma finished together

Longjing tea shrimp pairs peeled river shrimp with Longjing tea. The shrimp are first given a thin coating with egg white, starch and so on, cooked in oil while being separated, and then briefly combined in the pan with a little tea liquid and tea leaves. The aim is shrimp that are clean white and separate, soft in the mouth, with the tea aroma clearly noticeable beside them.

The starch in the coating absorbs water and [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), which helps the surface stay smooth; tenderness still depends on controlling how the shrimp meat takes heat. Once the shrimp's [protein](/principles/protein-coagulation/) has set, continued stir-frying tightens it and it loses water. When coating, drain off the extra water first and leave only a thin layer clinging to the surface of the shrimp; a thick coating sticks into clumps in the pan, and the finished dish has an obvious floury feel.

Tea liquid brings aroma and also water and astringency. Cook the shrimp first and combine the tea at the end, which shortens the time the tea aroma has to escape in a hot pan; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). The tea liquid only needs to moisten the shrimp; if too much is poured in, the reduction takes longer and the tenderness of the shrimp is lost with it. At home, reduce the amount of shrimp per batch and stir-fry with a little oil; the large oil bath of a professional kitchen separates a large quantity of shrimp quickly and evenly more easily.

## Fish paste that sets into a gel: the lightness of clear-soup fish balls

Clear-soup fish balls scrape fish meat into a fine paste, beat it with salt and water, squeeze it into rounds and cook it in water. Hangzhou has ways of using little or no starch, where the balls rely on the structure of the fish itself to hold water, and the texture aimed for is light and soft. Scraping the paste also removes sinew, membrane and fine bones, and the finer the work, the fewer rough bits in the result.

Salt helps some of the muscle protein dissolve out, and stirring makes the paste a sticky whole; when heated, the protein links into a network that holds water. For the mechanism, see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/) and [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). Water should be mixed in a little at a time, so that each addition is taken up by the paste. If there is too much water, the limited protein cannot support it, and further beating cannot rescue it.

Once the paste is sticky and even, cook one small ball first to check: it should hold its shape, be cooked through in the centre and still be soft in the mouth, and then deal with the rest. Avoid hard boiling and repeated stirring in the hot water to reduce knocking between the balls; after lifting them out, reheat them briefly in clear stock. The difficulties at home are removing the bones, the water content of the paste and the state of the beating; an ordinary pot and stove are enough. To learn this skill, practise first on a small amount of paste and adjust the water added bit by bit.

## Slow simmering and reduction: the softness and wholeness of Dongpo pork

Dongpo pork makes skin-on pork belly into fairly neat squares and simmers it slowly in wine, soy sauce and so on until soft. It should have soft skin and tender meat, and still hold its shape when picked up. The earlier heating cooks the centre through and softens the connective tissue, and the final reduction concentrates the flavour; keeping the two jobs separate makes it easier to find the stopping point.

Moist heat and time turn [collagen in the skin and connective tissue into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/); the meat gradually loosens and the sauce becomes slick and sticky. The muscle fibres lose water at the same time, so choose skin-on cuts with fat and stop the heat once they have softened. A hard boil and frequent turning make the softened meat break up; lifting with a spatula holds the square shape more easily than turning repeatedly with chopsticks.

A home heavy-bottomed pot with a lid, held at a gentle simmer over low heat, can do the slow simmering. First check with chopsticks whether the skin and meat of a thick piece are soft, and then reduce the sauce until it thinly coats the surface of the meat; if the meat is already very tender, take it out first, reduce the sauce on its own and pour it back. What this exercise needs most is time and observation; an ordinary stove is enough, and the saltiness after reducing should be judged last.
