Zhejiang has freshwater produce from rivers and lakes and fish from the coast, and tea, bamboo shoots and rice wine go into kitchens in different places. Zhejiang cooking can be clean and fresh, or soft and glutinous. Below, Hangzhou's Longjing tea shrimp, clear-soup fish balls and Dongpo pork show how one kitchen makes shrimp tender, fish light and pork fall-apart soft.

## Smooth stir-fried shrimp: tenderness and tea aroma finished together

Longjing tea shrimp pairs peeled river shrimp with Longjing tea. The shrimp are first given a thin coating of egg white, starch and so on, separated and cooked through in oil, and then briefly combined in the pan with a little tea liquid and tea leaves. The result is shrimp that are clean white and separate, soft in the mouth, with the tea aroma clear beside them.

The starch in the coating absorbs water and [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), which keeps the surface smooth, but tenderness still depends on how much heat the shrimp take. Once the shrimp's [protein](/principles/protein-coagulation/) has set, more stir-frying tightens it and squeezes out water. Before coating, drain off the extra water and leave only a thin layer clinging to the shrimp; a thick coating sticks into clumps in the pan and tastes floury.

Tea liquid brings aroma and also water and astringency. Cook the shrimp first and add the tea at the end, so the tea aroma has less time to escape in a hot pan; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/). The tea liquid only needs to moisten the shrimp; pour in too much and the sauce takes longer to reduce, and the shrimp lose their tenderness. At home, cook fewer shrimp per batch and stir-fry with a little oil; the large oil bath of a professional kitchen separates a big batch of shrimp quickly and evenly more easily.

## Fish paste that sets into a gel: the lightness of clear-soup fish balls

Clear-soup fish balls are made by scraping fish meat into a fine paste, beating it with salt and water, squeezing it into rounds and cooking them in water. Hangzhou has ways of using little or no starch, where the balls rely on the structure of the fish itself to hold water, and the texture aimed for is light and soft. Scraping the paste also removes sinew, membrane and fine bones; the finer the scraping, the fewer rough bits in the result.

Salt dissolves some of the muscle protein, and beating turns the paste into one sticky mass; when heated, the protein links into a network that holds water. For the mechanism, see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/) and [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). Mix the water in a little at a time, so the paste takes up each addition. If you add too much, the limited protein can't hold it, and more beating won't rescue it.

Once the paste is sticky and even, cook one small ball first to test it: it should hold its shape, be cooked through in the centre and still be soft in the mouth. Then make the rest. Don't let the water boil hard or stir it repeatedly, so the balls don't knock together; after lifting them out, just reheat them briefly in clear stock. The difficulties at home are removing the bones, the water content of the paste and the beating; an ordinary pot and stove are enough. To learn this skill, practise on a small amount of paste and adjust the water bit by bit.

## Slow simmering and reduction: Dongpo pork, soft and still whole

Dongpo pork is skin-on pork belly cut into neat squares and simmered slowly in wine, soy sauce and so on until soft. The skin should be soft and the meat tender, and a piece should still hold its shape when picked up. The first part of the cooking heats the centre through and softens the connective tissue, and the final reduction concentrates the flavour; keeping the two jobs separate makes it easier to find the stopping point.

Moist heat and time turn [collagen in the skin and connective tissue into gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/); the meat gradually loosens and the sauce becomes slick and sticky. The muscle fibres lose water at the same time, so choose a skin-on cut with fat and take it off the heat once it has softened. A hard boil and frequent turning break up the softened meat; lifting with a spatula holds the square shape better than turning repeatedly with chopsticks.

A heavy-bottomed home pot with a lid, held at a gentle simmer over low heat, can do the slow simmering. First test a thick piece with chopsticks to see if the skin and meat are soft, then reduce the sauce until it thinly coats the surface of the meat; if the meat is already very tender, take it out first, reduce the sauce on its own and pour it back. This exercise needs time and watching more than anything; an ordinary stove is enough, and the saltiness after reducing is something to judge last.
