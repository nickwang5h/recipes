Thai food first strikes people as "strongly flavoured", but what it is after is **balance**: a dish often has sour, hot, salty and sweet together, with no one taste standing out alone. The cook tastes and adjusts at the pan again and again, and the amounts in a recipe are only a starting point.

## Flavour is adjusted, not fixed

The four pivots of seasoning in the Thai kitchen:

| Taste | Common sources |
|---|---|
| Salty and savoury | Fish sauce, shrimp paste, Thai soy sauce |
| Sour | Lime juice, tamarind |
| Sweet | Palm sugar, white sugar |
| Hot | Fresh bird's eye chilies, dried chilies |

Fish sauce is the liquid from small fish long [fermented](/principles/fermentation/) with salt; the amino acids released from the protein give it both salt and [umami](/principles/umami/). Add lime juice after taking the pan off the heat, because long cooking cooks away the fresh aroma and leaves only sourness; see [Acid: Balance, Softening and Stability](/principles/acid/). Taste the seasoning together with the main ingredient: Thai food is eaten with rice, so it is normal for the sauce to taste strong on its own.

## Curry paste: pound first, then fry

The flavour of Thai curry comes from a wet paste of spices: lemongrass, galangal, makrut lime zest, cilantro root, garlic, shallots and chilies, pounded in a mortar. Pounding opens plant cells better than cutting does and releases the aroma compounds; see [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).

Once the paste is made, it is fried in oil until fragrant. The traditional method first cooks thick coconut cream until it "splits", so the water evaporates and the fat separates out, and then fries the paste in that fat. Coconut milk is an [emulsion](/principles/emulsification/) of fat dispersed in water, and this step of frying the paste deliberately breaks it. [Thai Green Curry with Chicken](/recipes/gaeng-keow-wan-gai/), [Thai Red Curry with Pork](/recipes/gaeng-phed-moo/) and [Panang Curry with Beef](/recipes/panang-neua/) all begin with this step.

## Herbs go in last

Basil, mint and cilantro are used like vegetables in Thai cooking, in large amounts, but almost always go into the pan last, or are tossed in raw. [Pad Kra Pao](/recipes/pad-kra-pao/) uses holy basil, which has a peppery pungency, and green curry uses sweet basil, which smells of anise; the two cannot be swapped.

## Shopping in Canada

Asian supermarkets such as T&T carry galangal, lemongrass, makrut lime leaves (often also in the freezer section), palm sugar and tamarind pulp blocks. Holy basil is harder to find, usually at Thai or Southeast Asian grocers. For fish sauce, choose one that contains only fish and salt (sugar is fine).
