The structure of Japanese home cooking is simple: a bowl of rice, a bowl of soup and a few side dishes. The flavours are generally light, and they rest on a good pot of **dashi** and a few fermented seasonings, not on heavy spices.

## Dashi and umami

[Dashi](/recipes/dashi/) uses only kombu and bonito flakes and can be made in a few minutes. In 1908 the chemist Kikunae Ikeda extracted glutamate from kombu broth and proposed the concept of "umami". Kombu supplies glutamate and bonito supplies inosinate; used together, the umami is far stronger than from either alone. See [Umami](/principles/umami/).

The keys to making dashi are a short time and well-controlled temperature: take the kombu out just before the water boils, and turn off the heat soon after the bonito flakes go in. Long cooking brings out sliminess and astringency; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). [Miso Soup](/recipes/miso-shiru/), [Chawanmushi](/recipes/chawanmushi/) and [Oyakodon](/recipes/oyakodon/) are all built on it.

## Fermented seasonings

Soy sauce, miso, mirin and sake are all products of [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/). Koikuchi (regular) soy sauce is dark and the most commonly used; usukuchi (light) soy sauce is paler in colour but actually higher in salt. Miso loses its aroma if heated too long, so miso soup always has the miso dissolved in last and is not boiled again.

There is a widely repeated mnemonic, "*sa-shi-su-se-so*", for the order in which seasonings go into simmered dishes: sugar, salt, vinegar, soy sauce, miso. Sugar molecules are large and penetrate slowly, so sugar goes in first; the aromas of soy sauce and miso escape easily, so they go in later.

## Eggs and temperature

Japanese cooking is exacting about the temperature of eggs. [Tamagoyaki](/recipes/tamagoyaki/) and chawanmushi both dilute the beaten egg with dashi, so that the protein network is looser after it sets and holds more water. See [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). If the heat is too high, the egg gets holes and weeps.

## Yōshoku

[Tonkatsu](/recipes/tonkatsu/) and [Japanese Curry Rice](/recipes/japanese-curry/) belong to *yōshoku*, Japanese dishes adapted from Western cooking after the Meiji era, now the most everyday home cooking.

## Shopping in Canada

Kombu, bonito flakes, miso, true mirin and Japanese short-grain rice are all available at T&T and H Mart. The "mirin-style seasoning" in mainstream supermarkets has salt and syrup added and is not the same as true mirin; cooking sake often has salt added too, so read the label.
