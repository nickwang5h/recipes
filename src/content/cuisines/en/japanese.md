The structure of Japanese home cooking is simple: a bowl of rice, a bowl of soup and a few side dishes. The flavours are light, and they rest on a good pot of **dashi** and a few fermented seasonings, not on heavy spices.

## Dashi gets its umami from two ingredients

[Dashi](/recipes/dashi/) uses only kombu and bonito flakes and can be made in a few minutes. In 1908 the chemist Kikunae Ikeda extracted glutamate from kombu broth and proposed the concept of "umami". Kombu gives glutamate and bonito gives inosinate; used together, the umami is far stronger than from either alone. See [Umami](/principles/umami/).

Make dashi quickly and keep the temperature under control: take the kombu out just before the water boils, and turn off the heat soon after the bonito flakes go in. Cook it long and it turns slimy and astringent; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/). [Miso Soup](/recipes/miso-shiru/), [Chawanmushi](/recipes/chawanmushi/) and [Oyakodon](/recipes/oyakodon/) are all built on it.

## Seasoning comes from a few fermented bottles

Soy sauce, miso, mirin and sake are all products of [fermentation](/principles/fermentation/). Koikuchi (regular) soy sauce is dark and the most commonly used; usukuchi (light) soy sauce is paler but actually higher in salt. Miso loses its aroma if heated too long, so miso soup always has the miso dissolved in last and is not boiled again.

A widely repeated mnemonic, "*sa-shi-su-se-so*", gives the order for seasoning simmered dishes: sugar, salt, vinegar, soy sauce, miso. Sugar molecules are large and penetrate slowly, so sugar goes in first; the aromas of soy sauce and miso escape easily, so they go in later.

## Eggs are afraid of high heat

Japanese cooking is exacting about the temperature of eggs. [Tamagoyaki](/recipes/tamagoyaki/) and chawanmushi both dilute the beaten egg with dashi, so the protein network is looser once it sets and holds more water. See [Protein Denaturation and Coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). Turn the heat up and the egg gets holes and weeps.

## Yōshoku is Western food made Japanese

[Tonkatsu](/recipes/tonkatsu/) and [Japanese Curry Rice](/recipes/japanese-curry/) belong to *yōshoku*, Japanese dishes adapted from Western cooking after the Meiji era and now very much everyday home cooking.

## Shopping in Canada

Kombu, bonito flakes, miso, true mirin and Japanese short-grain rice are all available at T&T and H Mart. The "mirin-style seasoning" in mainstream supermarkets has salt and syrup added and is not the same as true mirin; cooking sake often has salt added too, so read the label.
