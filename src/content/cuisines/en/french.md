"French cooking" here mainly means the classical system that took shape from the 19th century and later became the common language of Western professional kitchens, not the rich home cooking of France's regions. Its core is the **sauce**.

## Stock (*fond*)

In French, *fond* originally means "base", and stocks are called *fonds de cuisine*, "the foundations of the kitchen". White stock is made by simmering raw bones directly; brown stock first roasts the bones and vegetables until browned, then simmers them in water. During simmering, the collagen in the bones turns into [gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), which is why a good stock sets to a jelly when it cools. After reduction, it is mainly this gelatin that lets a sauce cling and shine.

## The browned bits in the pan

After searing meat, a brown layer sticks to the bottom of the pan. The French call it *sucs*, and it is a product of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). Pour in wine or stock, scrape it up and dissolve it (*déglacer*), then reduce: that is the most basic way to make a pan sauce.

## The mother sauces

Carême was the first to sort French sauces into a few large families. In *Le Guide culinaire* (1903), Escoffier set out the five **mother sauces** still used today:

| Mother sauce | Liquid | How it is thickened |
|---|---|---|
| [Béchamel](/recipes/bechamel/) | Milk | White roux |
| Velouté | White stock | Blond roux |
| Espagnole | Brown stock | Brown roux |
| Sauce tomate | Tomato | Reduction (traditionally also roux) |
| [Hollandaise](/recipes/hollandaise/) | Butter | Egg yolk emulsion |

Each mother sauce takes different additions to give dozens of daughter sauces. Learning French sauces is really learning the few ways of thickening behind this table.

## Three ways to thicken a sauce

1. **Starch**: roux, flour and butter cooked together before the liquid is added; see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
2. **Reduction**: boiling off water to concentrate the gelatin and flavour.
3. **Emulsion**: using egg yolk or butter to break fat up into the liquid; see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/).

A last step is often added, *monter au beurre*: take the sauce off the heat and stir in a few pieces of cold butter to make it glossier, smoother and thicker. [Beurre Blanc](/recipes/beurre-blanc/) takes this step to the limit: almost the whole sauce is emulsified butter.

## Mise en place

"Everything in its place": before the heat goes on, all the ingredients are cut, weighed and laid out in order. This habit of the French kitchen suits any cuisine.
