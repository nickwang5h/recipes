"French cooking" here means the classical system that took shape from the 19th century and became the common language of Western professional kitchens, not the home cooking of France's regions. At its core is the **sauce**.

## Stock (*fond*) is the base of a sauce

In French, *fond* means "base", and stocks are called *fonds de cuisine*, "the foundations of the kitchen". White stock is made by simmering raw bones directly; brown stock first roasts the bones and vegetables until browned, then simmers them in water. As the stock simmers, the collagen in the bones turns into [gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/), which is why a good stock sets to a jelly when it cools. When you reduce it, this gelatin is mostly what lets a sauce cling and shine.

## The browned bits in the pan are ready-made flavour

After searing meat, a brown layer sticks to the bottom of the pan. The French call it *sucs*, and it is a product of the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). Pour in wine or stock, scrape it up and dissolve it (*déglacer*), then reduce: that is the most basic pan sauce.

## Five mother sauces

Carême was the first to sort French sauces into a few large families. In *Le Guide culinaire* (1903), Escoffier set out the five **mother sauces** still used today:

| Mother sauce | Liquid | How it is thickened |
|---|---|---|
| [Béchamel](/recipes/bechamel/) | Milk | White roux |
| Velouté | White stock | Blond roux |
| Espagnole | Brown stock | Brown roux |
| Sauce tomate | Tomato | Reduction (traditionally also roux) |
| [Hollandaise](/recipes/hollandaise/) | Butter | Egg yolk emulsion |

Each mother sauce takes different additions to give dozens of daughter sauces. So learning French sauces is really learning the few ways of thickening behind this table.

## A sauce thickens in only three ways

1. **Starch**: roux is flour and butter cooked together before the liquid goes in; see [Starch Gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
2. **Reduction**: boil off the water, and the gelatin and flavour concentrate.
3. **Emulsion**: egg yolk or butter breaks the fat up into the liquid; see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/).

A last step often follows, *monter au beurre*: take the sauce off the heat and stir in a few pieces of cold butter to make it glossier, smoother and thicker. [Beurre Blanc](/recipes/beurre-blanc/) takes this step as far as it goes: almost the whole sauce is emulsified butter.

## Mise en place: have everything ready before the heat goes on

*Mise en place* means "everything in its place": before the heat goes on, the ingredients are cut, weighed and laid out in the order you will use them. The habit comes from the French kitchen and suits any cuisine.
