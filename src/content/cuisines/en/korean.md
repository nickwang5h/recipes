A Korean meal is usually rice, a pot of soup or stew, and a few side dishes (*banchan*). Its flavour base comes from several **fermented** foods, and cooking is more a matter of combining these ready-made flavours.

## Three *jang*

The foundation of the Korean kitchen is *jang* (sauces and pastes). All three are tied to soybean fermentation:

| Jang | How it is made | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Soy sauce (*ganjang*) | Fermented soybean blocks soaked in brine, the liquid drawn off | Salting, marinating meat |
| Doenjang | The soybean blocks left after the liquid is drawn off, aged further | [Doenjang Jjigae](/recipes/doenjang-jjigae/), seasoned vegetable dishes |
| Gochujang | Glutinous rice, chili powder, soybean koji and salt, fermented | [Tteokbokki](/recipes/tteokbokki/), [Bibimbap](/recipes/bibimbap/) |

[Fermentation](/principles/fermentation/) breaks soy protein down into amino acids, which gives the pastes their deep, savoury saltiness; see [Umami](/principles/umami/). These pastes are already very salty, so add the paste first and leave the salt until last to adjust; see [Salt: Seasoning, Osmosis and Curing](/principles/salt/).

## Kimchi

[Baechu Kimchi](/recipes/baechu-kimchi/) starts with salt, which draws water out of the napa cabbage and makes it pliable. Then it is rubbed with chili powder, garlic and fish sauce and left to lactic acid bacteria to sour slowly. Fresh kimchi is bright, hot and crisp, and is good eaten as is; sour, aged kimchi has a deeper flavour and is what [Kimchi Jjigae](/recipes/kimchi-jjigae/) and [Kimchi Bokkeumbap](/recipes/kimchi-bokkeumbap/) want. Ageing is slower in the refrigerator, but more controllable.

## Chili powder

Korean chili powder (*gochugaru*) is ground from sun-dried red chilies. It is bright red, milder than ordinary chili powder, with a sweetness and a fruity aroma. It cannot be swapped for ordinary chili powder in equal amounts, or the dish will be too hot and the colour dull. When making a red broth, first mix the chili powder with oil before it goes in the pot; dry powder hitting a hot pan scorches and turns bitter easily. See [Spices and Aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).

## Side dishes

Most side dishes are blanched or raw-tossed vegetables (*namul*), seasoned with garlic, toasted sesame oil, sesame seeds and a little soy sauce, such as [Kongnamul Muchim](/recipes/kongnamul-muchim/). The portions are small and the flavours clear, and they balance the strongly flavoured mains.
