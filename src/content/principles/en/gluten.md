## What it is

**Gluten** is the network that forms when the proteins in wheat flour absorb water. The two main proteins do different jobs: **glutenin** mainly provides elasticity, so dough that has been stretched wants to spring back; **gliadin** mainly provides extensibility, so dough can be pulled long. Together they decide whether a dough can both stretch and hold its shape.

Mixing, kneading, folding and rolling help the hydrated proteins link up into a continuous network. In bread, it traps the gas produced by fermentation; in noodles, it lets the dough be rolled thin and stay chewy in the pot. The starch, which makes up most of the flour, also takes part in shaping the dough; how it absorbs water as it heats is covered in [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).

## Flour and water set the starting point of the network

Bread flour usually has more of the proteins that can form gluten, and suits doughs that need support and chew; cake flour suits pastries that should be soft and tender. All-purpose flour is the usual choice for everyday noodles and dumpling wrappers. Protein content is a clue for choosing flour, but in the end what counts is how the dough stretches and springs back, and the texture of the finished food.

| Desired result | What the gluten has to do | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Bread that is airy and chewy | Trap gas and support the shape | Choose a stronger flour; hydrate fully, knead or fold |
| Noodles that roll thin and don't break after boiling | Stay continuous and tough | Knead evenly, rest, then roll thinner step by step |
| A tender, crumbly tart crust | Keep some structure, reduce toughness | Use less water and avoid overworking |

With too little water, the dough is stiff and some of the flour has not absorbed water fully; with too much, the dough is soft and sticks to your hands. Eggs and milk also bring in water, so count these liquids when judging how soft a dough is. If you keep dusting on flour whenever it sticks, you change the proportions of the original recipe and may end up with a dough that is too dry.

## Kneading builds the network, resting helps it stretch

The aim of kneading is to spread the water evenly and form the network. A dough that goes from rough and ragged to fairly smooth and able to stretch continuously is a more useful signal than how long you kneaded. Bread dough can be checked by stretching it into a thin film; a stiffer noodle dough is better judged by whether the cut face is even and whether it cracks when rolled.

**Resting** lets water keep moving into the flour and lets the tension built up by kneading and rolling gradually relax. If a sheet of dough shrinks back as soon as you roll it, cover it and let it rest, then keep rolling; forcing it longer tends to tear it or leave it uneven in thickness. Covering also keeps the surface from drying into a skin, and a dry skin gets in the way of further stretching.

Resting itself also lets gluten form, which is how no-knead bread works: water and time. Noodle dough keeps aligning its network through repeated rolling, and a pasta machine set thinner one notch at a time is less likely to tear than one pass down to a thin sheet.

## Fat and heat decide the final texture

Fat gets in the way of flour absorbing water fully and of the proteins linking to one another. For a flaky pastry, mix the butter into the flour first and then add a little water; this limits gluten development, though kneading it again and again after the dough is mixed can still make the crust tough. A buttery bread that needs a strong network is often kneaded to build structure first, and the butter is added gradually afterward.

When noodles are boiled or bread is baked, the proteins change with heat and the starch absorbs water and gelatinizes, so the formerly soft structure gradually sets. Egg noodles also carry the proteins from the egg; the support and texture of the finished food come from several components together, see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: soak a dough in water and knead and rinse it repeatedly; the starch washes away, and the elastic mass left in your hand is the gluten itself, from which baked wheat gluten (*kaofu*) and fried gluten puffs are made. For Lanzhou hand-pulled noodles, *penghui* (an alkaline ash) is added to the dough to make the gluten more elastic, so the noodles can be pulled long and thin without breaking.
- **French**: tart dough (*pâte brisée*) has the butter coat the flour first and then a little water added, which limits gluten formation so it bakes up crumbly rather than tough; a baguette is the opposite, relying on well-developed gluten to trap the gas produced by fermentation.
- **Italian**: [Fresh Egg Pasta](/recipes/fresh-egg-pasta/) gets a dough that can be rolled thin from even kneading and resting; after rolling step by step, the sheets are flat with few tears at the edges.
- **Vietnamese**: the thin, crisp little baguette (*bánh mì*) used for [Bánh Mì Thịt](/recipes/banh-mi-thit/) has a light, airy crumb. The gluten network of the wheat dough holds the gas produced by fermentation; the flour must also be chosen with a soft texture in mind.
- **Japanese**: tempura batter is mixed with ice water and stirred lightly just before frying, leaving a few small lumps of flour. Stirring little and shortening the time the batter sits limits gluten development, so the coating turns out light and crisp.
- **Korean**: knife-cut noodles (*kalguksu*) are made by rolling a wheat dough into a thin sheet, folding it, and cutting it into noodles. Alternating even kneading with resting makes the dough easier to roll out and keeps it chewy after boiling.
