## What it is

**Gluten** is the network that forms when the proteins in wheat flour absorb water. The two main proteins each do one job: **glutenin** mainly gives elasticity, so stretched dough wants to spring back; **gliadin** mainly gives extensibility, so dough can be pulled long. Together they let a dough stretch and still hold its shape.

Mixing, kneading, folding and rolling help the hydrated proteins link into one continuous network. In bread, the network traps the gas that fermentation produces; in noodles, it lets the dough be rolled thin and stay chewy in the pot. Starch, which makes up most of the flour, also helps set the shape; how it absorbs water as it heats is covered in [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).

## Flour and water set the starting point of the network

Bread flour usually has more of the proteins that form gluten, and suits doughs that need support and chew; cake flour suits pastries that should be soft and tender; all-purpose flour is the usual choice for everyday noodles and dumpling wrappers. Protein content is a clue for choosing flour, but in the end what counts is how the dough stretches and springs back, and the texture of the finished food.

| Desired result | What the gluten has to do | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Bread that is airy and chewy | Trap gas and support the shape | Choose a stronger flour; hydrate fully, knead or fold |
| Noodles that roll thin and don't break after boiling | Stay continuous and tough | Knead evenly, rest, then roll thinner step by step |
| A tender, crumbly tart crust | Keep a little structure, less toughness | Use less water and don't overwork |

With too little water, the dough is stiff and some of the flour has not absorbed water fully; with too much, the dough is soft and sticks to your hands. Eggs and milk also bring in water, so count them when judging how soft a dough is. If you keep dusting on flour whenever it sticks, you change the proportions of the recipe, and the dough may end up too dry.

## Knead to build the network, rest to make it easy to roll

Kneading spreads the water evenly and builds the network. A dough that goes from rough and ragged to fairly smooth and able to stretch continuously tells you more than how long you kneaded. Bread dough can be stretched into a thin film to check it; for a stiffer noodle dough, look at whether the cut face is even and whether it cracks when rolled.

While the dough **rests**, water keeps moving into the flour and the tension from kneading and rolling gradually relaxes. If a sheet of dough shrinks back as soon as you roll it, cover it and let it rest, then keep rolling; forcing it longer tends to tear it or leave it uneven in thickness. Covering also keeps the surface from drying into a skin, and a dry skin gets in the way of further stretching.

Resting also lets gluten form, which is how no-knead bread works: water and time. Noodle dough keeps smoothing out its network through repeated rolling, and a pasta machine set thinner one notch at a time is less likely to tear the dough than one pass down to a thin sheet.

## Fat and heat decide the final texture

Fat gets in the way of the flour absorbing water fully and of the proteins linking to one another. For a flaky pastry, mix the butter into the flour first and then add a little water; this holds gluten back, though kneading the dough again and again after it comes together can still make the crust tough. A buttery bread that needs a strong network is the opposite: knead to build structure first, then add the butter gradually.

When noodles are boiled or bread is baked, the proteins change with heat and the starch absorbs water and gelatinizes, so the soft structure gradually sets. Egg noodles also carry the proteins from the egg. The support and texture of the finished food come from several components together; see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: soak a dough in water and knead and rinse it repeatedly; the starch washes away, and the elastic mass left in your hand is the gluten itself, from which baked wheat gluten (*kaofu*) and fried gluten puffs are made. For Lanzhou hand-pulled noodles, *penghui* (an alkaline ash) is added to the dough to make the gluten more elastic, so the noodles can be pulled long and thin without breaking.
- **French**: tart dough (*pâte brisée*) has the butter coat the flour first and then a little water added, which holds gluten back so it bakes up crumbly rather than tough. A baguette is the opposite, relying on well-developed gluten to trap the gas that fermentation produces.
- **Italian**: [Fresh Egg Pasta](/recipes/fresh-egg-pasta/) gets a dough that can be rolled thin from even kneading and resting. After rolling step by step, the sheets are flat with few tears at the edges.
- **Vietnamese**: the thin, crisp little baguette (*bánh mì*) used for [Bánh Mì Thịt](/recipes/banh-mi-thit/) has a light, airy crumb. The gluten network of the wheat dough holds the gas that fermentation produces; the flour must also be chosen with a soft texture in mind.
- **Japanese**: tempura batter is mixed with ice water and stirred lightly just before frying, leaving a few small lumps of flour. Stirring little and not letting the batter sit holds gluten back, so the coating turns out light and crisp.
- **Korean**: knife-cut noodles (*kalguksu*) are made by rolling a wheat dough into a thin sheet, folding it, and cutting it into noodles. Alternating even kneading with resting makes the dough easier to roll out and keeps it chewy after boiling.
