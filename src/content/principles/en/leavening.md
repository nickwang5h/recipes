## What it is

A piece of dough swells in the steamer because its countless small bubbles expand with heat. **Leavening** means getting gas into a dough or batter first, then letting heat expand it and set it. The gas comes from several places: carbon dioxide released by fermenting **yeast** and by **baking soda** meeting acid, air beaten in by whipping (see [foams](/principles/foams/)), and steam from the water in the dough as it heats, which is what gives a [cheese soufflé](/recipes/souffle-au-fromage/) its last lift.

Gas alone is not enough; something has to hold it. In bread and steamed buns, the [gluten](/principles/gluten/) network holds it; in cakes, it is mainly egg protein. Lots of gas that cannot be held makes the dough collapse; plenty of structure with too little gas makes it dense.

## Yeast: slow, but it brings flavour

Yeast feeds on sugar. Enzymes first cut the starch in the flour into sugar, and the yeast turns the sugar into carbon dioxide and alcohol. The gluten network traps the carbon dioxide and the dough rises bit by bit; the alcohol and other products of fermentation give the dough its aroma, and most of the alcohol escapes during baking or steaming (see [fermented seasonings](/principles/fermentation/)).

Yeast is alive and fussy about temperature:

| Temperature | What the yeast is doing |
|---|---|
| About 4°C (39°F, refrigerator) | Too cold; barely working |
| About 27–35°C (80–95°F) | Making gas fastest, but the faster it rises the thinner the flavour; for a fuller flavour, keep the dough cooler and let it rise slowly |
| About 41–46°C (105–115°F) | Water for dissolving active dry yeast |
| About 55–60°C (130–140°F) | The yeast dies and stops making gas |
| About 93°C (200°F) | The centre of a just-baked loaf |

Active dry yeast must be dissolved in warm water first; instant yeast can be mixed straight into the flour.

## Baking soda and baking powder: fast, gas on contact

**Baking soda** is sodium bicarbonate, a base, and it only releases carbon dioxide when it meets an acid. The recipe has to contain one, such as yogurt, buttermilk or vinegar. It reacts with acid almost at once, so a batter made with it should go into the pan quickly.

**Baking powder** is baking soda plus acids, inert while dry. The common "double-acting" powder has two acids: one starts releasing gas as soon as it gets wet, the other only when the batter is both wet and hot. So a mixed batter can wait a little, and it rises again once it reaches the oven or steamer.

| | Yeast | Baking soda | Baking powder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Needs | Sugar, warmth, time | An acid in the recipe | Water and heat |
| How fast | An hour or two | At once | Partly at once, partly with heat |
| Brings | Gas and fermented flavour | Gas | Gas |

## Heat expands the bubbles, then sets them

As soon as the dough heats, the gas in the bubbles expands, water turns to steam and the dough grows taller; at 55–60°C (130–140°F) the yeast dies and stops making gas. As the temperature keeps rising, egg proteins set (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)), the starch takes up water and gelatinizes, the bubble walls firm up and the shape is fixed. Open the oven door or lift the steamer lid before that, or let the steam stop, and the risen dough falls back.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [mantou](/recipes/man-tou/) and [fresh pork buns](/recipes/xian-rou-bao-zi/) are raised with yeast; [char siu bao](/recipes/cha-shao-bao/) rise first with yeast and then get a second push from baking powder in the steamer that splits the tops. [Youtiao](/recipes/you-tiao/) use both baking powder and baking soda.
- **French**: the everyday [cake salé](/recipes/cake-sale/) uses baking powder, so the batter goes straight into the oven with no wait for fermentation.
- **Italian**: [pizza Margherita](/recipes/pizza-margherita/) and [focaccia genovese](/recipes/focaccia-genovese/) are yeast doughs whose gluten holds the gas, baking into a base with holes of all sizes; the little Carnival fritters, [castagnole](/recipes/castagnole/), use baking powder.
- **Japanese**: the pancakes of [dorayaki](/recipes/dorayaki/) get their lift both from air in the whipped eggs and from baking powder releasing gas in the pan.
- **Korean**: [hotteok](/recipes/hotteok/) wrap a soft yeast dough around a brown sugar filling, then flatten and fry it.
