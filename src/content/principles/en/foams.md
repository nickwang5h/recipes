## What it is

Whipped egg whites can grow to six to eight times their volume, and everything added is air. A **foam** is bubbles packed closely through a liquid that do not burst right away.

For the bubbles to last, each one needs a wall around it. Everything that whips has some ingredient that moves to the surface of the bubbles and builds that wall. In the kitchen, two things build it: **proteins** (egg white, whole egg, yolk) and **fat globules** (whipping cream).

## Proteins unfold at the bubble surface

The proteins in egg white start out curled into small balls, with some parts that like water and some that avoid it. When you whip, a protein that lands on a bubble has half of itself in air and half in water, so it unfolds and lets its water-avoiding parts reach into the air. The unfolded proteins then link to one another into a network that holds the bubbles. It is much like egg white setting when heated (see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).

Beaten until glossy, with peaks that curl over when you lift the beaters, the whites are at soft peaks; when the peaks stand straight up, they are at stiff peaks.

## Egg white foams hate fat and like a little acid

A tiny amount of fat stops egg whites from whipping: a trace of yolk or a film of grease on the bowl is enough. Plastic bowls tend to hold grease, so use stainless steel or glass.

A few other things change the result:

- **Acid**: a little cream of tartar, lemon juice or vinegar makes the foam more stable.
- **Salt**: makes egg white foam less stable.
- **Sugar**: beat the whites until foamy and about doubled, then add the sugar a spoonful at a time for the greatest volume and finest texture; sugar added at the start gives less volume.
- **Temperature**: eggs separate most easily cold; let the separated whites warm for 20–30 minutes and they whip to greater volume.

## Cream foams rely on partly solid fat

Whipping cream is about 35% fat. As it is whipped, the fat globules partly stick to one another in chains and clusters, gather around the air bubbles and link one bubble to the next, and the cream stiffens.

This needs some of the fat in each globule to be solid crystals, so whipping cream depends on temperature: the cream must be cold, so the globules stick together without melting into one. Warm cream has its fat fully liquid; the globules just merge into large drops and it won't whip. Whip too far the other way, and the globules stick together so much that butter grains separate out.

## Heat sets the foam

In the oven, the air in a foam's bubbles expands with heat and the volume grows further. The protein network around them sets at the same time, fixing the foam into a light solid that stays up even when bubbles burst. Take it out or open the oven before the network has set, and the bubbles shrink and the whole thing falls. A foam is one way of raising a batter, alongside baking soda and yeast (see [leavening](/principles/leavening/)).

## Across cuisines

- **French**: [cheese soufflé](/recipes/souffle-au-fromage/) and [chocolate mousse](/recipes/mousse-au-chocolat/) are both lightened with folded-in egg white foam. Spoon soft meringue into ovals, poach them in barely simmering water and float them on [crème anglaise](/recipes/creme-anglaise/), and you have *œufs à la neige*, also called floating island.
- **Italian**: [zabaione](/recipes/zabaione/) whisks yolks, sugar and Marsala over hot water into a warm yolk foam. [Pan di Spagna](/recipes/pan-di-spagna/) uses no baking powder; whole eggs and sugar are beaten for a quarter of an hour or more, and the whole-egg foam holds up the entire cake.
- **Chinese**: a home-made [steamed brown sugar sponge cake](/recipes/ma-la-gao/) (*ma lai go*) first beats whole eggs and brown sugar until lighter in colour and full of fine bubbles, then adds baking powder; [taro mochi cream balls](/recipes/yu-ni-xue-mei-niang/) holds whipped cream, which goes in only once the wrapper and filling are cold, because warmth makes the cream collapse.
- **Japanese**: for [dorayaki](/recipes/dorayaki/), eggs, sugar and honey are beaten until pale and falling in a ribbon, which keeps the pancakes soft.
