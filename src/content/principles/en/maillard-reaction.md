## What it is

The crust on a seared steak, the skin of toast, browned ground meat: most of their colour and aroma come from the **Maillard reaction**. When reducing sugars and amino acids (or the amino groups on proteins) are heated, they go through a long chain of reactions that makes brown compounds and hundreds of aroma molecules. In 1912, the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard was the first to describe it.

It is different from **caramelization**. Caramelization needs only sugar, and a higher temperature (sucrose needs about 160°C (320°F) or above). The Maillard reaction also needs protein or amino acids, so meat, bread, beans and onions all undergo it. In real cooking, the two often happen at the same time.

## Three things that set the speed

1. **Temperature**: the reaction happens at room temperature too, only extremely slowly. It generally takes about 120–140°C (250–285°F) or above to make noticeable browning and flavour in the time it takes to cook.
2. **Water**: as long as the surface is wet, the temperature can hardly rise above 100°C (212°F), and the food is steaming rather than searing. So dry the surface before it goes in the pan, and do not overfill the pan.
3. **pH**: alkaline conditions speed the reaction up. This is why alkaline noodles and German pretzels are so dark; a small pinch of baking soda added to onions browns them faster. Acidic conditions slow the reaction down.

## How to use it

- **Dry, and don't crowd**: pat meat dry with paper towels before it goes in the pan. Put too much in at once and the pan temperature drops sharply, the food releases water, and it ends up boiled.
- **Heat the pan first, and don't keep moving the food**: give the contact surface time to heat up and set.
- **The brown bits on the bottom are valuable**: after searing meat, dissolve the brown layer on the bottom of the pan with wine or stock, and you have the base of the sauce's flavour.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: "crisping" (*bian su*): in [Mapo Tofu](/recipes/mapo-tofu/), the ground beef is stir-fried until the moisture has cooked off, the colour turns brown and it starts to release oil, and only then are the seasonings added; the fragrance is completely different. *Wok hei* has complicated causes; it is generally thought to include the Maillard reaction at high heat, caramelization, and the smoky flavour produced when oil mist meets flame.
- **French**: the brown bits left in the pan after searing meat are called *sucs* in French (often called fond in English). In [Beef Bourguignon](/recipes/boeuf-bourguignon/), the beef is first seared in batches to a brown surface, then wine is poured in to dissolve the *sucs* (*déglacer*), and the searing flavour goes into the braising liquid.
- **Italian**: the crust edge and base of [Pizza Margherita](/recipes/pizza-margherita/) first lose water, then turn golden brown in the hot oven and take on a toasted-bread aroma. Drain the tomatoes and fresh cheese and spread them thin, so water doesn't hold the base back.
- **Thai**: the skin of [Thai Grilled Chicken](/recipes/gai-yang/) gradually loses water over the charcoal and turns golden brown, which gives the grilled aroma. If wet marinade is piled on the surface, scrape off the excess first, so the skin has a chance to dry and take on colour.
- **Vietnamese**: for [Grilled Pork with Rice Noodles](/recipes/bun-cha/), the pork slices and small patties are grilled to a brown aroma before going into the fish sauce dipping sauce. Spread the meat out in batches, because crowded meat releases water. The sugar in the marinade already gives colour, so check whether the surface is really grilled to a fragrant brown.
- **Japanese**: for [Chicken Teriyaki](/recipes/chicken-teriyaki/), the chicken skin is first seared golden brown, then the teriyaki sauce is added and reduced to a glaze. Searing first and reducing after lets you control the browned, toasted aroma of the skin and the sweet and salty gloss of the sauce separately.
- **Korean**: for [Bulgogi](/recipes/bulgogi/), the marinated thin beef is spread out in batches and grilled to browned edges. Drain the excess marinade first so the meat does not sit in liquid and boil. The marinade contains sugar, so once the meat has taken colour, check doneness promptly to keep it from burning bitter.

## Going too far

Past brown comes black and bitter. Starchy foods also produce acrylamide at high heat, so for fries and roast potatoes it is generally recommended to stop at golden, with no need to chase a deep brown.
