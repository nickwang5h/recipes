## What it is

Knife work first changes the shape of the food, and the shape then decides how it behaves in the pan. A thin slice has a short distance to the centre and cooks easily; the centre of a large chunk warms slowly, so its outer layer has to take heat longer. The same piece of meat sliced or left whole needs different cooking; see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/).

Cutting small also raises the ratio of **surface area to volume**: the same weight of food shows more surface and exchanges heat, water and seasoning faster. Cutting through the tissue also lets meat juices or vegetable juices run out. Knife work sets the starting point for heating; pan temperature, how full the pan is and time decide the result after that.

## Look at thickness first, then size

Heat travels from the surface to the centre, so the hardest place to heat through is usually the thickest. For slices, look at thickness first; for diced meat, look at the size in every direction. A few pieces of meat whose edges look the same size but whose centres differ in thickness will still cook unevenly in the pan, one piece done and another not.

| Cut | Heating and texture | Suited to |
|---|---|---|
| Thin slices, fine shreds | The centre warms quickly, but they also overcook and lose water easily | Short stir-frying or scalding, with the other ingredients prepared in advance |
| Dice of similar size | Easy to bring to similar doneness in the same batch | Quick stir-frying, with the order of adding set by how fast each cooks |
| Large chunks | The centre warms slowly and they can take long cooking | Stewing, letting connective tissue gradually soften |

When several ingredients go into the pan together, cutting them the same size will not necessarily make them finish together. Carrots hold up to cooking better than soft leafy greens, so cut them thinner or add them earlier. Set aside any minced bits and thin edges that come off the cutting board and add them later, rather than cooking them dry while you wait for the thick pieces.

## More surface browns easily and weeps easily

Cutting into small pieces gives more surface that can brown, but that surface first has to meet enough heat. If minced meat is piled together or wet vegetables fill the pan, the liquid collects at the bottom, the food cooks in water first, and browning is hard to get. For the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/), drain and pat dry after cutting, then add the food to the pan in batches.

Cutting small also lets [salt](/principles/salt/) work its way inside more easily, and lets liquid run out more easily. For a cold dressed dish you can use this; when pan-frying meat, watch whether the surface is wet. Finely minced garlic and onion heat fast in hot oil and burn easily once their water is gone. The finer you cut, the more you need the next ingredient ready, so you can add it as soon as the colour changes.

## The grain of the meat decides how it bites

The "grain" of meat is the **muscle fibres** lying in bundles. Cutting across the grain shortens the long fibres, so each bite has fewer long fibres to chew through. First find the parallel lines on the surface of the meat, then let the blade cross them; when the piece of meat turns, the knife turns with it.

Cutting across the grain changes the structure you meet when chewing. The fascia and connective tissue are still there, and muscle that has tightened from overcooking does not become juicy again. Meat for quick stir-frying is sliced thin and across the grain, with [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/) kept under control; cuts rich in connective tissue are left for [slow stewing](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: in [Kung Pao Chicken](/recipes/kung-pao-chicken/), the chicken dice, green onion pieces and peanuts are of similar size, so they finish together in one pan and every bite tastes the same. Pork kidney and squid are scored with *ji hua dao* (flower cuts): a grid cut diagonally without cutting through, which increases the surface area so they cook through in a few dozen seconds, and curl up into flowers when heated, holding the starch glaze better.
- **French**: the classic kitchen has names for cuts, such as *julienne* (fine strips) and *brunoise* (fine dice). How large the vegetable dice for a *mirepoix* are depends on how long they will cook: a quick sauce gets fine dice, while [Beef Bourguignon](/recipes/boeuf-bourguignon/) and stocks, which cook for hours, get large chunks.
- **Italian**: in *aglio e olio* ([Spaghetti Aglio e Olio](/recipes/aglio-olio/)), the garlic slices need to be close in thickness so they turn pale gold together; thin minced bits burn first and make the whole pan of oil bitter.
- **Japanese**: *ran-giri* (rolling cut) turns the ingredient after each cut, producing pieces with many angled faces and similar size; the carrots in [Nikujaga](/recipes/nikujaga/) suit this cut. Change where you cut between the thick and thin ends so each piece has a similar thickness, and they soften together.
- **Korean**: [Bulgogi](/recipes/bulgogi/) is first sliced thin across the grain of the muscle, which shortens the long fibres so it is easier to chew. Spread out, the thin slices cook quickly, and even thickness keeps some from drying out hard while others are still raw.
