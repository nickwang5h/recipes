## What it is

Oil and water do not mix. Shake them hard together and the oil breaks into small droplets that hang in the water for a while, then rise again and join into one layer when left to stand. **Emulsification** is finding a way to keep one liquid spread through the other as tiny droplets for a long time.

Kitchen emulsions come in two kinds:

| Type | Continuous phase | Dispersed phase | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oil-in-water (O/W) | Water | Oil droplets | Mayonnaise, hollandaise, beurre blanc, the cheese sauce of cacio e pepe |
| Water-in-oil (W/O) | Fat | Water droplets | Butter itself (about 80% fat, the rest mainly water and milk solids) |

## Oil droplets that touch will merge

When oil droplets touch they merge, getting bigger and bigger, until they are lighter than the water and rise into a layer. So a stable emulsion has to do two things: **make the droplets small enough**, and **keep them from merging when they touch**.

## Four helpers

1. **Emulsifiers**: molecules with one water-loving end and one oil-loving end. They line up on the surface of the oil droplets like a shell and make the droplets bounce off each other. The lecithin and proteins in egg yolk, the casein in dairy and the mucilage in mustard are common kitchen emulsifiers.
2. **Stabilizers**: thicken the continuous phase, or put a barrier between the droplets, so the droplets collide less. Starch (pasta water, starch slurries), gelatin and fine solid particles can all do this. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
3. **Mechanical force**: whisking, swirling the pan and using a blender all tear the oil into smaller droplets. The smaller the droplets, the more stable the emulsion.
4. **Temperature**: most kitchen emulsions have a "comfort zone". Too hot, and the proteins coagulate or the fat melts completely, so the shell fails; too cold, and the fat in butter solidifies again and the sauce turns hard.

One more thing is easy to overlook: **there must be enough continuous phase**. In an oil-in-water sauce, water is the continuous phase. If oil goes in too fast and there is too little water, the sauce first turns thick and stiff, then suddenly releases oil. This is why mayonnaise starts with oil added a drop at a time.

## Across cuisines

- **French**: mayonnaise and [Hollandaise Sauce](/recipes/hollandaise/) are emulsified by egg yolk. [Beurre Blanc](/recipes/beurre-blanc/) relies only on the milk proteins in the butter itself and the little water left in the reduction. Adding cold butter to finish a sauce (*monter au beurre*) is also a small emulsification.
- **Italian**: the starch in pasta water and stock steadies the fat of the cheese and butter. [Cacio e Pepe](/recipes/cacio-e-pepe/), [Spaghetti Carbonara](/recipes/carbonara/), [Spaghetti Aglio e Olio](/recipes/aglio-olio/) and the *mantecatura* of risotto all use this trick.
- **Chinese**: milky-white fish soup and bone broth are generally explained like this: a hard rolling boil beats the fat into fine droplets, and the gelatin and proteins that dissolve into the soup then hold them. That is why the boil has to be hard; a low, slow simmer gives a clear soup.
- **Thai**: [Thai Green Curry with Chicken](/recipes/gaeng-keow-wan-gai/) first cooks thick coconut cream until oil beads separate out, then fries the curry paste in this oil until fragrant. Coconut milk is naturally an oil-in-water emulsion, and this step makes it separate on purpose.
- **Japanese**: the rich soup of Hakata-style *tonkotsu ramen* keeps pork bones at a steady rolling boil, beating the fat into the soup. The emulsified oil droplets and the suspended fine particles together turn the soup opaque and thick white.
- **Korean**: *seolleongtang* boils beef bones continuously until the soup turns milky. Boiling disperses the fat into the soup, which is part of how the thick texture forms; the heat has to be high enough to keep a rolling boil.

## Rescuing a broken sauce

A sauce that releases oil and separates has had most of its oil droplets merge. Whisking hard in the same pan usually cannot save it. A more reliable way is to **start a new small continuous phase**: put 1 tbsp warm water (for a butter sauce) or one egg yolk (for mayonnaise) in a clean bowl and whisk the broken sauce into it a little at a time, as if making the emulsion again from the start.
