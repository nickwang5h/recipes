## What it is

Oil and water do not mix. Shake them hard together and the oil is broken into small droplets that hang in the water for a while, then rise again and join into one layer when left to stand. **Emulsification** is finding a way to keep one liquid dispersed in the other as tiny droplets for a long time.

Kitchen emulsions come in two kinds:

| Type | Continuous phase | Dispersed phase | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oil-in-water (O/W) | Water | Oil droplets | Mayonnaise, hollandaise, beurre blanc, the cheese sauce of cacio e pepe |
| Water-in-oil (W/O) | Fat | Water droplets | Butter itself (about 80% fat, the rest mainly water and milk solids) |

## Why it separates

When oil droplets touch they merge, getting bigger and bigger, and in the end rise and form a layer because they are lighter than water. So a stable emulsion has to do two things: **make the droplets small enough**, and **keep the droplets from merging when they touch**.

## Four helpers

1. **Emulsifiers**: molecules with one water-loving end and one oil-loving end line up on the surface of the oil droplets like a shell and make the droplets bounce off each other. The lecithin and proteins in egg yolk, the casein in dairy and the mucilage in mustard are common kitchen emulsifiers.
2. **Stabilizers**: thickening the continuous phase, or forming a barrier between the droplets, makes it harder for them to collide. Starch (pasta water, starch slurries), gelatin and fine solid particles can all do this. See [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
3. **Mechanical force**: whisking, swirling the pan and using a blender all tear the oil into smaller droplets. The smaller the droplets, the more stable the emulsion.
4. **Temperature**: most kitchen emulsions have a "comfort zone". Too hot, and proteins coagulate or the fat melts completely, so the shell fails; too cold, and the fat in butter solidifies again and the sauce turns hard.

One more factor is often overlooked: **there must be enough continuous phase**. In an oil-in-water sauce, water is the continuous phase. If oil goes in too fast and there is too little water, the sauce first turns thick and stiff, then suddenly releases oil. This is why mayonnaise starts with oil added a drop at a time.

## Across cuisines

- **French**: mayonnaise and [Hollandaise Sauce](/recipes/hollandaise/) are emulsified by egg yolk; [Beurre Blanc](/recipes/beurre-blanc/) relies only on the milk proteins in the butter itself and the small amount of water in the reduction; adding cold butter to finish a sauce (*monter au beurre*) is also a small emulsification.
- **Italian**: [Cacio e Pepe](/recipes/cacio-e-pepe/), [Spaghetti Carbonara](/recipes/carbonara/), [Spaghetti Aglio e Olio](/recipes/aglio-olio/) and the *mantecatura* of risotto all use the starch in pasta water or stock to stabilize the fat of the cheese and butter.
- **Chinese**: milky-white fish soup and bone broth are generally explained this way: a hard rolling boil beats the fat into fine droplets, which are then stabilized by gelatin and proteins dissolved into the soup. This is why it has to be a "hard boil"; a low, slow simmer gives a clear soup.
- **Thai**: [Thai Green Curry with Chicken](/recipes/gaeng-keow-wan-gai/) first cooks thick coconut cream until oil beads separate out, then uses this oil to fry the curry paste until fragrant. Coconut milk is naturally an oil-in-water emulsion, and this step deliberately makes it separate.
- **Japanese**: the rich soup of Hakata-style *tonkotsu ramen* keeps pork bones at a steady rolling boil, beating the fat into the soup. The emulsified oil droplets and the suspended fine particles together make the soup opaque and thick white.
- **Korean**: *seolleongtang* boils beef bones continuously until the soup turns milky. Boiling disperses the fat into the soup, which is part of how the thick texture forms; the heat has to be enough to keep a rolling boil.

## Rescuing a broken sauce

A sauce that releases oil and separates means the oil droplets have largely merged. Whisking hard in the same pan usually cannot save it. A more reliable way is to **start a new small continuous phase**: put 1 tbsp warm water (for a butter sauce) or one egg yolk (for mayonnaise) in a clean bowl and whisk the broken sauce into it a little at a time, as if making the emulsion again from the start.
