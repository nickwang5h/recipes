## What it is

When raw egg heats, it thickens and finally sets; proteins do this. A protein is a folded chain of molecules. Heat breaks some of the forces that keep it folded and lets the chain unfold; this is **denaturation**. The unfolded proteins link to one another and form a network; this is **coagulation**. Denaturation changes the shape of a single molecule, while coagulation joins many molecules together.

The network can hold water inside it, so a steamed egg both sets and stays soft. With continued heating, the links between proteins multiply and the network tightens, squeezing the water out, and the egg turns hard and weeps. Meat also undergoes protein denaturation and shrinkage as it heats; cooked too long, it loses more water and its texture easily turns dry.

## Temperature and time decide the result together

Protein coagulation happens gradually. At first only a few proteins link up and the liquid thickens; as the links increase, a network forms that can support the whole food. An egg-based sauce should be stopped at the thickened stage, while a steamed egg needs the network to be strong enough to set.

| What you see | What the proteins are doing | Judgment in the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| The egg sauce starts to coat the noodles and still flows | Unfolding and gradually linking | Take it off the heat, keep stirring, and watch the thickness |
| The surface of the steamed egg has set and the centre jiggles slightly | The network is already holding the water | Stop heating; avoid steaming longer |
| The egg sauce turns grainy and the steamed egg weeps | The network has tightened and squeezed the water out | It is already overcooked; stop earlier next time |

High heat makes the egg touching the bottom of the pan set first while the inside is still very thin. Low heat, a water bath and constant stirring all reduce local overheating. After it comes off the heat, the pan and the food still hold heat, and coagulation continues. Plate the egg sauce as soon as it reaches the right thickness; waiting in the hot pan will dry it out too.

## The more water, the looser the network

Adding water or milk to beaten egg separates the protein molecules farther apart. They need a higher temperature to meet quickly and link into a network, and the network that forms is looser, so the texture is more tender. Adding sugar also delays the thickening of the egg. So plain beaten egg and a pudding with milk and sugar coagulate at different temperatures.

Dilution has its limit too. If too much water is added, the limited protein cannot support all the liquid, and the egg mixture can only thicken slightly; and if a steamed egg that has already set keeps heating, its loose network will likewise shrink and weep.

In sauces made with egg yolk, water has another job: it gives the oil droplets room to disperse. Pasta water helps the yolk paste mix smoothly, and the emulsifiers in the yolk help keep the fat in the sauce, see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/). Coagulation thickens the sauce; emulsification makes it smooth.

## Salt and acid make egg set earlier

The charge on the surface of protein molecules makes them repel one another. Salt weakens this repulsion, and acid can change the charge state of the proteins, so the egg starts to thicken at a lower temperature. Salt and acid used for seasoning also let proteins link earlier and tangle less tightly, giving more tender eggs.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: steamed egg relies on a diluted protein network to hold water; [Stir-fried Tomatoes and Eggs](/recipes/tomato-egg/) should come out of the pan just as the egg sets into large curds; [Steamed Whole Fish with Ginger and Green Onion](/recipes/steamed-fish/) should be taken off the heat when the flesh has just turned white and the part next to the bone is still slightly translucent.
- **French**: a [French Omelette](/recipes/french-omelette/) relies on fast stirring to set the egg into fine curds, set outside and still soft inside; [Hollandaise Sauce](/recipes/hollandaise/) uses both the thickening of the egg yolk and [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/), and must be heated gently while stirring.
- **Italian**: [Spaghetti Carbonara](/recipes/carbonara/) uses hot pasta and a warm pan to thicken the yolk sauce gradually, tossing it off the heat and stopping at the stage where it is smooth and coats the pasta.
- **Japanese**: [Chawanmushi](/recipes/chawanmushi/) dilutes the egg with *dashi* and then steams it gently until set, so the protein network holds the broth and forms a fine, smooth savoury custard. If the steam is too strong or the steaming goes on too long, the network tightens and the custard turns coarse and hard and weeps.
- **Korean**: [Gyeran Jjim](/recipes/gyeran-jjim/) puts water-diluted egg in a small earthenware pot and heats it covered directly over medium-low heat, setting into a fluffy, soft body. The bottom of the pot heats first and the centre later; too much heat leaves the bottom scorched and hard while the centre has not yet set.
