## What it is

Raw beaten egg thickens when heated and finally sets, and proteins do the work. A protein is a folded chain of molecules. Heat breaks some of the forces that hold the fold, so the chain unfolds; this is **denaturation**. The unfolded chains link up and weave a network; this is **coagulation**. Denaturation changes the shape of a single molecule, while coagulation joins many molecules together.

The network holds water inside it, so a steamed egg both sets and stays tender. But keep heating and the links multiply, the network tightens, and the water is squeezed out: the egg turns hard and weeps. Meat also denatures and shrinks as it heats; the longer it cooks, the more water it loses and the drier it gets.

## Temperature and time decide the result together

Proteins set a little at a time. At first only a few molecules link and the liquid thickens; as the links multiply, they weave a network that can hold up the whole dish. So an egg sauce has to stop at the thickened stage, while a steamed egg needs the network to be strong enough to set.

| What you see | What the proteins are doing | Judgment in the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| The egg sauce starts to coat the noodles and still flows | Unfolding and gradually linking | Take it off the heat, keep stirring, and watch the thickness |
| The surface of the steamed egg has set and the centre jiggles slightly | The network is already holding the water | Turn off the heat; don't steam longer |
| The egg sauce turns grainy and the steamed egg weeps | The network has tightened and squeezed the water out | It has gone too far; stop earlier next time |

High heat sets the egg touching the pan first while the inside is still thin. Low heat, a water bath and constant stirring all prevent hot spots. Off the heat, the pan and the food still hold warmth and the egg keeps setting, so plate an egg sauce as soon as it is thick enough; left in the hot pan, it dries out.

## The more water, the looser the network

Water or milk stirred into beaten egg puts the protein molecules farther apart. They need a higher temperature to meet quickly and link up, and the network they weave is looser, so the texture is more tender. Sugar also makes the egg thicken later. That is why plain beaten egg and a pudding with milk and sugar set at different temperatures.

But there is a limit to adding water. With too much, the limited protein cannot hold all the liquid, and the egg only thickens slightly. A steamed egg that has already set will still shrink and weep if it keeps heating, because its loose network contracts too.

In sauces made with egg yolk, water has another job: it gives the oil droplets room to spread out. Pasta water helps the yolk paste mix smoothly, and the emulsifiers in the yolk help keep the fat in the sauce, see [Emulsification](/principles/emulsification/). Coagulation thickens the sauce; emulsification makes it smooth.

## Salt and acid make egg set earlier

Protein molecules carry a charge on their surface, so they repel one another. Salt weakens that repulsion, and acid changes the charge on the proteins, so the egg starts to thicken at a lower temperature. Salt and acid added for seasoning also let the proteins link earlier and tangle less tightly, which gives a more tender egg.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: steamed egg relies on a diluted protein network to hold water; [Stir-fried Tomatoes and Eggs](/recipes/tomato-egg/) comes out of the pan just as the egg sets into large curds; [Steamed Whole Fish with Ginger and Green Onion](/recipes/steamed-fish/) comes off the heat when the flesh has just turned white and the part next to the bone is still slightly translucent.
- **French**: a [French Omelette](/recipes/french-omelette/) relies on fast stirring to set the egg into fine curds, set outside and still soft inside; [Hollandaise Sauce](/recipes/hollandaise/) uses both the thickening of the yolk and [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/), so it is heated gently while you stir.
- **Italian**: [Spaghetti Carbonara](/recipes/carbonara/) uses hot pasta and a warm pan to thicken the yolk sauce slowly; toss it off the heat and stop when it is smooth and coats the pasta.
- **Japanese**: [Chawanmushi](/recipes/chawanmushi/) dilutes the egg with *dashi* and steams it gently until set, so the protein network holds the broth and makes a fine, smooth savoury custard. If the steam is too strong or the steaming goes on too long, the network tightens and the custard turns coarse, hard and weepy.
- **Korean**: [Gyeran Jjim](/recipes/gyeran-jjim/) puts water-diluted egg in a small earthenware pot and heats it covered, directly over medium-low heat, until it sets into a fluffy, soft body. The bottom of the pot heats first and the centre later; with too much heat the bottom scorches and hardens before the centre has set.
