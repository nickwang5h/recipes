## What it is

**Umami** is a basic taste alongside sweet, sour, salty and bitter, often experienced as a broth-like savouriness. The main umami substances are **free glutamate** and **nucleotides** such as **inosinate** and **guanylate**. When they touch the **taste receptors**, the food tastes of umami. In 1908, the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda extracted glutamate from kombu broth and named this taste *umami*. The word "free" matters: glutamate still bound up inside whole proteins does not bring the same taste.

Tomatoes, kombu, soy sauce and aged cheese all supply glutamate; meat and fish often supply inosinate, and dried shiitake mushrooms supply guanylate. MSG is **monosodium glutamate**, and it gives this same kind of umami. The roasted smell of meat that has just been seared comes from volatile aroma compounds, which is another layer of flavour, see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).

## Pairing works better than simply adding more

When glutamate sits in the same bite as inosinate or guanylate, the umami is stronger than the sum of each alone. This is called **synergy**. Nucleotides strengthen the umami receptor's response to glutamate, so pairing two types of source often works better than adding more of just one ingredient.

| Ingredients that supply glutamate | Nucleotide sources to pair | Combination in the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| Soy sauce, fermented black beans | Meat, fish | Sauce for steamed fish; fermented black bean dishes with meat |
| Tomato | Beef | Tomato stewed together with meat |
| Kombu | Dried shiitake mushrooms | A savoury broth that can be made without meat |

When pairing, look first at what is already in the pot. If the soup already has meat in it, soy sauce alone may supply the other type of umami substance; pile in several strongly salty sauces at once and it turns too salty first. Synergy shows which way to pair, but how much to add still depends on the taste of the whole bite.

## Fermentation and ageing release flavour

When soy sauce, fermented black beans and cheese are made, enzymes break proteins down into smaller fragments and free amino acids, glutamate among them. That is why a little aged cheese or a fermented soy product can add noticeable savouriness to a whole pot. Some of the umami in food was there from the start, and some is released in processing.

## Water and salt decide the final bite

Umami substances have to dissolve in the liquid before they can reach the taste receptors as you take a bite. Too much water dilutes the flavour; reducing the sauce concentrates the flavours already there, and the salt along with them. When making a sauce, reduce it to the thickness you want first, then make the final adjustments with [salt](/principles/salt/).

Umami-rich ingredients often carry salt of their own. MSG supplies umami and also contains sodium, and it feels different in the mouth from table salt, so season with each in small amounts, separately. If the soup is already salty but still thin in flavour, add unsalted tomato, mushroom or meat broth; more soy sauce only pushes the saltiness higher. If the umami itself is too strong, dilute with unsalted broth.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: Cantonese superior stock is simmered from Jinhua ham, an old hen and lean meat; the glutamate of the aged ham with the inosinate of the chicken is synergy. The chili bean paste and fermented black beans paired with minced beef in [Mapo Tofu](/recipes/mapo-tofu/) are the same combination.
- **French**: [French Onion Soup](/recipes/french-onion-soup/) uses beef stock to carry the onions, and the aged cheese on top adds another layer of flavour; the browned, roasted aroma of the onions comes from heat.
- **Italian**: *Ragù alla bolognese* ([Ragù alla Bolognese](/recipes/ragu-alla-bolognese/)) pairs tomato with meat. The aged cheese added at the table brings free glutamate too.
- **Thai**: [Som Tam](/recipes/som-tam/) pounds fish sauce and dried shrimp into a sweet and sour dressing, so even raw fruit and vegetables get a salty, savoury base. The free glutamate released as the fish sauce ferments is an important source of this savouriness.
- **Vietnamese**: [Nước Chấm](/recipes/nuoc-cham/) dilutes fish sauce with water, lime juice and sugar, carrying its savouriness into a whole bite of rice noodles, lettuce and rice-paper rolls. The free glutamate in fish sauce brings the umami while its salt sets the saltiness, so consider the two together when mixing the sauce.
- **Japanese**: [Dashi](/recipes/dashi/) combines the glutamate of kombu with the inosinate of bonito flakes in one pot, a classic case of umami synergy. The finished dashi then goes into [Miso Soup](/recipes/miso-shiru/) or [Chawanmushi](/recipes/chawanmushi/), where a little seasoning is enough to carry the flavour of the whole dish.
- **Korean**: [Doenjang Jjigae](/recipes/doenjang-jjigae/) simmers fermented *doenjang* with dried anchovies and a little shrimp; the free amino acids in the *doenjang* and the savouriness of the seafood together hold up the base of the soup. Get the flavour out of these in the pot first, then judge the saltiness, so you don't oversalt the soup by adding more and more *doenjang*.
