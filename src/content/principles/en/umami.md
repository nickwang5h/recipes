## What it is

**Umami** is a basic taste alongside sweet, sour, salty and bitter, often experienced as a broth-like savouriness. The important umami substances are **free glutamate**, and **nucleotides** such as **inosinate** and **guanylate**. When they touch **taste receptors**, they give food its umami. In 1908, the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda extracted glutamate from kombu broth and named this taste *umami*. The word "free" here is crucial: glutamate still bound up inside whole proteins cannot directly bring the same taste.

Tomatoes, kombu, soy sauce and aged cheese can all supply glutamate; meat and fish often supply inosinate, and dried shiitake mushrooms are a source of guanylate. MSG is **monosodium glutamate**, and it supplies this same line of umami. The roasted smell you get from meat that has just been seared comes from volatile aroma compounds, which is another layer of flavour, see the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).

## Pairing works better than simply adding more

When glutamate is present in the same bite as inosinate or guanylate, the umami is stronger than the simple sum of each alone. This is called **synergy**. Nucleotides strengthen the umami receptor's response to glutamate, so pairing two types of source is often more effective than increasing just one ingredient.

| Ingredients that supply glutamate | Nucleotide sources to pair | Combination in the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| Soy sauce, fermented black beans | Meat, fish | Sauce for steamed fish; fermented black bean dishes with meat |
| Tomato | Beef | Tomato stewed together with meat |
| Kombu | Dried shiitake mushrooms | A savoury broth that can be made without meat |

When pairing, look first at what is already there. If the soup already has meat in it, adding soy sauce alone may supply the other type of umami substance; piling in several strongly salty sauces at once easily makes it too salty first. Synergy shows the direction to pair in, but how much to add still has to be judged by the taste of the whole bite.

## Fermentation and ageing release flavour

During the making of soy sauce, fermented black beans and cheese, enzymes break proteins down into smaller fragments and free amino acids, glutamate among them. This explains why a small amount of aged cheese or a fermented soy product can add noticeable savouriness to a whole pot. The umami in food comes both from ingredients that were already there and from components released during processing.

## Water and salt decide the final bite

Umami substances have to dissolve in the liquid to reach the taste receptors when you take a bite. Too much water dilutes the flavour; reducing the sauce concentrates the flavours already there, and the salt along with them. When making a sauce, reduce it to the target thickness first, then make final adjustments with [salt](/principles/salt/).

Umami-rich ingredients often carry salt of their own. MSG supplies umami and also contains sodium; its effect in the mouth differs from table salt, so season with each in small amounts, separately. If the soup is already salty but still lacks a full-bodied flavour, add unsalted tomato, mushroom or meat broth; adding more soy sauce will push the saltiness higher. If even the umami is too strong, dilute with unsalted broth.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: Cantonese superior stock is simmered from Jinhua ham, an old hen and lean meat; the glutamate of the aged ham combined with the inosinate of the chicken is exactly synergy. The chili bean paste and fermented black beans paired with minced beef in [Mapo Tofu](/recipes/mapo-tofu/) are the same combination.
- **French**: [French Onion Soup](/recipes/french-onion-soup/) uses beef stock to carry the onions, with aged cheese on top adding another layer of flavour; the browned, roasted aroma of the onions is formed by heating.
- **Italian**: *Ragù alla bolognese* ([Ragù alla Bolognese](/recipes/ragu-alla-bolognese/)) pairs tomato with meat. The aged cheese added at the table also supplies free glutamate.
- **Thai**: [Som Tam](/recipes/som-tam/) pounds fish sauce and dried shrimp into a sweet and sour dressing, so even raw fruit and vegetables have a salty, savoury base. The free glutamate released as the fish sauce ferments is an important source of this savouriness.
- **Vietnamese**: [Nước Chấm](/recipes/nuoc-cham/) dilutes fish sauce with water, lime juice and sugar, carrying its savouriness into a whole bite of rice noodles, lettuce and rice-paper rolls. The free glutamate in fish sauce brings the umami while its salt sets the saltiness, so consider the two together when mixing the sauce.
- **Japanese**: [Dashi](/recipes/dashi/) combines the glutamate of kombu with the inosinate of bonito flakes in one pot, a classic case of umami synergy. The finished dashi is then used for [Miso Soup](/recipes/miso-shiru/) or [Chawanmushi](/recipes/chawanmushi/), where a little seasoning is enough to carry the flavour of the whole dish.
- **Korean**: [Doenjang Jjigae](/recipes/doenjang-jjigae/) simmers fermented *doenjang* with dried anchovies and a little shrimp; the free amino acids in the *doenjang* and the savouriness of the seafood together hold up the base of the soup. Get these flavours out in the pot first and then judge the saltiness, to avoid overseasoning the soup by adding more and more *doenjang*.
