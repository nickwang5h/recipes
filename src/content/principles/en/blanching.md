## What it is

**Blanching** (*chao shui*) is a brief boil in water before the actual cooking. Changing the water after blanching meat and bones carries away the exudate on the surface and some of the off-odours; blanching vegetables is often done to remove the raw taste, reduce bitterness or astringency, or cook the texture to the point the next step needs.

The red liquid that seeps out of meat is often called "blood water". It is mainly meat juice, and the red comes from **myoglobin** in the muscle; bone-in cuts may also hold some blood. When heated, the soluble proteins [denature and coagulate](/principles/protein-coagulation/), gather into grey-brown particles, and float up mixed with fat, debris and bubbles to form **scum**. How much there is depends on the cuts, the cut surfaces and how well the meat was washed.

## Cold water or boiling water

| How it starts | Common use | What to judge |
|---|---|---|
| Meat and bones in cold water, heated together | Pre-treatment for stews and light-coloured bone broth | Remove once the surface changes colour and the scum rises in a clump, then wash off the attached particles |
| Ingredients added after the water boils | Greens, spinach and other vegetables | Use plenty of water, add in batches, and remove when as soft or firm as the next step needs |

Starting in cold water gives the soluble substances time to enter the water and then clump together, so the scum is easy to clear in one go. Putting vegetables into boiling water heats them quickly, shortens the softening, and keeps the colour a brighter green.

Blanching meat only has to finish cleaning the surface. Continuing to cook lets the juices and flavour flow into water that will be thrown away, and lean meat also dries out more easily. If blanched greens will be used later, cooling them promptly slows softening from the residual heat; for ones to be stir-fried right away, just lift them out and drain.

## Where the off-odour goes

The off-odours of meat and fish come from many substances. The **trimethylamine** common in sea fish carries a fishy smell; aldehydes and the like formed by fat oxidation also bring unpleasant smells. Blanching lets some of these substances dissolve into the water and others evaporate with the heat, and after the water is changed the amount left in the dish is lower.

**Shaoxing wine** provides a wine aroma, and the water and ethanol in the wine can also help dissolve different flavour compounds. Heating in an open pot lets some of the volatile smells escape, and the effect comes from the wine, the heat and evaporation working together. The pungent aroma of ginger and the sulphur-containing aroma of green onion change the combination of smells in the pot and soften the meaty off-odour. Cut ginger and green onion release their aroma more easily, see [spices and aromatics](/principles/aromatics/).

When preparing fish, also remove the gills, the innards and the bloodline completely. Delicate fish that is blanched or boiled for a long time just to remove the fishy smell loses its tenderness first. If the ingredient already smells spoiled, throw it away.

## Browning and soup colour afterward

Blanched meat is grey-white and moist on the surface. To brown it afterward, drain it thoroughly and pat it dry; until the water evaporates, the surface can hardly form an obvious [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). In red-braising, the caramel colour and the soy sauce do the colouring separately; blanching only means there is one less layer of grey scum on the surface.

For a clear soup, cleaning the ingredients and the pot after blanching and then simmering gently in fresh water reduces suspended protein particles. For a milky-white soup, the following hard boil has to break up the fat, with proteins and the like helping to stabilize it, see [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/). A clear soup also needs skimming and controlled boiling during the later cooking, to reduce the particles and fat dispersing into the soup.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Pork Rib, Chinese Yam and Corn Soup](/recipes/pai-gu-shan-yao-yu-mi-tang/) first blanches the ribs and washes off the scum, then stews them in fresh water, which makes it easier to get a clean-looking surface.
- **French**: [Blanquette de Veau](/recipes/blanquette-de-veau/) first blanches and rinses the meat, then simmers it gently in fresh stock, keeping the meat pieces and the sauce light in colour.
- **Italian**: spinach is boiled until soft, drained and squeezed dry, then tossed in butter, a typical way of cooking vegetables twice. The first step adjusts the texture, and the second coats the spinach in fat and aroma.
- **Vietnamese**: for [Phở Bò](/recipes/pho-bo/), beef bones start in cold water, are boiled briefly, the water is poured off, the bones and pot are washed, and fresh water goes in to make the broth. It is still simmered gently and skimmed afterward; blanching the bones is only the first step to a clear broth.
- **Japanese**: [Horenso no Goma-ae](/recipes/horenso-no-gomaae/) puts spinach into boiling water briefly, cools it, squeezes out the water, and then dresses it with sesame paste. The brief blanch adjusts how soft or firm the leaves are, and squeezing keeps leftover water from thinning the dressing.
- **Korean**: spinach side dish (*sigeumchi-namul*) is blanched briefly, cooled under cold water and squeezed, then mixed with garlic, soy sauce, toasted sesame oil and sesame seeds. The spinach in [Bibimbap](/recipes/bibimbap/) is also blanched and dressed first, then combined with the other toppings over the rice.
