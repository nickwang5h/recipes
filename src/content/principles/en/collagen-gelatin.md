## What it is

**Collagen** is a structural protein in connective tissue. It surrounds muscle fibres and bundles, and is a major component of tendons and skin. Its molecules are three chains twisted into a triple helix, forming a tough structure. One main reason sinewy meat is hard to chew is that this structure is still intact.

Under water and sustained heat, the collagen helix unwinds and some of its cross-links gradually break, forming **gelatin**, which can disperse in hot water. With less holding the meat together, it pulls apart easily; the gelatin that enters the liquid adds a thick, smooth feel. Tenderness and a rich, glossy sauce therefore often come together.

## The cut decides whether a long cook is worth it

Parts that exercise and bear weight usually have more connective tissue. Beef shoulder, beef brisket and pig's trotters suit slow softening in moist heat; tenderloin has little connective tissue, and a long stew mainly makes the muscle lose water. The animal's age also changes how firm the collagen cross-links are, so pieces of the same size can soften at different speeds.

| Ingredient | Main result of long moist heat | What to check in the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| Much fascia and connective tissue | Collagen turns to gelatin and the meat gradually loosens | Check with a fork whether the sinew and the meat separate |
| Mostly lean meat, little connective tissue | Muscle fibres keep losing water | Stop at the right doneness |
| With skin and tendon | Gelatin gradually builds up in the liquid | Watch both the softening of skin and tendon and the texture of the liquid |

The tough connective tissue on meat may also contain **elastin**, which hardly softens in ordinary stewing. Thick, tough fascia is best trimmed off beforehand; you cannot expect all the "sinew" to dissolve in the pot.

## Temperature speeds the change, time completes the softening

Collagen turning into gelatin is a gradual process. Collagen fibres begin to shrink at about 60°C (140°F), squeezing out the meat juices; to convert in quantity, they need to stay above 70°C (160°F) long enough. The higher the temperature, the faster the change, but the centre of a piece has to heat up first; the outside can be soft while tough sinew remains inside. Even-sized pieces and a steady supply of heat from liquid and steam reduce the differences between pieces in the same pot, see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).

Muscle proteins also shrink and expel water when heated, see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). A stew therefore passes through a "cooked but still tough" stage: the muscle is cooked and the collagen has not yet softened enough. Continuing at a gentle simmer from there is easier to control than a hard rolling boil.

Judge the stopping point on the thickest piece. A fork that goes in fairly easily, meat that parts along the fibres with a light twist, and sinew that is soft too, are close to the state a stew is after. Cook on and the meat can still become looser, but it may also get drier and drier. The slipperiness gelatin gives improves the mouthfeel, but it cannot put back the juices that have already been lost.

## After cooling, how gelatin sets

In hot broth the gelatin chains are dispersed; on cooling, some segments form links again, weaving a network that traps water, which is called a **gel**. A broth that sets to a jelly in the refrigerator and flows again when reheated shows this network forming and unravelling.

Whether a broth sets also depends on the gelatin concentration. With a lot of water the gelatin is diluted and the broth may stay liquid when cold; reducing it moderately raises the concentration. The layer of set fat on top of a rich broth is different from gelatin jelly: fat tends to collect in the upper layer, while the gelatin gel runs through the water phase.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Red-Braised Pork Belly](/recipes/hong-shao-rou/) lets the skin and connective tissue soften gradually, and the final reduction concentrates the gelatin and the seasoned sauce together.
- **French**: [Beef Bourguignon](/recipes/boeuf-bourguignon/) suits beef with more connective tissue, slow-stewed until the pieces part easily.
- **Italian**: the Milanese *ossobuco* (braised veal shank) uses collagen-rich shank, cut into bone-in slices and slow-braised until the meat leaves the bone and the sauce turns sticky; the minced meat in [Ragù alla Bolognese](/recipes/ragu-alla-bolognese/) also becomes tender after several hours of gentle simmering, and the sauce gets thicker.
- **Thai**: [Massaman Curry](/recipes/gaeng-massaman/) stews beef shank slowly in coconut milk so the collagen in the connective tissue gradually turns to gelatin. When the meat is fully cooked the sinew may still be hard; wait until the sinew is soft too before stopping.
- **Vietnamese**: [Phở Bò](/recipes/pho-bo/) makes its broth gently from beef bones and brisket, and the collagen in the connective tissue turns to gelatin, giving the clear broth a thick, smooth feel. The meat is lifted out once it reaches the softness wanted, and the bones can keep simmering.
- **Japanese**: *kakuni* cooks pork belly slowly until soft, then seasons it with soy sauce, mirin and the like. Long moist heat softens the connective tissue so the pieces pull apart easily.
- **Korean**: *jokbal* simmers skin-on pig's trotters for a long time in a seasoned broth until the meat comes off the bone easily. The collagen in the skin and tendon turning into gelatin is the key to their going from tough to soft.
