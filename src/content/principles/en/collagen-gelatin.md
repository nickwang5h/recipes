## What it is

**Collagen** is a structural protein in connective tissue. It wraps around muscle fibres and bundles, and it is the main component of tendons and skin. Its molecules are three chains twisted into a triple helix, which makes a very tough structure. Sinewy meat is hard to chew mainly because that structure is still intact.

With water and steady heat, the helix unwinds, some of its cross-links break, and the collagen becomes **gelatin**, which disperses in hot water. With less holding the meat together, it pulls apart easily; the gelatin that reaches the liquid makes it thick and smooth. That is why tender meat and a rich, glossy sauce usually arrive together.

## Cuts with a lot of sinew are worth a long cook

Muscles that move and bear weight have more connective tissue. Beef shoulder, beef brisket and pig's trotters soften slowly in moist heat; tenderloin has little connective tissue, so a long stew only makes the muscle lose water. The animal's age also changes how firm the collagen cross-links are, so pieces of the same size can soften at different speeds.

| Ingredient | Result of a long cook in moist heat | How to tell |
|---|---|---|
| Much fascia and connective tissue | Collagen turns to gelatin and the meat gradually loosens | Test with a fork: the sinew and the meat separate |
| Mostly lean meat, little connective tissue | Muscle fibres keep losing water | Stop when the meat is done |
| With skin and tendon | Gelatin builds up in the liquid | Check that the skin and tendon are soft and the liquid has thickened |

The toughest connective tissue on meat may also contain **elastin**, which hardly softens in ordinary stewing. Trim thick, tough fascia off beforehand; not all the "sinew" will dissolve in the pot.

## Higher heat is faster, but it takes time to soften

Collagen turns into gelatin gradually. Collagen fibres begin to shrink at about 60°C (140°F), squeezing out the meat juices; they need to stay above 70°C (160°F) long enough to convert in quantity. The higher the temperature, the faster the change, but the centre of a piece is always the last to heat up: the outside can be soft while tough sinew remains inside. Cut the pieces to an even size and keep heat coming steadily from the liquid and steam, and the pieces in one pot soften together; see [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).

Muscle proteins also shrink and expel water when heated; see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). So partway through a stew there is a spell when the meat is cooked but the sinew is still tough: the muscle is done and the collagen has not softened. Keep it at a gentle simmer from there; that is easier to control than a hard rolling boil.

Test the thickest piece to decide when to stop. When a fork slides in fairly easily, the meat parts along the fibres with a light twist, and the sinew is soft too, the stew is ready. Cook on and the meat will loosen further, but it also gets drier and drier. The silkiness of gelatin improves the mouthfeel, but it cannot put back the juices already lost.

## Once the broth cools, the gelatin sets

In hot broth the gelatin chains are spread out. As it cools, some segments link up again and weave a network that traps water; this is a **gel**. Broth that sets to a jelly in the refrigerator and turns liquid again when reheated is showing that network forming and coming apart.

Whether a broth sets depends on how much gelatin it holds. With a lot of water the gelatin is diluted and the broth may stay liquid when cold; reducing it moderately raises the concentration. The layer of set fat on top of a rich broth is not gelatin jelly: fat tends to collect in the upper layer, while the gel runs through the water.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Red-Braised Pork Belly](/recipes/hong-shao-rou/) lets the skin and connective tissue soften gradually, and the final reduction concentrates the gelatin and the seasoned sauce together.
- **French**: [Beef Bourguignon](/recipes/boeuf-bourguignon/) suits beef with plenty of connective tissue, slow-stewed until the pieces fall apart at a touch.
- **Italian**: the Milanese *ossobuco* (braised veal shank) uses collagen-rich shank, cut into bone-in slices and slow-braised until the meat leaves the bone and the sauce turns sticky; the minced meat in [Ragù alla Bolognese](/recipes/ragu-alla-bolognese/) also turns tender after several hours of gentle simmering, and the sauce thickens.
- **Thai**: [Massaman Curry](/recipes/gaeng-massaman/) stews beef shank slowly in coconut milk, and the collagen in the connective tissue gradually turns to gelatin. The sinew may still be hard when the meat is cooked through; wait until it is soft too before stopping.
- **Vietnamese**: [Phở Bò](/recipes/pho-bo/) simmers beef bones and brisket gently, and the collagen in the connective tissue turns to gelatin, so the clear broth feels thick and smooth. Lift the meat out once it is soft enough; the bones can keep simmering.
- **Japanese**: *kakuni* cooks pork belly slowly until soft, then seasons it with soy sauce, mirin and the like. Long moist heat softens the connective tissue so the pieces fall apart at a touch.
- **Korean**: *jokbal* simmers skin-on pig's trotters for a long time in a seasoned broth until the meat comes off the bone easily. The collagen in the skin and tendon turns to gelatin, and that is what takes the trotters from tough to soft.
