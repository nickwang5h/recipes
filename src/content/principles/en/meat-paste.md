## What it is

Stir salt into a bowl of minced meat for a while and it changes from separate bits into a shiny mass that pulls into strands. In the pot, that paste sets into one springy piece that is still juicy when you bite it. Fish balls, meatballs, har gow filling and fish cakes all come about this way.

The key players are the meat's **myofibrillar proteins**, above all **myosin**. They barely dissolve in plain water; salt is what dissolves and spreads them out. Once out, the proteins tangle into a thick **meat paste** or **fish paste**; when heated, they link into a three-dimensional network that holds water in its gaps. That is **gelling**.

## Salt first, then mix

Commercial fish-paste products usually contain 1–3% salt. Without salt the paste does not gel when heated, or only sets into a loose crumble. So in a filling the salt goes in first, before anything else.

Once the salt is in, mix. Mixing spreads the dissolved proteins out so they meet and join; the paste slowly thickens and turns sticky. Chinese cooks call this point *shang jin*. Cook it before it gets there and the balls fall apart at a touch.

## The network holds the water

A paste that has been mixed until sticky can take in a good deal of water; this is "beating in water" for a filling. Add the water in several lots, waiting each time until the last lot has gone in completely and none is left loose in the bottom of the bowl. There is only so much protein: add too much water too fast and the network can't hold it, and no amount of mixing brings it back.

| What you do | Why | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Salt first | salt dissolves the myofibrillar proteins | the mince starts to turn sticky |
| Mix in one direction | the dissolved proteins spread out and link up | the paste thickens, shines and pulls into strands |
| Add water in lots | the network holds only so much water | no loose water in the bottom of the bowl |
| Add egg white | egg white is the protein most often added to help fish paste gel | the paste sets more firmly |

## Temperature: cold first, then gentle heat

Salted fish paste held for a while between 5 and 40°C (40 and 105°F) first sets into a very soft, stretchy gel called *suwari*. An enzyme that occurs naturally in fish muscle links the proteins together below 40°C (105°F), and after this step the gel heats into something firmer. Proteins from different fish unfold at different temperatures, so the best time and temperature vary too.

Keep the ingredients cold while you make the paste. Industrial fish paste is washed in water at 5–10°C (40–50°F); Escoffier set his fine forcemeat in a bowl on ice before working in the cream little by little.

The final heating should be gentle: keep the water below a boil so the soft balls are not knocked apart or broken. Overcook them and the protein network tightens, squeezes out water, and the balls turn hard and dry (see [protein coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/)).

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: for [Hangzhou fish balls in clear soup](/recipes/qing-tang-yu-yuan/), the fish paste is salted and stirred in one direction for half an hour, egg white is added, and the balls go into cold water that is heated slowly and never allowed to boil. [Clear-stewed lion's head meatballs](/recipes/qing-dun-shi-zi-tou/) use hand-chopped pork, salted and mixed until sticky, then water beaten in over three additions; the meat keeps its texture, so the balls are tender, while a version pounded to a fine paste comes out firmer.
- **French**: Escoffier's *farce mousseline* pounds chicken or fish with salt to a fine paste, works in egg white little by little and sieves it, then sets the whole bowl on ice and slowly works in thick cream to make exceptionally delicate *quenelles*. He points out that without egg white the forcemeat takes up much less cream.
- **Italian**: [meatballs in tomato sauce](/recipes/polpette-al-sugo/) go the other way: milk-soaked bread and egg hold them together, and everything is mixed only until even, never packed or slapped. The meatballs are soft and tender rather than springy.
- **Thai**: for [tod mun pla](/recipes/tod-mun-pla/), fish and salty red curry paste are blended together into a fine, sticky paste that holds a heap on a spoon; long beans are mixed in and the paste is fried into springy little cakes.
