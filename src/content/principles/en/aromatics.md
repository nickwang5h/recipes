## What it is

The smell of a spice comes from **volatile aroma compounds** that enter the air and reach the nose. They sit in the plant's oil cells and glands. Cutting, crushing and grinding break these open, and heat makes the aroma come out faster and spread further.

Many aroma compounds in spices are strongly **fat-soluble** and move into fat more easily. The oil carries them through the dish and releases the aroma gradually in the mouth. Water extracts some flavour too, so spices still work in soup. The heat of chilies comes from **capsaicin**, which doesn't evaporate easily, so after a long cook the aroma may fade while the heat stays.

## Whole spices infuse slowly, powders release at once

| Form | How it releases | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Whole pieces | Small surface area, slower infusion, easy to fish out | Star anise and cassia bark in braising liquids and stews |
| Cracked or coarsely crushed | Interior exposed, faster release | Sichuan peppercorns and black pepper, briefly stir-fried or infused in oil |
| Fine powder | Large surface area, releases fast, burns easily and loses aroma easily | Thirteen-spice powder or chili powder, heated briefly or mixed into wet ingredients |

The **anethole** in star anise and the **cinnamaldehyde** in cassia bark each give the spice its main note. Powders spread that smell out quickly, while whole spices give it up gradually as they cook. Thirteen-spice powder is a blend of many ground spices, and the recipe and fineness vary, so start with a small amount, mix well and taste before adding more. That way no single aroma overwhelms the pot.

Ground spices also meet the air more easily. After a powder sits for a long time its aroma fades, and using more won't bring back the original layers. Grind fresh where you can, and store spices sealed, away from light, and away from the heat and steam of the stove.

## Sizzling in oil, pouring hot oil and braising each use their own heat and time

**Sizzling aromatics in oil** (*qiang guo*) puts green onion, ginger, garlic, Sichuan peppercorns or dried chilies in the oil first, lets the aroma go into the fat, and then lets the main ingredient pick up that layer of flavour. Once you smell the aroma and the colour has just started to change, don't wait longer. Finely chopped garlic and chili powder burn especially easily, so never wait until they turn black. The heat has to suit how finely things are chopped, see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/).

**Pouring hot oil** (*you po*) lets hot oil run briefly over ground spices and brings their aroma out fast. The powder is fine and has a large surface area, so oil that is too hot scorches it. Pour in stages and stir at once; check that it foams normally and smells fragrant before pouring more. Black specks, heavy smoke and an acrid burnt smell mean the oil was too hot. How hot the oil should be depends on the recipe and the coarseness of the powder.

**Braising liquids** (*lu shui*) draw flavour out with water and fat together. Whole star anise and cassia bark can go into a spice bag, cook in early on, and come out once the flavour is enough. Taking out the spices doesn't take out the flavour that has already dissolved into the liquid. As a braising liquid is reduced again and again, the salt and some of the less volatile flavours concentrate too.

## The longer it cooks, the more the aroma changes

Heat speeds up infusion, and it speeds up evaporation too. When the whole kitchen fills with fragrance as the pot comes to a boil, part of the aroma is leaving the food. After a long cook, the easily evaporated fresh top notes drop away and the proportions of what remains change; the spices themselves also undergo chemical changes.

So whole dry spices go into the pot early, to give them time to infuse. Fine powders should be stir-fried briefly until fragrant, then the main ingredient or a liquid should go in promptly to bring the pan temperature down. Tender fresh herbs such as parsley and basil go in at the end; thyme and bay leaves are often used to build the base flavour of a stew. When to add a spice depends on which aroma you want to keep.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Sizzling Chili Oil](/recipes/you-po-la-zi/) uses hot oil to extract the flavour of chilies and spices; [Spiced Soy-Braised Beef Shank](/recipes/lu-cai/) infuses whole spices slowly instead, for a different layer of aroma.
- **French**: a *bouquet garni* often ties thyme, bay leaf and parsley stems together to go into the soup, so they can be fished out together at the end. [Beef Bourguignon](/recipes/boeuf-bourguignon/) also builds its base with thyme and bay leaf.
- **Italian**: [Spaghetti Aglio e Olio](/recipes/aglio-olio/) first carries the flavour of garlic and chili into the olive oil over low heat, then adds the pasta and pasta water. The parsley is tossed in off the heat to keep its fresh aroma.
- **Thai**: curry pastes pound lemongrass, galangal and the like into a fine purée, then fry it in fat until fragrant. [Thai Green Curry with Chicken](/recipes/gaeng-keow-wan-gai/) fries the paste in thick coconut cream and its oil, and the sweet basil is held back and tossed in off the heat.
- **Vietnamese**: [Phở Bò](/recipes/pho-bo/) first chars ginger and onion for fragrance, removes the burnt skin, and puts them into the soup with whole star anise, cassia bark and cloves. The spices infuse in a gentle simmer, while fresh cilantro and the green part of the green onion wait until the bowl is assembled.
- **Japanese**: [Horenso no Goma-ae](/recipes/horenso-no-gomaae/) first toasts the sesame seeds until fragrant, then grinds them in a mortar into a dressing. Toasting brings out the aroma, and grinding lets the sesame's oil and aroma spread into the dressing.
- **Korean**: [Sundubu Jjigae](/recipes/sundubu-jjigae/) first stir-fries garlic and onion until fragrant, while the Korean chili flakes are mixed with oil into a paste before going into the soup. The oil carries the chili's flavour out, and the paste also keeps the powder off a dry hot pan.
