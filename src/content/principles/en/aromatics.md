## What it is

The smell of a spice comes from **volatile aroma compounds** that can enter the air and reach the nose. They sit in structures such as the plant's oil cells and glands. Cutting, crushing and grinding break these structures, and heat speeds up the release and spread of the aroma compounds.

Many aroma compounds in spices are strongly **fat-soluble** and move into fat more easily. The oil carries them through the dish and releases the aroma gradually in the mouth. Water can also extract some flavour, so spices still work in soup; the heat of chilies also comes from **capsaicin**, which does not evaporate easily, so after a long cook the aroma may fade while the heat remains.

## Whole versus ground

| Form | How it releases | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Whole pieces | Small surface area, slower infusion, easy to fish out | Star anise and cassia bark in braising liquids and stews |
| Cracked or coarsely crushed | Interior exposed, faster release | Sichuan peppercorns and black pepper, briefly stir-fried or infused in oil |
| Fine powder | Large surface area, releases fast, burns easily and loses aroma easily | Thirteen-spice powder or chili powder, heated briefly or mixed into wet ingredients |

The **anethole** in star anise and the **cinnamaldehyde** in cassia bark form their own main notes. Powders spread this kind of smell out quickly, while whole spices infuse gradually during cooking. Thirteen-spice powder is a blend of many ground spices, and its recipe and fineness vary; start with a small amount, mix well, taste, and add more if needed, so that no single aroma overwhelms the pot.

Grinding also exposes spices to air more easily. After powders sit for a long time their aroma fades, and using more may not bring back the original layers. Grind fresh where you can, and store spices sealed, away from light, and away from the heat and steam of the stove.

## Sizzling in oil, pouring hot oil, and braising liquids

**Sizzling aromatics in oil** (*qiang guo*) puts green onion, ginger, garlic, Sichuan peppercorns or dried chilies in the oil first, lets the aroma go into the fat, and then has the main ingredient pick up that layer of flavour. Move on to the next step when you smell the aroma and the colour has just started to change. Finely chopped garlic and chili powder burn especially easily, so do not wait until they turn black. The heat has to suit how finely things are chopped, see [oil temperature and deep frying](/principles/oil-temperature/).

**Pouring hot oil** (*you po*) lets hot oil touch the ground spices briefly and release their aroma fast. The powder is fine and has a large surface area, so oil that is too hot will scorch it. Pour in stages and stir at once; first check that it foams normally and smells fragrant before continuing. Black specks, heavy smoke and an acrid burnt smell mean it got too hot. The exact oil temperature should be adjusted to the recipe and the coarseness of the powder.

**Braising liquids** (*lu shui*) draw flavour out with water and fat together. Whole star anise and cassia bark can go into a spice bag, be cooked in during the first stage, and be removed once the flavour is enough; taking out the spices does not remove the flavour that has already dissolved into the liquid. As a braising liquid is reduced again and again, salt and some of the less volatile flavours also become concentrated.

## How aroma changes the longer it is heated

Heating speeds up infusion on one hand and evaporation on the other. When the whole kitchen fills with fragrance as the pot comes to a boil, part of the aroma is leaving the food. After a long cook, the easily evaporated fresh top notes decrease and the proportions of what remains change; the spices themselves also undergo chemical changes.

Whole dry spices suit going into the pot early, to give them time to infuse. Fine powders should be stir-fried briefly until fragrant, then the main ingredient or a liquid should go in promptly to bring the temperature of the pan down. Tender fresh herbs such as parsley and basil suit being added at the end; thyme and bay leaves are often used to build the base flavour of a stew. The moment of adding should follow the aroma you want to keep.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Sizzling Chili Oil](/recipes/you-po-la-zi/) uses hot oil to extract the flavour of chilies and spices; [Spiced Soy-Braised Beef Shank](/recipes/lu-cai/) instead infuses whole spices slowly, giving a different layer of aroma.
- **French**: a *bouquet garni* often ties thyme, bay leaf and parsley stems together to go into the soup, so it can be fished out at the end. [Beef Bourguignon](/recipes/boeuf-bourguignon/) also builds its base with thyme and bay leaf.
- **Italian**: [Spaghetti Aglio e Olio](/recipes/aglio-olio/) first carries the flavour of garlic and chili into the olive oil over low heat, then adds the pasta and pasta water; parsley is tossed in off the heat to keep its fresh aroma.
- **Thai**: curry pastes pound lemongrass, galangal and the like into a fine purée and then fry it in fat until fragrant. [Thai Green Curry with Chicken](/recipes/gaeng-keow-wan-gai/) fries the paste in thick coconut cream and its oil, and sweet basil is held back and tossed in off the heat.
- **Vietnamese**: [Phở Bò](/recipes/pho-bo/) first chars ginger and onion for fragrance, removes the burnt skin, and puts them into the soup with whole star anise, cassia bark and cloves. The spices infuse in a gentle simmer, while fresh cilantro and the green part of the green onion are kept for when the bowl is assembled.
- **Japanese**: [Horenso no Goma-ae](/recipes/horenso-no-gomaae/) first toasts the sesame seeds until fragrant, then grinds them in a mortar and mixes them into a dressing. Toasting builds the aroma, and grinding lets the sesame's oil and aroma spread into the dressing.
- **Korean**: [Sundubu Jjigae](/recipes/sundubu-jjigae/) first stir-fries garlic and onion until fragrant, while Korean chili flakes are mixed with oil into a paste before going into the soup. The oil carries out the chili's flavour, and the powder also avoids direct scorching on a dry hot pan.
