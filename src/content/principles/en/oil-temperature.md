## What it is

**Deep frying** heats food by immersing it in hot oil. Flowing oil carries heat to the surface, and the heat then conducts inward. Water at the surface keeps evaporating, leaving a dry, porous crust; browning reactions bring colour and aroma, and the inside can stay soft.

Deep frying at normal pressure usually uses oil at about 160–190°C (320–375°F). This is the common working range; the specific target should be chosen to suit the ingredient and recipe. The oil is far above the boiling point of water, yet the moist interior does not reach the oil's temperature right away: evaporating water carries heat away, and the temperatures of the dry crust and the moist inner layer gradually pull apart. For the path heat takes, see [Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation](/principles/heat-transfer/).

## The temperature after the food goes in is what counts

When food goes into the pan, it draws heat out of the oil and the oil temperature falls. The more water in the food, the colder the food, and the more you add at once, the bigger the drop. If the oil has not recovered and the next batch goes in, the frying drags on and the crust is slow to crisp.

Use a thermometer to measure the oil, with the probe kept off the bottom of the pot. Read it once before the food goes in and keep watching after, waiting until the temperature recovers to the recipe's target before frying more. Without a thermometer, bubbles can only help you see how strongly water vapour is escaping; bubbles around a chopstick depend on the chopstick's moisture and material and cannot be matched to a fixed temperature.

| What's happening in the pan | Likely cause | What to adjust |
|---|---|---|
| Slow to colour, soft crust, frying drags on | Oil too cool after the food goes in | Fry fewer pieces per batch and let the oil recover |
| Outside darkens quickly, inside not cooked | Oil too hot, or the food too thick | Lower the temperature and check that the pieces are even |
| Some pieces burnt and some pale in the same pot | Uneven sizes, or pieces piled together | Separate the food and fry in batches by size |

## Crispness and oil uptake both come down to water

The starch in a dredging or batter [gelatinizes](/principles/starch-gelatinization/), and only by continuing to lose water does it form a crisp crust. A thin, even coating dries easily; with a thick coating, the surface may already be coloured while the batter inside is still wet and sticky. The sugars and proteins in the meat and the flour can also undergo the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).

Water vapour flowing outward can block some of the oil from getting in, but the crisp crust still has pores. As the food cools after coming out, the steam inside condenses and the oil clinging to the surface can be drawn in. Draining promptly after lifting out, blotting off the excess oil, and then spreading the pieces out to let steam escape both reduces surface oil and keeps the pieces from steaming each other soft.

Double-frying separates cooking through from the final drying: first let the inside cook, then return it to the oil briefly so the crust dries further. Whether a dish needs a second fry depends on thickness and the state of the crust; a thin piece that is already deep in colour will only turn more bitter if fried longer.

## Choosing oil and smoke

The **smoke point** is the temperature at which oil starts to smoke continuously, and it relates to how refined the oil is, its impurities and how it has been used. Choose an oil suited to high-temperature cooking and keep the actual oil temperature below its smoke point. Smoke means it is time to lower the temperature; do not treat it as a signal to add the food. Crumbs left over after frying will keep burning in the next batch and also taint the flavour.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: oil temperature is traditionally described as "how many tenths hot" (*ji cheng re*), judged by the ripples on the oil surface and how much it smokes, with no uniform temperature equivalents. Diced meat coated with a starch marinade is often first passed through warm oil so that the coating sets and the meat turns white without taking colour; dry-frying and double-frying need hotter oil. The chicken dice in [Kung Pao Chicken](/recipes/kung-pao-chicken/) are also set quickly by a hot pan and hot oil.
- **French**: for french fries, cooking through the inside and drying the surface are handled separately; the crisp crust after the second fry comes from water continuing to be lost, a mechanism you can compare with [starch gelatinization](/principles/starch-gelatinization/).
- **Italian**: in Sicilian *arancini*, the breadcrumb coating loses water and turns crisp while the rice inside stays soft; for how the rice grains absorb water as they heat, see [Risotto alla Milanese](/recipes/risotto-alla-milanese/).
- **Vietnamese**: rice-paper-wrapped fried spring rolls (*chả giò*) can be fried first at a lower oil temperature and then at a higher one to double-fry, making the wrapper crisper. Rice paper itself does not take colour easily, so don't keep extending the frying in pursuit of a deep brown.
- **Japanese**: [Karaage](/recipes/karaage/) is drained of its marinade and thinly dredged, then goes into the oil in batches. A thin coating loses water and turns crisp easily; if too much goes in at once, the oil temperature drops and the drying of the coating slows.
- **Korean**: Korean sauce-glazed fried chicken (*dakgangjeong*) is dredged in starch and fried, lifted out to drain and rest briefly, then returned to the oil and fried until golden and crisp. Only after the second fry is it tossed in the sweet and salty sauce, so the crisp crust is made first and the sauce then clings to its surface.
