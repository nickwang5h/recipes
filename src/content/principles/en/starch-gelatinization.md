## What it is

Raw starch is made of tightly packed, semi-crystalline granules, composed of amylose and amylopectin, and in cold water they simply sink to the bottom. Once heated with water to a certain temperature, the granules absorb a lot of water and swell, their internal crystalline structure collapses, and some of the starch molecules dissolve into the water. The swollen granules plus the dissolved long-chain molecules thicken the liquid, and often make it more translucent. This process is called **gelatinization**.

Two corollaries:

- **No thickening before it reaches temperature**. A starch slurry poured into a soup that isn't hot enough leaves the soup cloudy, with a raw starch taste.
- **Without dispersing first, it clumps**. Starch sprinkled straight into hot soup gelatinizes on the outside at once and wraps the dry powder inside. So mix it with cold water first (a starch slurry), or first cook the flour in fat to separate it (roux).

## Different starches have different personalities

| Starch | Character | Common use |
|---|---|---|
| Potato starch | Lower gelatinization temperature; thickens fast and clear; thins out with long cooking or hard stirring | Thickening and coating with a starch marinade in Chinese cooking |
| Cornstarch | Needs to come close to a boil to thicken fully; holds up better to long cooking; sets into a gel when cooled | Thickening, desserts, puddings |
| Wheat flour | Contains protein, turns opaque after gelatinizing, and has to cook a while longer to remove the raw flour taste | French roux, thick soups |

French **roux** is flour cooked together with butter. The longer it is cooked, the darker the colour (white, blond, brown) and the stronger the aroma, but the weaker its thickening power, because some of the starch has broken down in the dry heat.

## These also affect the result

- **Acid and long cooking**: they cut the starch chains shorter, and the sauce thins. When thickening a sauce that contains acid, do it at the end and don't cook it long afterward.
- **Sugar**: competes with the starch for water, raising the gelatinization temperature and lowering the thickness.
- **Retrogradation**: on cooling, amylose realigns and crystallizes, so the starch turns hard and releases water. Day-old rice turning hard and a refrigerated starch-thickened sauce weeping are both retrogradation. Day-old rice is good for [Egg Fried Rice](/recipes/egg-fried-rice/) precisely because it is drier, firmer and doesn't clump.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: when thickening with a starch slurry, drizzle it in stages into simmering liquid, pushing as you pour, and wait for the liquid to turn glossy again before adding the next, see [Mapo Tofu](/recipes/mapo-tofu/). Coating with a starch marinade (meat slices coated in egg white and starch) uses gelatinized starch to form a protective film on the surface of the meat, keeping it tender and smooth.
- **French**: of the five mother sauces, three, [béchamel](/recipes/bechamel/), velouté and espagnole, are thickened with roux.
- **Italian**: the pasta water carries the starch released from the surface of the pasta, and it is not just a thickener but also a stabilizer for [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/), see [Cacio e Pepe](/recipes/cacio-e-pepe/). The creaminess of [Risotto alla Milanese](/recipes/risotto-alla-milanese/) also comes from the starch slowly released from the outer layer of the rice grains.
- **Thai**: for [Mango Sticky Rice](/recipes/khao-niao-mamuang/), glutinous rice is soaked first so the grains absorb enough water, then steamed until there is no hard core in the centre; the starch gelatinizes as it heats, which is what makes the grains sticky and soft. The sweet coconut milk is stirred in hot once the rice is steamed through, and the sauce for pouring over is thickened separately with a little rice flour.
- **Vietnamese**: for steamed rice rolls, *bánh cuốn*, a batter of rice flour, tapioca starch and water is spread thin over a cloth stretched tight across the mouth of a steamer and steamed covered into a translucent rice sheet. Starch gelatinization sets the batter, and once the sheet is lifted off it can wrap a filling of minced pork and wood ear mushrooms.
- **Japanese**: [Japanese Curry Rice](/recipes/japanese-curry/) is thickened by the flour in the curry roux; the fat disperses the flour first, then the starch absorbs water and gelatinizes in the liquid. When using curry blocks, take the pot off the heat and dissolve them first, then return it to low heat and stir until thick, which reduces clumping and scorching.
- **Korean**: the paste for [Baechu Kimchi](/recipes/baechu-kimchi/) starts with glutinous rice flour and water cooked into a porridge, which is cooled before the chili flakes, garlic and fish sauce are mixed in. The gelatinized rice paste helps the seasoning cling to the leaves, so the paste does not run off.
