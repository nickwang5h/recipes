## What it is

The acetic acid in vinegar, the citric acid in lemons and the lactic acid in yogurt all bring sourness. Acidity is usually expressed as **pH**: the lower the number, the more acidic the environment. Acid changes the charge and shape of proteins and affects the links between plant cells, so it is both a seasoning and a tool for changing texture.

Sourness makes a fat-rich sauce taste lighter without removing the fat. How sour something tastes cannot be read from pH alone: how much acid the food contains, which acid it is, and the sugar and salt alongside it all change the impression in the mouth. When you swap vinegar for lemon juice, add it a little at a time and taste, rather than copying the volume.

## For seasoning, decide when to add it first

Adding acid just before the dish leaves the pan makes the final taste easier to judge and keeps the fresh aroma of ingredients such as lemon. Trying a little in a spoonful of the sauce, then taking the direction you like back to the whole pot, is easier to control than pouring in a lot of vinegar at once. Taste with the main ingredient included; tasting only the concentrated sauce can make the whole dish too sour.

In dishes stewed with wine or vinegar, the acid is part of the cooking environment from the start. Heat drives off some aromas, and reducing the sauce concentrates the acid that remains; cooked wine or vinegar can still be very sour. Sweetness can soften sourness but does not remove the acid already there. When a dish is clearly too sour, dilute it first with unsoured liquid or by adding more of the main ingredient.

## Meat and vegetables react to acid differently

Acid can **denature** the proteins in meat and change the texture of the outer layer. A short acid marinade works mainly on the outside; the thicker the piece, the harder it is for the centre to change. A longer soak can make the surface lose its springiness first. Getting a braise tender still depends on a suitable cut and sustained heat, see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).

Vegetables are different. **Pectin** and similar substances hold the tissue together between cells, and an acidic environment slows their dissolving under ordinary cooking conditions. That is why potatoes or beans in tomato juice can stay hard even after a long cook. For a soft, falling-apart result, cook them in water until close to the texture you want, then add the acid; to keep their shape, you can use this effect.

## Stabilizing foam, and also causing curdling

| Where the acid goes | Effect on structure | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Whipped egg whites | Reduces excessive clumping of proteins, helping the foam stay fine | The foam is still glossy, with no coarse grains or weeping |
| Milk | Weakens the repulsion between **casein** molecules so they clump | Curds appear and the whey separates |
| Egg mixtures to be heated | Changes the proteins' charge so they link up earlier | Adjust the heat as soon as the mixture starts to thicken |

A little acid makes egg-white foam more tolerant of whipping, but whipping hard for too long can still make it lose water. Acid also does not guarantee that every dairy sauce stays stable; pouring an acidic liquid straight into hot milk can give curds. When making an egg-based sauce, control acid and temperature together, see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: the lychee flavour of [Kung Pao Chicken](/recipes/kung-pao-chicken/) comes from balancing vinegar and sugar, sweet first and then sour in the mouth. Many dishes get vinegar added along the edge of the wok just before they come out (*peng cu*): the vinegar hits the hot wok and vaporizes quickly, the aroma rushes out, and the sourness left in the dish is softer.
- **French**: *Beurre blanc* ([Beurre Blanc](/recipes/beurre-blanc/)) is reduced with wine vinegar to balance the richness of the butter; the smooth structure also depends on water and [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/).
- **Italian**: the Sicilian [*caponata*](/recipes/caponata/) uses vinegar and sugar for a sweet and sour (*agrodolce*) taste that balances the oiliness of fried eggplant; it is often eaten cold or the next day, when the sweet and sour blend together more.
- **Thai**: [Som Tam](/recipes/som-tam/) uses lime juice, fish sauce and palm sugar for sour, salty and sweet, then pounds them together with chilies. The lime's sourness should be bright, and the sugar softens the sour impression; taste together with the shredded papaya.
- **Vietnamese**: [Nước Chấm](/recipes/nuoc-cham/) first builds a sweet and sour base from lime juice, sugar and water, then adds fish sauce for saltiness and umami. The balance of sour, sweet and salty, once dipped with fresh vegetables or tossed into rice noodles, is the taste of the whole mouthful.
- **Japanese**: sushi rice mixes vinegar, sugar and salt into cooked rice, so the sourness becomes the base flavour of the rice. *Chirashi*, rolled sushi and hand-pressed sushi all pair this vinegared rice with other ingredients.
- **Korean**: [Kimchi Jjigae](/recipes/kimchi-jjigae/) builds its sour base from aged, already-sour kimchi and its brine, and the sourness comes from [lactic acid fermentation](/principles/fermentation/). Fresh kimchi does not yet have the same sourness; a little sugar in the stew softens the sour impression.
