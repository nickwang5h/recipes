## What it is

The acetic acid in vinegar, the citric acid in lemons and the lactic acid in yogurt all bring sourness. Acidity is measured as **pH**: the lower the number, the more acidic. Acid changes the charge and shape of proteins and affects the links between plant cells, so it is both a seasoning and a way to change texture.

Sourness makes a fat-rich sauce taste lighter, though the fat is still there. How sour something tastes can't be read from pH alone: how much acid the food has, which acid it is, and the sugar and salt around it all change the taste. So when you swap vinegar for lemon juice, don't copy the volume. Add a little at a time and taste.

## For seasoning, decide when to add the acid

Acid added just before the dish leaves the pan is the easiest to control. You taste the final flavour directly, and the fresh aroma of something like lemon is still there. Try a little in a spoonful of the sauce first, then take what you like back to the whole pot; that beats pouring in a lot of vinegar at once. Taste with the main ingredient included, because tasting only the concentrated sauce makes the whole dish too sour.

In dishes stewed with wine or vinegar, the acid is part of the liquid from the start. Heat drives off some aroma and reducing the sauce concentrates the acid that's left, so cooked wine or vinegar can still be very sour. Sweetness softens sourness but doesn't remove it. If a dish tastes clearly too sour, dilute it with unsoured liquid or add more of the main ingredient.

## Meat and vegetables react to acid differently

Acid **denatures** the proteins in meat, and the outer layer changes texture first. A short acid marinade works only on the outside, and the thicker the piece, the less the centre changes; soak too long and the surface loses its springiness first. A tender braise still depends on the right cut and steady heat, see [collagen and gelatin](/principles/collagen-gelatin/).

Vegetables are another matter. **Pectin** and similar substances hold the cells together, and an acidic environment makes them dissolve more slowly under ordinary cooking conditions. That is why potatoes or beans in tomato juice can stay hard even after a long cook. For soft, falling-apart vegetables, cook them in water until close to the softness you want, then add the acid. To keep their shape, use this effect.

## Acid steadies foam, and it also curdles

| Where the acid goes | Effect on structure | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Whipped egg whites | Reduces excessive clumping of proteins, helping the foam stay fine | The foam is still glossy, with no coarse grains or weeping |
| Milk | Weakens the repulsion between **casein** molecules so they clump | Curds appear and the whey separates |
| Egg mixtures to be heated | Changes the proteins' charge so they link up earlier | Adjust the heat as soon as the mixture starts to thicken |

A little acid makes egg-white foam hold up better to whipping, but whip hard for too long and it still weeps. Acid doesn't make every dairy sauce stable either: pouring an acidic liquid straight into hot milk can give curds. When making an egg-based sauce, manage the acid and the temperature together, see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/).

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: the lychee flavour of [Kung Pao Chicken](/recipes/kung-pao-chicken/) comes from balancing vinegar and sugar, sweet first and then sour in the mouth. Many dishes get vinegar added along the edge of the wok just before they come out (*peng cu*): the vinegar hits the hot wok and vaporizes at once, the aroma rushes out, and the sourness left in the dish is softer.
- **French**: *Beurre blanc* ([Beurre Blanc](/recipes/beurre-blanc/)) is reduced with wine vinegar to balance the richness of the butter. The smooth texture also depends on water and [emulsification](/principles/emulsification/).
- **Italian**: the Sicilian [*caponata*](/recipes/caponata/) uses vinegar and sugar for a sweet and sour (*agrodolce*) taste that balances the oiliness of fried eggplant. It is often eaten cold or the next day, when the sweet and sour blend together more.
- **Thai**: [Som Tam](/recipes/som-tam/) uses lime juice, fish sauce and palm sugar for sour, salty and sweet, then pounds them together with chilies. The lime's sourness should be bright, and the sugar softens it; taste together with the shredded papaya.
- **Vietnamese**: [Nước Chấm](/recipes/nuoc-cham/) starts with a sweet and sour base of lime juice, sugar and water, then fish sauce adds salt and umami. Once it is dipped with fresh vegetables or tossed into rice noodles, the balance of sour, sweet and salty is the taste of the whole mouthful.
- **Japanese**: sushi rice mixes vinegar, sugar and salt into cooked rice, so the sourness becomes the base flavour of the rice. *Chirashi*, rolled sushi and hand-pressed sushi all pair this vinegared rice with other ingredients.
- **Korean**: [Kimchi Jjigae](/recipes/kimchi-jjigae/) gets its sour base from aged, already-sour kimchi and its brine, and that sourness comes from [lactic acid fermentation](/principles/fermentation/). Fresh kimchi doesn't have the same sourness; a little sugar in the stew softens it.
