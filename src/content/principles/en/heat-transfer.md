## What it is

**Heat transfer** is heat flowing from where it is hotter to where it is cooler. The flame heats the pan, the pan heats the food, and the heat at the food's surface moves toward its centre. Understanding this path is how you tell the distance between "the heat is high" and "the centre is cooked".

There are three ways heat is transferred in the kitchen, and a dish often uses several at once.

| Method | How the heat moves | Kitchen example |
|---|---|---|
| **Conduction** | Passes through matter in contact | A hot pan touches beaten egg; heat at the surface of a fish fillet moves inward |
| **Convection** | A flowing liquid or gas carries the heat | Boiling water circulates around noodles; a fan pushes hot air around the oven |
| **Radiation** | Transferred as electromagnetic waves; in cooking, usually infrared | Hot oven walls and glowing charcoal send heat to the food's surface |

## Contact and flow decide how the surface is heated

When you pan-fry, the part touching the bottom of the pan heats first. A thin layer of oil fills the small gaps between pan and food, making contact more even. If the food curls up or is stacked, it heats faster in some places and slower in others. Flipping, spreading out and moving pieces around all change the path by which heat enters the food.

When you boil, stirring carries hot water around the food. Steaming adds one more step: steam meets a cooler surface, condenses into water, and releases heat. The steam has to be able to reach around the food; packing plates into the steamer and blocking the gaps slows heating in places.

In an oven, hot air and radiation from the hot walls heat the food together, and the pan also conducts heat in from below. At the same set temperature, changing the rack position or turning on the fan changes how fast the surface browns. When the surface darkens too fast, adjust the position or shield it first, then decide whether the temperature needs to come down.

## Thickness decides when the centre catches up

The centre needs time to warm up. The outside of a thick piece of meat is already hot while the inside may still be cool; small pieces and thin slices have a short path for heat and cook quickly. Cutting evenly is more useful than simply cutting small, otherwise the thin slices are already tough while the thick ones are not yet cooked. For related handling, see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/).

Once the surface of a thick piece of food has reached the colour you want, you can turn down the heat and let the inside keep warming. After it comes off the heat, the heat in the outer layer keeps moving toward the centre; this is called **carryover cooking**. The thicker the food and the bigger the gap between inside and outside, the more you need to allow for what happens after it leaves the heat; a thin egg crêpe, by contrast, should be moved out of the hot pan quickly.

## Water decides where the heat goes

When there is water on the surface, some of the heat is first spent evaporating it. Wiping meat dry before pan-frying and adding stir-fry ingredients in batches reduce the water pooling in the pan, so the surface reaches the state suited to the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) sooner.

The more food in the wok, the more water there is to heat and evaporate. If you keep piling in ingredients at this point, the pan may be busy boiling water even over high heat. Judge the heat by what is in the pan: if liquid is pooling at the bottom, you are boiling; only when the surface gradually dries out and starts to colour are you stir-frying.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Steamed Whole Fish with Ginger and Green Onion](/recipes/steamed-fish/) relies on steam condensing on the surface to deliver heat, which then conducts inward through the flesh; the thickest part of the fish decides whether it is cooked through.
- **French**: a [French Omelette](/recipes/french-omelette/) relies on conduction from the bottom of the pan. Stirring the eggs and taking the pan off the heat in time shorten how long any one spot stays hot, which controls protein coagulation.
- **Italian**: [Risotto alla Milanese](/recipes/risotto-alla-milanese/) surrounds the grains of rice with hot stock; stirring helps them heat evenly, and then heat and water enter the grains and drive starch gelatinization.
- **Thai**: for [Gai Yang](/recipes/gai-yang/), the chicken is first cut open and flattened, then grilled over charcoal. The radiation from the charcoal heats the surface, and heat then conducts toward the part next to the bone; flattening reduces the difference in thickness, and flipping and moving the pieces control how the surface browns.
- **Vietnamese**: the pork slices and small patties in [Bún Chả](/recipes/bun-cha/) are grilled over charcoal; heat reaches the surface first and then moves toward the centre. Spreading the slices out and pressing the patties thin shorten the distance heat has to travel to the centre, and reduce the case of burnt outside and raw inside.
- **Japanese**: [Chawanmushi](/recipes/chawanmushi/) puts the egg mixture in small lidded cups and heats it with gentle steam. Heat passes through the cup wall into the custard, and the centre warms more slowly than the outer ring; if the steam is too strong, the outer ring sets too much first, leaving holes and weeping liquid.
- **Korean**: the earthenware pot version of [Gyeran Jjim](/recipes/gyeran-jjim/) is heated directly on the stove; the bottom of the pot passes heat to the egg first and then gradually heats the centre. The pot keeps sending out heat after it comes off the stove, so take it off once the centre is cooked through and serve it promptly.
