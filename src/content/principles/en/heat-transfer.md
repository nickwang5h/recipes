## What it is

**Heat transfer** is heat flowing from where it is hotter to where it is cooler. The flame heats the pan, the pan heats the food, and the heat at the food's surface then moves toward the centre. A high flame doesn't mean the centre is cooked; that path is the gap between the two.

There are three ways heat is transferred in the kitchen, and a dish often uses several at once.

| Method | How the heat moves | Kitchen example |
|---|---|---|
| **Conduction** | Passes through matter in contact | A hot pan touches beaten egg; heat at the surface of a fish fillet moves inward |
| **Convection** | A flowing liquid or gas carries the heat | Boiling water circulates around noodles; a fan pushes hot air around the oven |
| **Radiation** | Transferred as electromagnetic waves; in cooking, usually infrared | Hot oven walls and glowing charcoal send heat to the food's surface |

## The surface heats through contact and flow

When you pan-fry, the part touching the bottom of the pan heats first. A thin layer of oil fills the small gaps between pan and food, so contact is more even. If the food curls up or is stacked, some spots heat faster and others slower. That is why you flip, spread out and move pieces around: each changes the path heat takes into the food.

When you boil, stirring carries hot water around the food. Steaming adds one more step: steam meets a cooler surface, condenses into water, and gives off heat. The steam has to reach all around the food, so a steamer packed with plates that block the gaps heats slowly in places.

In an oven, hot air and radiation from the hot walls heat the food together, and the pan also conducts heat in from below. At the same set temperature, a different rack position or the fan turned on changes how fast the surface browns. When the surface darkens too fast, move the pan or shield the food first, then decide whether to turn the temperature down.

## The centre catches up according to thickness

The centre needs time to warm up. The outside of a thick piece of meat is already hot while the inside may still be cool; small pieces and thin slices have a short path and cook quickly. So cutting evenly helps more than cutting small, otherwise the thin slices are already tough while the thick ones are not yet cooked. For how to cut, see [Knife Work and Cooking](/principles/knife-work/).

Once the surface of a thick piece has the colour you want, turn down the heat and let the inside keep warming. After it comes off the heat, the heat in the outer layer keeps moving toward the centre; this is called **carryover cooking**. The thicker the food and the bigger the gap between inside and outside, the more of that extra warming you need to count on. A thin egg crêpe, by contrast, should come out of the hot pan right away.

## Water takes the heat first

When there is water on the surface, the heat first goes into evaporating it. Wiping meat dry before pan-frying and adding stir-fry ingredients in batches leave less water in the pan, so the surface reaches the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) sooner.

The more food in the wok, the more water there is to heat and evaporate. Keep piling in ingredients and the pan spends its heat boiling water, even over high heat. Look at what is in the pan: liquid pooling at the bottom means you are boiling; a surface that gradually dries out and starts to colour means you are stir-frying.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Steamed Whole Fish with Ginger and Green Onion](/recipes/steamed-fish/) relies on steam condensing on the surface to deliver heat, which then conducts inward through the flesh. The thickest part of the fish cooks last, so when it is done the whole fish is done.
- **French**: a [French Omelette](/recipes/french-omelette/) relies on conduction from the bottom of the pan. Stirring the eggs and taking the pan off the heat in time shorten how long any one spot stays hot, which keeps protein coagulation under control.
- **Italian**: [Risotto alla Milanese](/recipes/risotto-alla-milanese/) surrounds the grains of rice with hot stock, and stirring helps them heat evenly. Heat and water then enter the grains and drive starch gelatinization.
- **Thai**: for [Gai Yang](/recipes/gai-yang/), the chicken is cut open and flattened, then grilled over charcoal. The radiation from the charcoal heats the surface, and heat then conducts toward the part next to the bone. Flattening evens out the thickness, and flipping and moving the pieces control how the surface browns.
- **Vietnamese**: the pork slices and small patties in [Bún Chả](/recipes/bun-cha/) are grilled over charcoal; heat reaches the surface first and then moves toward the centre. Spread the slices out and press the patties thin, and heat has a shorter way to the centre, so the outside is less likely to burn while the inside is still raw.
- **Japanese**: [Chawanmushi](/recipes/chawanmushi/) puts the egg mixture in small lidded cups and heats it with gentle steam. Heat passes through the cup wall into the custard, and the centre warms more slowly than the outer ring. If the steam is too strong, the outer ring sets too much first, leaving holes and weeping liquid.
- **Korean**: the earthenware pot version of [Gyeran Jjim](/recipes/gyeran-jjim/) is heated directly on the stove. The bottom of the pot passes heat to the egg first and then gradually heats the centre. The pot keeps sending out heat after it comes off the stove, so take it off as soon as the centre is cooked through and serve it right away.
