## What it is

The main component of table salt is **sodium chloride**, which dissolves in water into sodium and chloride ions. Salt gives saltiness, and it can also suppress some bitterness, making the food's own sweetness and umami easier to taste. Adding salt to a bland soup often makes the flavour of the ingredients clear; keep adding, and the saltiness covers up everything else.

Salt enters food by **diffusion**: dissolved ions move from where they are concentrated to where they are not. **Osmosis** refers specifically to water moving across a semipermeable membrane. The pool of water that appears after you toss cucumber with salt is the result of water moving outward; meat holding more water after a brine also involves the effect of salt on muscle proteins. Keep the two things separate.

## Count the salt already there

Soy sauce, chili bean paste, cured meats and cheese all carry salt. Count those ingredients into the seasoning first, then decide whether to add table salt. When a sauce reduces, water evaporates and the salt stays in the pan, so the saltiness gets stronger and stronger. For dishes that need reducing, hold back at first and taste again when the sauce is close to its final thickness.

Fine salt and coarse salt scooped with the same spoon can differ greatly in weight. Grain shape and the gaps between grains differ, so volume does not equal the amount of salt. When you need to repeat a curing result, record it by weight; for seasoning before a dish leaves the pan, add a little at a time, mix well, and taste a bite of the ingredient together with some sauce.

## Thickness and time decide how far salt travels

Salt travels inward from the surface it touches. Thin slices season through to the centre easily, while thick pieces take longer; a heavy hand with the salt will make the outer layer too salty first. When curing meat, spread the salt evenly and wait in the refrigerator, which gives a more even flavour than sprinkling one side just before the meat goes in the pan.

After dry salt is sprinkled on, the surface may at first turn wet: the salt dissolves in the juices that seep out, forming a thin layer of brine, which then keeps diffusing inward. If you sear the meat at this point, the wet surface slows down the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). Pat the surface dry before it goes in the pan, to leave room for browning and aroma.

## Giving up water or holding it depends on the food's structure

| Food and treatment | Main change | Use in the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| Raw cucumber or napa cabbage tossed with salt | Cells lose some of their water and the tissue softens | Removes excess liquid so a dressing or filling is less easily diluted |
| Meat cured in brine | Salt alters the structure of muscle proteins and raises their water-holding capacity | Keeps lean meat that tends to dry out more juicy after cooking |
| Meat cured heavily with salt over a long time | Water content drops and the texture firms up | Making salt-cured foods such as salt pork |

Lightly salting meat and drawing water out of vegetables aim at different results. Salted meat still loses water when heated; if the temperature is too high or it is cooked too long, it still turns dry, see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). Meat salted for everyday seasoning still has to be refrigerated, and should not be treated as a long-keeping cured meat.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: when seasoning a meat filling, add the salt first and beat in one direction until it turns sticky and springy (*shang jin*). The salt draws some of the proteins in the muscle out to link into a sticky network, which holds the water added in stages afterward (*da shui*, "beating in water") and makes meatballs and dumpling fillings bouncy and juicy. The chili bean paste and fermented black beans in [Mapo Tofu](/recipes/mapo-tofu/) are salty themselves, so let them go into the sauce first, then judge whether more salt is needed.
- **French**: both the stock and the cheese on top of [French Onion Soup](/recipes/french-onion-soup/) are salty. Season with salt only after the soup has reduced, so that it is not too salty once the cheese goes on at the end.
- **Italian**: the cured pork cheek and the cheese in [Spaghetti Carbonara](/recipes/carbonara/) are already very salty. The pasta water also goes into the sauce, so count all of these salts together when tasting.
- **Thai**: [Green Papaya Salad](/recipes/som-tam/) gets its saltiness from fish sauce and dried shrimp, and the saltiness has to be tasted together with the shredded papaya. Salt makes fruit and vegetables gradually release water, so pound and toss it only just before eating and serve as soon as it is mixed.
- **Vietnamese**: for [Nước Chấm](/recipes/nuoc-cham/), build a sweet and sour base first from lime, sugar and water, then add the fish sauce in stages, adjusting the salt level to suit dipping or dressing rice noodles. When tasting, include unseasoned rice noodles or lettuce, so you can judge whether the whole mouthful is salty enough.
- **Japanese**: pickled vegetables, *shiozuke*, use brine to cure cucumber, daikon and the like. The salt diffuses inward while the vegetables lose some of their water and turn pliable. Brine strength and curing time together decide how salty the result is, and thin-cut vegetables take on flavour faster than whole pieces.
- **Korean**: for [Napa Cabbage Kimchi](/recipes/baechu-kimchi/), salt is sprinkled layer by layer so the thick ribs release water and become bendable, and the extra salt is then rinsed off and the cabbage drained before the paste goes on. Put more salt on the thick ribs during salting so that they and the thin leaves both turn pliable.
