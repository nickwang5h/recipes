## What it is

The main component of table salt is **sodium chloride**, which dissolves in water into sodium and chloride ions. Salt gives saltiness, and it also suppresses some bitterness, so the food's own sweetness and umami are easier to taste. A little salt in a bland soup often makes the flavour of the ingredients come through; keep adding, and the saltiness covers up everything else.

Salt gets into food by **diffusion**: dissolved ions move from where they are concentrated to where they are not. **Osmosis** is specifically water moving across a semipermeable membrane. The pool of water that appears after you toss cucumber with salt is water moving out; meat holding more water after a brine also involves what salt does to muscle proteins. They are two different things.

## Count the salt already there

Soy sauce, chili bean paste, cured meats and cheese all carry salt. Count them in first, then decide whether to add table salt. When a sauce reduces, the water evaporates and the salt stays in the pan, so the sauce gets saltier and saltier. For a dish that reduces, go light at first and taste again when the sauce is close to its final thickness.

Fine salt and coarse salt scooped with the same spoon can differ greatly in weight: grain shape and the gaps between grains differ, so volume is not the amount of salt. To repeat a cure, record it by weight. For seasoning before a dish leaves the pan, add a little at a time, mix well, and taste a bite of the ingredient with some sauce.

## Thickness and time decide how far salt travels

Salt travels inward from the surface it touches. Thin slices season through to the centre quickly, while thick pieces take much longer; add a lot of salt at once and the outer layer turns too salty first. When curing meat, spread the salt evenly and leave it in the refrigerator; the flavour comes out more even than if you sprinkle one side just before the meat goes in the pan.

Dry salt makes the surface wet at first: it dissolves in the juices that seep out, forming a thin layer of brine, which then keeps diffusing inward. If you sear the meat at this point, the wet surface slows down the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/). So pat the surface dry before it goes in the pan, to give browning and aroma a chance.

## Giving up water or holding it depends on the food's structure

| Food and treatment | Main change | Use in the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| Raw cucumber or napa cabbage tossed with salt | Cells lose some of their water and the tissue softens | Removes excess liquid so a dressing or filling is not diluted |
| Meat cured in brine | Salt alters the structure of muscle proteins and raises their water-holding capacity | Lean meat that tends to dry out stays juicier after cooking |
| Meat cured heavily with salt over a long time | Water content drops and the texture firms up | Making salt-cured foods such as salt pork |

Lightly salting meat and drawing water out of vegetables aim at different results. Salted meat still loses water when heated; if the temperature is too high or it cooks too long, it still turns dry, see [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). Meat salted for everyday seasoning still has to be refrigerated; it is not a long-keeping cured meat.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: when seasoning a meat filling, add the salt first and beat in one direction until it turns sticky and springy (*shang jin*). The salt draws some of the proteins in the muscle out to link into a sticky network, which holds the water added in stages afterward (*da shui*, "beating in water"), so meatballs and dumpling fillings come out bouncy and juicy. The chili bean paste and fermented black beans in [Mapo Tofu](/recipes/mapo-tofu/) are salty themselves, so let them go into the sauce first, then judge whether more salt is needed.
- **French**: both the stock and the cheese on top of [French Onion Soup](/recipes/french-onion-soup/) are salty. Season with salt after the soup has reduced, and it won't be too salty once the cheese goes on at the end.
- **Italian**: the cured pork cheek and the cheese in [Spaghetti Carbonara](/recipes/carbonara/) are already very salty, and the pasta water goes into the sauce too, so count all of these salts together when you taste.
- **Thai**: the saltiness of [Green Papaya Salad](/recipes/som-tam/) comes from fish sauce and dried shrimp, and you taste it together with the shredded papaya. Salt makes fruit and vegetables slowly release water, so pound and toss it only just before eating and serve as soon as it is mixed.
- **Vietnamese**: for [Nước Chấm](/recipes/nuoc-cham/), build a sweet and sour base first from lime, sugar and water, then add the fish sauce in stages until the saltiness suits dipping or dressing rice noodles. Taste it with unseasoned rice noodles or lettuce, and you will know whether the whole mouthful is salty enough.
- **Japanese**: *shiozuke*, salt-pickled vegetables, uses brine to cure cucumber, daikon and the like. The salt diffuses inward while the vegetables lose some of their water and turn pliable. Brine strength and curing time together decide how salty the result is, and thin-cut vegetables take on flavour faster than whole pieces.
- **Korean**: for [Napa Cabbage Kimchi](/recipes/baechu-kimchi/), salt goes on layer by layer so the thick ribs release water and become bendable; then the extra salt is rinsed off and the cabbage drained before the paste goes on. The thick ribs get more salt so that they and the thin leaves turn pliable together.
