## What it is

**Caramelization** is a series of chemical changes that sugar undergoes when heated. Sugar molecules break down and lose water, and also recombine, producing new aroma compounds and brown compounds. White sugar goes from clear syrup to golden and amber and then darker, and sweetness gradually gives way to a more complex roasted aroma and bitterness.

The process can happen with sugar alone, but it needs a very high temperature: sucrose starts to change colour noticeably only above about 160°C (320°F), far above the boiling point of water. The [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) needs sugar and amino compounds together. Browning in meat, bread and onions may involve several reactions at once, so when you see brown, judge by the ingredient and the cooking conditions.

## With a lot of water, it concentrates before it colours

**Wet caramel** first dissolves the sugar in water. At the start of heating, the temperature of the syrup is held just above 100°C (210°F) by the water, which is just boiling and evaporating; as the water gets less, the boiling point of the syrup rises, and the colour begins to change only as it nears the caramelizing temperature, and then it changes faster and faster. The water at the start helps the heating stay even and leaves time to watch and adjust the heat.

**Dry caramel** heats the sugar directly, and the part touching the bottom of the pan changes first. If the sugar is spread too thick or the heat is uneven, one side can already be dark while the other is still white granules. A heavy-bottomed pan, evenly spread sugar and lower heat reduce local overheating. How the surface and the bottom of the pan exchange heat is in [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).

When sugar is cooked in oil, the oil helps carry the heat, but the change in the sugar itself still has to be judged by colour and smell. With a small amount of sugar and a hot pan bottom, the colour moves especially fast. Have the ingredients or liquid to be added next ready beforehand, so you have a chance to stop at the right colour.

## Colour decides flavour, residual heat decides where you stop

The darker the sugar, the more obvious the roasted aroma and bitterness usually are. Light caramel leans sweet, dark caramel has more bitter fragrance; keep cooking to black with an acrid smell and the flavour has gone too far. The colour of the pan interferes with judging, and a light-coloured pan bottom makes the changes in the syrup easier to see.

| What you see | Flavour direction | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Clear syrup, no colour yet | Mainly sweet | Keep watching the concentration and colour change |
| Golden to amber | Caramel aroma becoming noticeable | Lower the heat near the target and get ready to take it off |
| Deep brown, clear bitter note | More bitterness | Stop the heating promptly |
| Burnt black, acrid | Heavy burnt bitterness | Make a new batch |

After the heat is off, the pan keeps passing heat to the sugar. Moving the pan off the heat as the colour nears the target is easier to control than waiting until the colour is just right to turn it off. Adding water or cream cools and dilutes the caramel, but it boils up suddenly and spatters; add it slowly in stages off the heat, keeping your face and hands away from the mouth of the pan.

## Crystallization is a different change

White grains appearing in the syrup, or the syrup turning cloudy, is **crystallization**: sugar molecules rearranging into crystals. Undissolved sugar grains on the side of the pan can become seeds and bring more sugar out of solution. At the start of wet caramel, let the sugar dissolve completely, afterward just swirl the pan gently so the colour mixes evenly, and avoid stirring the grains from the side back into the syrup.

If it turns grainy before it has taken colour, you can add water to dissolve it again and keep cooking. The bitterness of burnt sugar cannot be removed with water. Crystallization and caramelization change the physical structure and the chemical composition respectively, so they are handled differently.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Red-Braised Pork Belly](/recipes/hong-shao-rou/) cooks the sugar to a jujube red (*chao tang se*), which brings colour and a slightly bitter caramel aroma; [Candied Potatoes (Spun Sugar Potatoes)](/recipes/ba-si-tu-dou/) (*ba si*) cooks the sugar just until it changes colour and can be pulled into fine threads, then coats the ingredient while it is hot.
- **French**: [Crème Caramel](/recipes/creme-caramel/) (*crème caramel*) first pours a layer of caramel into the bottom of the mould, and the custard sets through [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). In [French Onion Soup](/recipes/french-onion-soup/) the onions slowly turn deep brown, with both the caramelization of sugars and the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).
- **Italian**: almond brittle (*croccante*) cooks sugar to an amber caramel, stirs in toasted almonds, presses it flat while hot, and it turns crisp as it cools.
- **Vietnamese**: the caramel colouring *nước màu* first cooks sugar to a deep brown and then dilutes it with water, bringing colour and a slightly bitter caramel aroma to braised dishes. [Cá Kho Tộ](/recipes/ca-kho-to/) and [Thịt Kho Trứng](/recipes/thit-kho-trung/) both use it; the caramel is cooked before the main ingredient goes in the pot.
- **Korean**: *dalgona* melts white sugar over low heat into a light-brown syrup, then stirs in baking soda so it puffs up, and when it cools it forms a porous brittle candy. Caramelization brings the colour and the caramel aroma, and the puffing comes from the baking soda added afterward.
