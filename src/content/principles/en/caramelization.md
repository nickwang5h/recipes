## What it is

**Caramelization** is a series of chemical changes that sugar goes through when heated. Sugar molecules break down and lose water, and they also recombine, producing new aroma compounds and brown compounds. White sugar goes from clear syrup to golden and amber and then darker, and the sweetness gradually gives way to a more complex roasted aroma and bitterness.

Sugar alone can caramelize, but it takes a very high temperature: sucrose starts to change colour noticeably only above about 160°C (320°F), far above the boiling point of water. The [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/) needs sugar and amino compounds together. Browning in meat, bread and onions may involve several reactions at once, so when you see brown, judge by the ingredient and the cooking conditions.

## With a lot of water, it concentrates before it colours

**Wet caramel** starts by dissolving the sugar in water. At first the water holds the syrup just above 100°C (210°F) while it boils and evaporates. As the water gets less, the boiling point of the syrup rises; only when it nears caramelizing temperature does the colour begin to change, and then it changes faster and faster. The water at the start helps the sugar heat evenly and gives you time to watch and adjust the heat.

**Dry caramel** heats the sugar directly, and the part touching the bottom of the pan changes first. If the sugar is spread too thick or the heat is uneven, one side gets dark while the other is still white granules. A heavy-bottomed pan, evenly spread sugar and lower heat keep it from overheating in one spot. How the surface and the bottom of the pan exchange heat is in [heat transfer](/principles/heat-transfer/).

When sugar is cooked in oil, the oil helps carry the heat, but you still judge how far the sugar has gone by its colour and smell. With a small amount of sugar and a hot pan bottom, the colour moves especially fast. Have the ingredients or liquid you'll add next ready beforehand, so you can stop at the right colour.

## Colour decides flavour, residual heat decides when to stop

The darker the sugar, the more obvious the roasted aroma and bitterness usually are. Light caramel leans sweet and dark caramel is more bitter and fragrant; keep cooking to black with an acrid smell and the flavour has gone too far. The colour of the pan can fool you, and a light-coloured pan bottom makes the changes in the syrup easier to see.

| What you see | Flavour direction | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Clear syrup, no colour yet | Mainly sweet | Keep watching the concentration and colour change |
| Golden to amber | Caramel aroma becoming noticeable | Lower the heat near the target and get ready to take it off |
| Deep brown, clear bitter note | More bitterness | Stop the heating promptly |
| Burnt black, acrid | Heavy burnt bitterness | Make a new batch |

After the heat is off, the pan keeps passing heat to the sugar. So take the pan off the heat when the colour is nearly there; that's easier to control than waiting until it's just right and then turning off the heat. Adding water or cream cools and dilutes the caramel, but it boils up suddenly and spatters. Add it slowly in stages off the heat, with your face and hands well away from the mouth of the pan.

## Crystallization is a different thing

White grains in the syrup, or syrup turning cloudy, is **crystallization**: sugar molecules rearranging into crystals. Undissolved sugar grains on the side of the pan can become seeds and bring more sugar out of solution. At the start of wet caramel, let the sugar dissolve completely, then just swirl the pan gently so the colour mixes evenly, and don't stir the grains from the side back into the syrup.

If it turns grainy before it has taken colour, add water to dissolve it again and keep cooking. Water can't remove the bitterness of burnt sugar. Crystallization changes the physical structure and caramelization changes the chemical composition, so they are handled differently.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: [Red-Braised Pork Belly](/recipes/hong-shao-rou/) cooks the sugar to a jujube red (*chao tang se*), which brings colour and a slightly bitter caramel aroma; [Candied Potatoes (Spun Sugar Potatoes)](/recipes/ba-si-tu-dou/) (*ba si*) cooks the sugar just until it changes colour and can be pulled into fine threads, then coats the ingredient while it is hot.
- **French**: [Crème Caramel](/recipes/creme-caramel/) (*crème caramel*) first pours a layer of caramel into the bottom of the mould, and the custard sets through [protein denaturation and coagulation](/principles/protein-coagulation/). In [French Onion Soup](/recipes/french-onion-soup/) the onions slowly turn deep brown, through both the caramelization of sugars and the [Maillard reaction](/principles/maillard-reaction/).
- **Italian**: almond brittle (*croccante*) cooks sugar to an amber caramel, stirs in toasted almonds and presses it flat while hot; it turns crisp as it cools.
- **Vietnamese**: the caramel colouring *nước màu* starts by cooking sugar to a deep brown, then dilutes it with water, bringing colour and a slightly bitter caramel aroma to braised dishes. [Cá Kho Tộ](/recipes/ca-kho-to/) and [Thịt Kho Trứng](/recipes/thit-kho-trung/) both use it; the caramel is cooked before the main ingredient goes in the pot.
- **Korean**: *dalgona* melts white sugar over low heat into a light-brown syrup, then stirs in baking soda so it puffs up, and when it cools it forms a porous brittle candy. Caramelization brings the colour and the caramel aroma, and the puffing comes from the baking soda added afterward.
