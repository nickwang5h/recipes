## What it is

**Cooking sugar** means boiling the water out of a sugar syrup little by little. White sugar is **sucrose**; dissolved in water it becomes syrup. As the syrup boils, water leaves, the sugar gets more concentrated and the syrup's temperature rises with it. So the temperature in the pan tells you how much water is left: the highest stage it reached decides how hard the syrup is once it cools.

Once almost all the water is gone, more heat starts to colour the sugar; that is [caramelization](/principles/caramelization/). Cooking sugar is about the stretch before the colour changes, and about what the cooked syrup does as it cools.

## Temperature is concentration

Candy recipes say "cook to soft ball" or "cook to hard crack". The names come from an old test: drop a little syrup into cold water and see what it does as it cools. The syrup is very hot: let it cool in the water for a few seconds before you touch it.

| Stage | Temperature | Sugar concentration | Dropped into cold water | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thread | 110–113°C (230–235°F) | about 80% | a thread that won't form a ball | syrup to pour over ice cream |
| Soft ball | 113–116°C (235–240°F) | about 85% | a soft ball that flattens soon after you lift it out | fudge, fondant |
| Hard ball | 121–129°C (250–265°F) | about 92% | a hard ball you can still squash | nougat, marshmallows |
| Soft crack | 132–143°C (270–290°F) | about 95% | threads that bend a little before breaking | saltwater taffy, butterscotch |
| Hard crack | 149–154°C (300–310°F) | about 99% | hard threads that snap when bent | toffee, nut brittle, lollipops |

Above that, at about 160°C (320°F) the water is gone and the sugar is a light amber liquid; at about 170°C (340°F) it starts to brown; past about 177°C (350°F) it burns and turns bitter.

The temperatures in the table are for sea level. Test your thermometer in boiling water first: it should read 100°C (212°F); correct your readings by however far off it is. At altitude, take about 0.5°C (1°F) off every stage for each 150 m (500 ft) above sea level.

## A cooling syrup wants to crystallize

Hot water dissolves more sugar than cold water. When a syrup cooked to a high temperature cools, it holds far more dissolved sugar than it could at that temperature; it is **supersaturated**. That state is unstable: a little stirring or a knock, and the sugar starts lining up into crystals again.

Some sweets are meant to contain crystals, such as fudge and fondant. Others must have none at all: lollipops and toffee need to be smooth, and crystals make them grainy.

## Stopping crystals: other sugars, acid and fat

Sucrose crystallizes when sucrose molecules line up neatly side by side. Mix in molecules of a different shape and they can't line up.

- **Other sugars**: glucose and fructose. Corn syrup is mostly glucose, and some lollipop recipes are half corn syrup.
- **Acid**: a little lemon juice or cream of tartar splits some of the sucrose into glucose and fructose, making the "other sugars" right in the pan.
- **Fat**: butter also gets in the way of the sucrose molecules. Toffee has a lot of butter, which is why it is smooth and snaps cleanly.

When Escoffier cooks sugar he does two more things: he skims off the scum, and he wipes the sugar crystals off the sides of the pan with a wet finger or a damp cloth, so they don't fall back in and turn the whole pan grainy. He also suggests a few drops of lemon juice, or a tablespoon of glucose for every 450 g of sugar.

## Across cuisines

- **Chinese**: the same syrup can go two ways. For [tanghulu](/recipes/bing-tang-hu-lu/), a drop of syrup is tested in cold water until the sugar snaps cleanly: hard crack. For [candied potatoes](/recipes/ba-si-tu-dou/), the syrup is cooked to pale gold and pulled into threads while hot, and the crystals on the side of the pan are left alone. [Sugar-frosted taro](/recipes/fan-sha-yu-tou/) does the opposite: the syrup is tossed off the heat without stopping, so it crystallizes on purpose into a white frost.
- **French**: Escoffier divides cooked sugar into small thread, large thread, small ball, large ball, small crack and large crack. For Italian meringue (*meringue à l'italienne*) he cooks the sugar to large ball, about 120°C (250°F), and pours it into the whipped egg whites.
- **Italian**: [almond brittle](/recipes/croccante/) cooks sugar and honey to about 170°C (340°F), until the colour turns amber, then stirs in toasted almonds and spreads it thin to cool and set crisp. The lemon juice keeps the sugar from turning grainy, and the honey stands in for glucose and also helps stop crystals.
